# Romans 9

> "Therefore hath he mercy on whom he will have mercy, and whom he will he hardeneth." (Romans 9:18, KJV)

> "Nay but, O man, who art thou that repliest against God? Shall the thing formed say to him that formed it, Why hast thou made me thus? Hath not the potter power over the clay, of the same lump to make one vessel unto honour, and another unto dishonour? What if God, willing to shew his wrath, and to make his power known, endured with much longsuffering the vessels of wrath fitted to destruction:" (Romans 9:20-22, KJV)

## Significance
The single most-cited Calvinist proof-text for **unconditional election**. [[Tim Stratton]] recounts that as a five-point Calvinist he "put tons of emphasis on Romans nine," reading the potter-and-clay imagery to mean "everything the clay does is determined by God."[^1] The chapter (with its "Jacob have I loved, but Esau have I hated," v.13) is the passage the [[Predestination Does Not Entail Determinism|conflation of predestination with determinism]] leans on hardest.

## How traditions read it
- **Calvinist:** God unconditionally elects individuals to salvation or reprobation before birth; the clay has no say. The steelman for [[The Creation Dilemma Is Strongest Against Calvinism and Catholicism|the Creation Dilemma]] runs through Romans 9.
- **Molinist / non-Calvinist:** The "hardening" and "vessels" language is about God's freedom in the *corporate*, historical outworking of the covenant (Israel and the nations), not a decree fixing each person's eternal destiny; it must be read alongside the passages that "heavily imply libertarian freedom," which a whole-of-Scripture reading cannot discard.[^2] Compare the LDS reading of the same potter-clay motif in [[Wax and Clay Heart Analogy Rebutted]].
