# The Anti-Flesh Body-as-Sin View Is Gnostic Not Biblical

#type/rebuttal

## The Claim

The popular Christian idea that "the flesh is what makes us sin" — that the body is the enemy, that spirits are imprisoned in cells of flesh, and that salvation means transcending the material — is **middle-Platonic and Gnostic**, not the biblical picture. [[Dan McClellan]] argues that early Christianity ran the *opposite* direction: it looked forward to the **resurrection of the flesh** and eternal life in bodies, an idea Greek and Gnostic thinkers found repugnant. That very clash is what pressured the church into developing [[Creation Ex Nihilo Originated Late 2nd Century CE|creation ex nihilo]] to establish that the material world comes directly from God and is therefore good.[^1] This is offered as a rebuttal to an apologetic content creator who claimed the flesh is "everything that is not of God" and that choosing the flesh is the essence of sin.

## Supporting Evidence

1. **The "flesh-prison" framing is Gnostic/middle-Platonic.** When the content creator says our flesh makes us sin, McClellan responds: "now it sounds like we're getting into a more middle Platonic gnostic of our spirits being imprisoned in cells made of flesh and that salvation is about overcoming and transcending the flesh so that we can return to dwell with the pleroma."[^1] *(Evidence rating: Strong — "pleroma," the spirit-prison, and escape-from-matter are hallmark Gnostic/Platonic motifs, not Hebrew ones.)*

2. **It is a specific late-antique philosophy, not timeless Christianity.** McClellan dates the worldview: "this is a first, second, third century CE philosophical idea that I don't think it is particularly helpful to try to assert in the 21st century."[^2] *(Evidence rating: Moderate; Argument weight: Secondary — a chronological placement, not a proof, but it locates the idea in Hellenistic philosophy rather than in Genesis.)*

3. **Early Christians affirmed the resurrection *of the flesh*.** Against the body-as-prison view, "early Christians were talking about looking forward to the resurrection of the flesh and living eternally in bodies of flesh. And for Greek thinkers and Gnostics, that was a horrendous idea."[^3] The mainstream Christian hope was *more* embodiment, not less. *(Evidence rating: Strong — the bodily resurrection is early and central; cf. 1 Corinthians 15, the creeds' "resurrection of the body.")*

4. **The Gnostic reaction proves the contrast.** To Greek and Gnostic sensibilities, "Why would you look forward to spending eternity mucking around in the material pigsty of the flesh?... resurrection of the flesh sounds awful to Gnostics and to other Greek thinkers."[^4] The people who held the content creator's premise (flesh = bad) were exactly the ones who *rejected* the Christian resurrection. *(Evidence rating: Strong — the anti-body reaction is well attested in the resurrection debates.)*

5. **The body-as-sin view was rejected by most early Christians.** McClellan's conclusion: the idea "that the flesh is fundamentally opposed to God and is something that we're trying to overcome, and that anytime we choose the flesh, that is sin... ultimately was rejected by most early Christians."[^5] Its pressure instead produced creation ex nihilo (see [[Creation Ex Nihilo Originated Late 2nd Century CE]]). *(Evidence rating: Strong; Argument weight: Primary.)*

## Apologetic Engagement

**Steelman (the Pauline flesh/spirit case).** The content creator's view is not baseless: Paul repeatedly opposes "the flesh" (*sarx*) to "the spirit" — "the flesh lusteth against the Spirit" (Galatians 5:17), "in my flesh dwelleth no good thing" (Romans 7:18), "they that are in the flesh cannot please God" (Romans 8:8). A defender can argue that a negative valuation of "the flesh" is genuinely biblical, and that later ascetic and Augustinian traditions are developing a real Pauline seed, not importing Gnosticism wholesale. The strongest form: Paul's *sarx* names the fallen, self-oriented human condition — a moral orientation — which a careful reader need not equate with the physical body as such.

**Where McClellan's reading supports the Latter-day Saint view.** This is exactly the distinction the rebuttal turns on. Paul's *sarx* is a moral-existential term, not a metaphysical verdict that matter is evil; when apologetics slides from "walk not after the flesh" to "the body is the enemy," it has crossed into Gnostic territory that the early church fought. McClellan's history — resurrection *of the flesh* as the mainstream hope, the body-prison as the losing side — vindicates a thoroughly **pro-body** theology. That aligns tightly with LDS anthropology: the body is a divine gift and a step *up*, not a prison to escape.

**Where it diverges.** McClellan treats both the pro-flesh and anti-flesh positions descriptively, as competing ancient options, and offers no theological endorsement of a divine-body doctrine here; his point is historical placement. He also does not adjudicate the *exegesis* of Paul's *sarx*, only diagnose the content creator's stronger claim as Gnostic. The LDS conclusion that embodiment is essential to exaltation is compatible with his history but is not established by it.

## LDS Relevance

Latter-day Saint theology is arguably the most emphatically **pro-body** tradition in Christianity: the physical body is a necessary gift for progression, the resurrection restores a glorified body permanently, God himself is embodied, and matter is eternal and good rather than a fall from pure spirit ("There is no such thing as immaterial matter," D&C 131:7-8). McClellan's argument dismantles a common Protestant/ascetic assumption — that the flesh is intrinsically sinful — and shows it to be the *Gnostic* side of an ancient debate the church ultimately rejected. This supports the LDS affirmation of embodiment and dovetails with [[The Image of God Is Divine Resemblance and Royal Representation|the embodied imago Dei]] and [[Anthropomorphic God in the Hebrew Bible|the corporeal God of the Hebrew Bible]]. Caveat: the payoff is a *negative* clearing of Gnostic assumptions; the positive LDS doctrine of the body's role in exaltation rests on Restoration scripture, not on McClellan's history alone.
