# The Lego Argument - Divine Simplicity Forces a Monotheism That Excludes Distinct Divine Persons

## The Claim
Thoughtful Faith argues that **divine simplicity** is the hidden engine of the whole Trinity problem. The doctrine — "the most fundamental thing can't have parts" — is "actually pretty airtight philosophically,"[^1] but once granted it entails a *hard* monotheism in which only one undivided "thing" can be God, which in turn *rules out* the Father and the Son both being God, and so forces the Trinity into its "three things into one thing" contortions.[^2] The video presents this not to defend simplicity (as classical theists do) but to show it is the imported Greek premise that makes the Godhead unthinkable and the Trinity necessary.

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. The Lego illustration of simplicity (rating: strong; clear exposition)
"If I have a Lego house, the house is one thing, but it is one thing that is actually made up of many other things. So the Lego house depends on the other things, its parts, for its existence... So the house is not the most ultimate and fundamental thing." Therefore the most fundamental reality "can't be made of multiple things. It can't have parts."[^1] This is a faithful popular statement of the classical doctrine defended in [[Divine Simplicity - God Has No Parts of Any Kind]].

### 2. Simplicity entails hard monotheism (rating: strong; follows from the premise)
"In classical theism, God must be one, totally unique thing. Like the one Lego that started it all. This is why classical theism is also a hard monotheism. It says you can only have one God... there can only be one fundamental thing that can properly be called God."[^3] This is exactly the classical inference stated positively in [[Divine Unity - There Can Be Only One God]] — the video simply reads it as a liability rather than an asset.

### 3. Hard monotheism excludes distinct divine persons (rating: moderate; the pivotal step)
"This immediately rules out the possibility of Jesus and the Father both being properly called God. Because you can only have one thing, and Jesus and the Father are clearly two different things."[^2] The video then presses the individuation question: the Father is not the Son, the Son is not the Spirit, the Spirit is not the Father — "then what makes them different?... There has to be something. Because if there isn't, then they are literally the same person."[^4] *Argument weight: primary.* Compare the substance-equivocation form of the same dilemma in [[The Trinity Cannot Count to Three]].

### 4. The Trinity is the resulting contortion (rating: moderate; polemical)
Because they must keep the persons distinct *and* keep God one "thing," "theologians have now spent centuries trying to explain how three genuinely distinct persons, three different things, are actually just one thing. And this is where the doctrine of the Trinity comes from. It's an attempt to try and turn three things into one thing"[^5] — which is why the explanations "constantly become really strange and abstract." The video's exhibit is an embedded clip of an analytic philosopher defending the Trinity via one divine "substance" bearing distinct location-specific "aspects" and first-person perspectives at disjoint regions R1, R2, R3 (building on Lynn Rudder Baker's constitution view of persons) — offered as evidence the model no longer "sound[s] like Peter preaching on Pentecost" but "like philosophers trying to solve a problem created by philosophy."[^6]

### 5. Biblical "oneness" need not mean one substance (rating: moderate; exegetical)
The video grants "the Father and the Son are one in the Bible, but what does that mean?" and answers that "people can be one in the sense that they're perfectly united, totally in unison" — the unity of [[John 10.30|John 10:30]] and the Shema ([[Deuteronomy 6.4|Deuteronomy 6:4]]) read as covenantal/relational oneness, not numerical identity of being.[^7] This is the reading developed in [[John 17 Oneness Is Unity Not Shared Being]].

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
**The strongest opposing case.** The classical theist replies that the argument equivocates on "thing." (a) The doctrine of the Trinity precisely *denies* that the persons are three "things" in the sense that would violate simplicity: they are three *subsistent relations* (or *hypostases*) within one indivisible divine essence, distinguished by relations of origin (unbegotten / begotten / proceeding) rather than by being separate parts or substances. So "what distinguishes them?" has a classical answer — relation — that does not add parts to God. (b) Simplicity secures real goods: it answers "what grounds God?" without regress and blocks God from being merely one more member of a genus (a "superangel"). (c) The "sounds like philosophers, not Peter" move is a genetic/aesthetic complaint, not a disproof; that a formulation is technical does not make it false, and the Nicene fathers claimed to be *protecting* the biblical data (Jesus worshipped as God; the Father as the one God) from misreading, not inventing a puzzle.

**The LDS response.** "Subsistent relations" is exactly where the LDS presses: distinguishing persons *only* by relation, while insisting they are one simple being, is precisely the move critics find unintelligible — a distinction with no distinct *thing* to bear it (the dilemma of §3). The Latter-day Saint bites the bullet the video sets up: the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost really are three distinct persons/beings, "one" in perfect unity of mind, will, and purpose (the [[John 17 Oneness Is Unity Not Shared Being|John 17]] model), and God's ultimacy is secured by the Father's *self-existence*, not by a simplicity that must then be paid for with centuries of paradox. The real dispute is whether simplicity is a scriptural datum or an imported axiom — which the video argues is Greek (see [[The Deepest Apostasy Was Trading the God of Abraham for the God of Aristotle]]).
