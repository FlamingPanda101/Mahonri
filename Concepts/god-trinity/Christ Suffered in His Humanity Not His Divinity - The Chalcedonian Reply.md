# Christ Suffered in His Humanity Not His Divinity - The Chalcedonian Reply

## The Claim (Catholic rebuttal)

This is the Catholic answer to [[Jacob Hansen]]'s syllogism that Catholics *cannot* claim God became man: (P1) a single person cannot have two mutually exclusive natures; (P2) divine and human natures are mutually exclusive (uncreated/impassible/unchangeable vs. created/passible/changeable); (C) therefore no one person can be both fully divine and fully human.[^1] The ten Catholics reject **premise one** using the Chalcedonian distinction between *person* and *nature* plus the **communication of idioms**: the one divine person of Christ genuinely suffered and died *in his humanity*, while the divine nature, present, underwent no modification. The contradiction, they argue, arises only from Hansen's mislocation of *operations* (like suffering) in *natures* rather than in the *person*.

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. Person is not nature (rating: the load-bearing distinction; argument weight: primary)
"A nature is a set of properties... it describes what you *are*"; a *person* is "the particular instantiation of a nature," its concrete subject. Chalcedon confesses one person (hypostasis) subsisting *in two natures* "without confusion, change, division, or separation" — the natures are *not fused* into a third hybrid thing. See [[Ephesus and Chalcedon Defined the Two Natures of Christ]] and [[Hypostatic Union]].[^2]

### 2. The square-and-triangle, corrected (rating: clarifying analogy; argument weight: secondary)
When Hansen presses that "one shape" cannot be both a square and a circle, the Catholic accepts the analogy but corrects its use: the incarnation is "not that the square and the triangle *form* to make one shape that is both." Rather, "Jesus *possesses* both a square and a triangle at the same time without conflict" — two natures held by one subject, unmixed. Hansen's "one shape that is both" pictures the *monophysite* error Chalcedon condemned, not the Catholic view.[^3]

### 3. Communication of idioms — operations belong to the person through the proper nature (rating: the crux; argument weight: primary)
"Natures don't suffer, people do." So on the cross "a divine person... suffered and died *in his humanity*"; "the suffering is proper to the humanity of Jesus." The person is the single subject to whom both the divine and human predicates truly attach, but each operation proceeds from its proper nature. Thus one can say *God died* (true of the person) without saying *the divine nature died* (false).[^4]

### 4. Impassibility preserved (rating: internal consistency; argument weight: primary)
The Catholics grant Hansen's premise that the divine nature cannot change or suffer — and keep it: "modifications occur to all things *except* the divine nature." The divine nature was "present on the cross," but "the person suffered"; the divine nature did not. See [[God Is Impassible - Acting Without Being Acted Upon]].[^5]

### 5. Death is separation of soul and flesh, not of spirit and soul (rating: a distinctive move; argument weight: primary)
Their most technical reply: Scripture principles God as *spirit* ("God is spirit," [[John 4.24|John 4:24]]) and man as *soul* ("Jesus Christ has a human soul," Mark 10:45). Death is defined as "the separation of soul and flesh." So the passion/suffering "is not placed between spirit and soul but rather between soul and flesh." The union of the divine *spirit* with the human *soul* is therefore *not* broken by the death on the cross — "that does not disunify the principles" — which is how the divine person can truly die *in the flesh* without the divine nature being sundered or altered.[^6]

### 6. Galatians 4:4 — two verbs, one person (rating: scriptural support; argument weight: secondary)
Paul writes that God "*sent forth* (ἐξαπέστειλεν, *exapostellō*) his Son, *born* (γενόμενον, *ginomenon*) of a woman" ([[Galatians 4.4|Galatians 4:4]]). The Catholics argue the two distinct verbs — one for the Son's procession from the Father (pre-existent/divine), one for his being born of Mary (human) — are predicated of *one* person, scripturally signaling the two natures/origins united in a single subject.[^7]

### 7. "Person" is not merely a center of consciousness (rating: an internal-critique block; argument weight: primary)
Hansen defines a person "minimally... as a center of consciousness," then argues two minds = two persons. The Catholics reject the premise: that is "not what a person is for us." Since his argument was billed as an *internal* critique ("Catholics cannot affirm..."), importing a non-Catholic definition of *person* means he has not refuted the Catholic position on its own terms. (Constantinople III already affirmed Christ has *two wills*, not two persons.)[^8]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

Hansen's objection at full strength, and his rejoinders:

1. **A nature is what you *are*, not what you *possess*.** "There's a little linguistic game that gets played there where you say, 'well, Jesus *possesses* these two things.' But... a nature describes what you *are*." If one subject genuinely *is* both created and uncreated, impassible and passible, omniscient and non-omniscient, that is a single contradictory list of properties — relabeling it "possession" does not dissolve it.[^9]

2. **The one sufferer on the cross.** "There's only one person on that cross." If that person *is* fully divine (has impassibility as a property of what he is), "that one person can't suffer" — yet the New Testament says he wept, did not know the hour (Mark 13:32), and genuinely suffered. So the *person* both can and cannot suffer: "a flaw in basic logic."[^10]

3. **Infinite and finite in one.** "Can something be both infinite and finite at the same time?" Since a nature is what a thing *is*, a subject that is *both* an infinite thing and a finite thing is incoherent, not merely mysterious.[^11]

4. **Two minds, two consciousnesses.** If the orthodox grant Jesus *two minds*, then "you have two centers of consciousness" — which, on Hansen's analysis, is two persons, undercutting the one-person confession.[^12]

**Where the tension sits.** The debate reduces to one question: does *suffering* (and impassibility, createdness, etc.) belong to the **person** or to the **nature**? The Catholic insists these are predicated of the *person through a nature*, so no single subject holds contradictory properties *in the same respect*; Hansen insists a nature just *is* the subject's list of properties, so splitting them across "natures" is a verbal maneuver that relocates rather than removes the contradiction. Notably, Hansen never grants premise one is *refuted* — he grants only that he "understands the Chalcedonian take" — while the Catholics never grant that operations can be assigned to natures. The two frameworks talk past each other at the person/nature seam.

## LDS Relevance

The Latter-day Saint escape is different from either horn: LDS theology denies premise two's *ontological gap* — "we don't make that ontological distinction between God and man" (see [[God and Man Are the Same Species]]). If divinity and humanity are not different *kinds*, there is no "mutually exclusive natures" problem to solve, and the hypostatic union is unnecessary. Hansen's syllogism is thus aimed squarely at the *creedal* framework; the broader LDS critique — that the whole apparatus is a Greek solution to a Greek problem — is developed in [[The Hypostatic Union Is a Greek Solution to a Greek Problem]].
