# The Image of God Is Divine Resemblance and Royal Representation

#type/claim

## The Claim

To be created "in the image of God" (*tselem*) and "after his likeness" (*demut*) in [[Genesis 1.26-27|Genesis 1:26-27]] means, on the majority scholarly view, that humanity is a **divine image** in the ancient sense of an idol or cult statue. That carries two layers. **First and fundamentally, appearance:** the authors of Genesis 1 and 2 conceived of God as human-shaped, so humans are made in "more or less the same shape, the same appearance as God." **Second, by extension, representation:** an image *indexes* and *manifests* the deity — bears his presence, power, authority, and agency on earth — so humanity is God's counterpart and representative. Genesis 1 further **democratizes** a royal ideology (the Mesopotamian/Egyptian king as "the image of a god"), extending that status from the king to all humanity.[^1] [[Dan McClellan]] presents this as the mainstream biblical-studies reading, drawing on [[W. Randall Garr]] and others.

## Supporting Evidence

1. **The lexical data: *tselem* = divine image, *demut* = outward form.** *Tselem* is "the word for a divine image"; the cognate Akkadian *tsalmu* in Mesopotamia is likewise the word for a divine image, and *demut* means "likeness, appearance, outward form." Both appear in Genesis 1.[^2] *(Evidence rating: Strong — this is standard comparative-Semitic lexicography; [[W. Randall Garr]]'s monograph is the fullest treatment.)*

2. **The fundamental sense is appearance — because God is human-shaped.** A divine image "usually was supposed to in some sense or combination of senses physically outwardly resemble the deity." Therefore "for the author of Genesis 1 and the author of Genesis 2, the idea would have been God is human-shaped. And so humans are created in more or less the same shape, the same appearance as God."[^3] *(Evidence rating: Strong; coheres with the broader corporeal-God consensus, see [[Anthropomorphic God in the Hebrew Bible]].)*

3. **An image indexes and manifests the deity — it is not a mere object of worship.** McClellan corrects a common misconception: "A lot of people think they are just objects of worship, which is simply not true. The divine image was there to manifest the presence, the power, the agency of the deity" for petition, gratitude, worship, communion, or monitoring. So a human, as *imago Dei*, is "the outward sign of the presence and the power and the authority of the deity who is unseen."[^4] *(Evidence rating: Strong — the cognitive mechanism is developed at length in [[Partible Personhood and the Logic of Divine Images]].)*

4. **The royal-ideology layer, then democratized.** Genesis 1 is written in the exilic/post-exilic period "in light of the ideologies about royalty... the notion of royalty as the image of God." Kings in Mesopotamian and Egyptian literature "are referred to as the image, the divine image, the idol of a given deity," a status "restricted to royalty." Genesis 1 democratizes it so that "all humanity, and particularly us" become "the royalty of Earth," possessing and manifesting "God's authority, God's agency on Earth."[^5] *(Evidence rating: Strong; Argument weight: Primary — this is the distinctive move of the priestly account.)*

5. **Garr fuses image with the divine council.** Per [[W. Randall Garr]], the *elohim* of the council look like both God and humans, so *tselem*/*demut* "indicate that human beings resemble God because they resemble angelic godlike beings that resemble God" — binding the image to the plural "let us" of the same verse (see [[The Plural of Genesis 1 Is the Divine Council Not the Trinity or Royal We]]).[^1] *(Evidence rating: Moderate — Garr's synthesis; McClellan endorses the monograph while quibbling with its monotheism framing.)*

## Apologetic Engagement

**Steelman (the traditional/creedal reading first).** Classical and much modern Christian theology reads the *imago Dei* as a **non-physical** likeness — rationality, moral agency, relationality, or vocation — precisely so that the incorporeal God of the creeds ("without body, parts, or passions") can still be "imaged" by embodied creatures. On this reading, "image" language is analogical: humans mirror God's *capacities*, not his *shape*. This is a coherent position with a long pedigree (Augustine's rational soul; Barth's relational reading; Middleton's functional-royal reading, which McClellan also praises), and it can absorb the royal-representative layer without conceding divine embodiment: a king can image an invisible god functionally.

**Where McClellan's reading supports the Latter-day Saint view.** The *historical-grammatical* core he defends is strongly congenial to LDS theology. If *tselem*/*demut* are "fundamentally about outward appearance" and the authors thought "God is human-shaped," then Genesis 1 anchors an **embodied, anthropomorphic God** whose form humans literally share — exactly the Restoration claim that "the Father has a body of flesh and bones as tangible as man's" (D&C 130:22) and that Adam was created in the physical image of God (Moses 6:9). The royal-representative layer maps cleanly onto the LDS reading of human divine potential and stewardship. And Garr's fusion of image with the divine council dovetails with the LDS divine-council cosmology.

**Where it diverges.** McClellan is doing critical scholarship, not apologetics, and two cautions follow. (a) He frames the representative layer as an ancient *ideological construct* — Israel "one-upping" Mesopotamian royal propaganda after the exile — not as a metaphysical claim that humans are literally divine offspring; the LDS reading supplies that theological surplus. (b) He explicitly "quibbles" with the sharp "dichotomous notion of humanity and divinity," which cuts against any tidy creature/Creator wall but does not, by itself, deliver ontological kinship. The verse establishes shared *shape and function*, and underdetermines the further question of shared *nature* — which LDS theology answers from Restoration scripture, not from Genesis 1 alone.

## LDS Relevance

This is one of the most LDS-friendly readings in mainstream biblical studies. It reads Genesis 1:26-27 the way the Restoration does: God is embodied and human-shaped, humanity physically resembles him, and humans are his authorized representatives and agents on earth — a "democratized" royal/priestly status that resonates with the LDS doctrines of the divine potential of every person, [[Anthropomorphic God Grounds Heavenly Mother and Eternal Families|Heavenly Parents]], and stewardship over the earth. It directly undercuts the creedal move to spiritualize the image in order to protect an incorporeal God. The chief caveat: McClellan grounds the embodiment in ancient authorial intent and comparative Near Eastern data, not in a doctrine of literal divine parentage; the LDS reading extends his exegesis with covenant theology it does not supply on its own.
