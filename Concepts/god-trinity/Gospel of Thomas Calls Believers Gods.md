# Gospel of Thomas Calls Believers Gods

## The Claim
A Gospel of Thomas saying read on air — "Where there are gods, they are gods; where there are two or one, I am with them" — is, [[Jonah Barnes]] argues, Jesus **calling human beings** ***gods***. The saying's second half mirrors [[Matthew 18.20|Matthew 18:20]] ("where two or three are gathered in my name, there am I in the midst of them"), so it reads as an alternate, more explicit version of that logion in which the gathered disciples are named divine. [^1]

## Supporting Evidence

1. **The wording predicates deity of the plurality, not just of Christ.** *Argument weight: Primary.* "Where there are three gods, they are gods; where there are two or one, I am with them." The panel's reading: "He's calling us gods… I think he is." The subject of "they are gods" is the gathered believers. [^1]
2. **It is a doublet of a canonical saying.** *Argument weight: Secondary.* It runs parallel to Matthew 18:20 — "this is saying a different version of that" — which lends it plausibility as authentic Jesus tradition while sharpening the divinization language the canonical form leaves implicit. [^2]
3. **It coheres with Thomas's wider divine-sonship theme.** *Argument weight: Secondary.* Read alongside logion 3's "sons of the living father" (see [[Gospel of Thomas Teaches Self-Knowledge as Divine Sonship]]), Thomas repeatedly places believers on the divine side of the ledger — the deification pattern the vault documents in [[Divine Council and Deification Were Native to the Hebrew Bible]] and [[Disciples Share the Throne and Divine Glory]]. [^2]

## Best Quotes
> "Where there are gods, they are gods; where there are two or one, I am with them… He's calling us gods." [^1]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
- **The Greek witness reads the opposite.** The famous Oxyrhynchus Greek form of this saying (logion 30) is not "where there are gods, they are gods" but "where there are three, they are *without God*, and where there is but one, I am with him." The manuscripts are unstable at exactly this line, and the "they are gods" reading Barnes uses is the Coptic Nag Hammadi version — so the deification wording may be a *translation-dependent* variant, not the primitive text.
- **The panel offers the deflationary reading itself.** [[Austin Falter]] proposes it may mean the *Godhead* — "the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit… where any one or two of them, the Father is with them" — i.e. a statement about divine presence, not about deifying the disciples. On that reading nothing distinctively LDS follows.
- **Even granting "ye are gods," it is not unique to Thomas.** The strongest deification prooftext is canonical — Psalm 82 / [[John 10-35's Unbreakable Scripture Is the Deification Text Ye Are Gods|John 10:34-35]] — so this logion adds color, not a load-bearing new argument. Its value is corroborative.
- **A single, textually contested logion carries little weight.** Barnes concedes elsewhere the fragment "doesn't say anything that supports Mormon doctrine particularly"; the deification reading rests on a disputed line in a source rated tier C. Present it as suggestive resonance, cite the canonical deification notes as the real evidence.
