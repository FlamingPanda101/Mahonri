# Anthropomorphism Is the Cognitive Default for Conceptualizing Deity

#type/claim

## The Claim

Conceiving of God as an unembodied, non-anthropomorphic essence is not the natural human baseline; it is an effortful, *reflective* override of a deeper **intuitive** default. [[Dan McClellan]] argues, drawing on the cognitive science of religion (esp. [[Justin Barrett]]), that because deities are **unseen agents**, humans most naturally and intuitively represent them as anthropomorphic — human-shaped, occupying a single point in space and time. Even orthodox believers revert to anthropomorphic God-talk when not actively policing their theology. The abstract, incorporeal God is a later, philosophically-driven achievement that has to be maintained *against* the cognitive grain — and in the biblical period that intuitive, anthropomorphic conception "sat much closer to the surface."[^1] This supplies a *cognitive* argument alongside the *textual* one in [[Anthropomorphic God in the Hebrew Bible]].

## Supporting Evidence

1. **The biblical baseline is anthropomorphic.** "When we look at the Bible, God is fundamentally conceptualized as anthropomorphic, as a human-shaped, male-presenting entity who occupies a single point in space and time." The non-anthropomorphic exceptions are "generally figurative or metaphorical... or it is the imposition after the Bible was completed of the standards of philosophy."[^1] *(Evidence rating: Strong; Argument weight: Primary.)*

2. **It was the ancient rule, not an Israelite quirk.** "That anthropomorphic conceptualization of deity was the rule throughout ancient West Asia and down to the periods of the composition of the New Testament. Gods were fundamentally anthropomorphic."[^2] Even where a culture avoided picturing a god's body, "they still imposed anthropopathic ideas about their thinking, their perception, their speech."[^3] *(Evidence rating: Strong — comparative ANE consensus.)*

3. **The cognitive mechanism: unseen agents default to anthropomorphic.** Because "deities are unseen agents in the world around us... they are going to be intuitively, most naturally conceptualized as anthropomorphic." McClellan cites "research by Barrett and others" that "even Orthodox Christians today, when they're not actively contemplating specific features of the Orthodox conceptualization of deity, will retreat to anthropomorphic representations of God... because that is more intuitive, that is more natural."[^4] *(Evidence rating: Strong — this is Barrett's well-replicated "theological correctness" finding; see [[Justin Barrett]].)*

4. **Orthodoxy is a *reflective* override that must suppress the intuition.** Orthodoxy "has given rise to a more influential reflective cognition that frequently has to overrule the more intuitive side of things." When believers "are cued to the Orthodox beliefs on which they have signed off, they tend to try to suppress the more intuitive conceptualizations of deity so that they can signal their adherence to the credences."[^5] On the "spectrum between intuitive cognition to reflective cognition, the non-anthropomorphic concept of deity is more reflective, and so frequently has to do battle against the more intuitive conceptualization of God as anthropomorphic."[^6] *(Evidence rating: Strong.)*

5. **In the biblical period the intuitive side sat closer to the surface.** "When we go back to the biblical periods, the more intuitive side of things sat much closer to the surface, the more reflective side of things less commonly overruled." Where reflective cognition *did* overrule it, "that was generally when we're talking about philosophical inquiry" — so "the more philosophically oriented the thinker, the more likely they are to push back against the gravitational pull of the intuitive conceptualization of deity as anthropomorphic."[^7] *(Evidence rating: Moderate — a plausible historical inference from the cognitive model; Argument weight: Secondary.)*

## Best Quotes

## Apologetic Engagement

**Steelman (the classical-theist reply).** A classical theist can grant the entire cognitive story and deny that it settles anything about *truth*. That anthropomorphism is intuitive is a fact about human *psychology*, not about God; indeed the natural-default status of a concept can count *against* its accuracy (we also intuitively perceive the sun as orbiting the earth). On this reading, the movement from an intuitive humanlike God to the incorporeal God of Nicaea and the *Summa* is precisely intellectual and spiritual *progress* — revelation and reason correcting a crude default — not the corruption of an authentic original. The apologist can even reclaim Barrett: the reflective override exists because it is *closer to the truth*, which is why disciplined theology insists on it.

**Where McClellan's reading supports the Latter-day Saint view.** The apologetic payoff is not that "intuitive = true," but that it dissolves a rhetorical asymmetry. Creedal theology often presents the incorporeal God as the *obvious, higher, default* view and the embodied God as a naive misreading. McClellan inverts the burden: the anthropomorphic God is the **native** conception of the biblical authors and audiences, and incorporeality is the *late import* requiring philosophical machinery to sustain. For a tradition (LDS) that reads Genesis and the theophanies at face value, that is a strong tailwind — the "plain sense" is not a concession to naivety but the historically primary reading. See [[Anthropomorphic God in the Hebrew Bible]].

**Where it diverges.** McClellan's argument is historical-descriptive and metaphysically neutral: it establishes what ancient people *believed* and why it was cognitively natural, not that an embodied God *exists*. It cannot, by itself, defeat the classical theist's "intuitive-but-mistaken" rejoinder — that requires the separate textual, exegetical, and theological case. The LDS apologist should deploy this as *removing the presumption* in favor of incorporeality, not as a positive proof of divine embodiment.

## LDS Relevance

This reframes the entire embodiment debate to the Restoration's advantage. The stock objection to an embodied God is that it is a primitive, anthropomorphic projection later "outgrown" by sophisticated theology. McClellan's cognitive-science case shows the embodied, humanlike God is the *original and native* conception of the biblical writers, while the incorporeal essence is the reflective latecomer that must constantly suppress the intuitive reading. The Latter-day Saint who affirms a corporeal, anthropomorphic God (D&C 130:22) is therefore reading *with* the grain of the ancient text and its authors, not against it — and the creedal insistence on incorporeality is exposed as the innovation. The limit: cognitive naturalness is not proof of truth, so this clears the ground rather than closing the case; pair it with [[The Image of God Is Divine Resemblance and Royal Representation]] and the theophany texts.
