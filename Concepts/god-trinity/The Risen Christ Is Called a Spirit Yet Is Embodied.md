# The Risen Christ Is Called a Spirit Yet Is Embodied

#type/rebuttal

## The Claim (Objection Rebutted)
The evangelical / anti-Mormon soundbite: [[John 4.24|John 4:24]] says "God is a Spirit," and Latter-day Saints say God the Father "has a body of flesh and bone," so the LDS view is "incompatible with Scripture." The panel grants the potency of the attack — "in two sentences I just took down Mormonism for some 15-year-old that's listening."[^1] This note gives the Latter-day Saint response that turns on the Bible's *own* spirit-language, rather than on John 4:24's context or Greek (for those, see [[John 4-24 - God Is Spirit Rebutted]]).

## Supporting Evidence

1. **The consistency test — the Bible calls the embodied Christ a "spirit."** Ground the objector in Scripture, then apply the same rule elsewhere: "Do you believe Jesus Christ is a spirit?" — "No, he has a resurrected body." — "Then why does the Bible say that he's a spirit?"[^2] [[1 Corinthians 15.45-46|1 Corinthians 15:45]]: "the last Adam was made a quickening spirit," and "the last Adam is Jesus Christ."[^3] Trinitarians make Christ's *embodiment* a defining trait, yet here Scripture calls him a spirit — "thus proving that it's not mutually exclusive." The reader who takes "God is spirit" (John 4:24) to mean "God is bodiless" must, to stay consistent, take "the last Adam a quickening spirit" to mean the risen Christ is bodiless — which he denies. *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Strong as an internal (tu-quoque) consistency check.*

2. **Modern translations keep the spirit-language.** It is not a KJV quirk: NIV — "the last Adam a life-giving spirit"; New Living Translation — "the last Adam, that is Christ, is a life-giving spirit." The label "spirit" for Christ survives across versions.[^4] *Argument weight: Secondary.*

3. **The "a spirit" vs "spirit" retreat is a translation artifact.** Some critics regroup: John 4:24 says God is "spirit" (no article) whereas 1 Corinthians 15:45 says Christ became "a … spirit." But Koine Greek has no indefinite article — the "a" is inserted by English translators — so the two constructions are grammatically the same, and the distinction "is a moot point."[^5] *Argument weight: Secondary (closes a rebuttal escape route).*

4. **The lexicon is non-LDS.** The qualitative range of *pneuma* is not an LDS invention: a Blue Letter Bible / Strong's entry (an evangelical resource) glosses it as "breath, blast, or a breeze … figuratively a spirit … the rational soul … or … divine." So "God is spirit ⇒ formless, substanceless, no body" is "an incorrect interpretation, or at least … not totally fair or accurate to the original."[^6] Detail in [[John 4-24 - God Is Spirit Rebutted]].

## Best Quotes

> "How come you ignore the Bible when it says that Jesus Christ is a spirit in 1 Corinthians 15:45?"[^2]

> "According to Trinitarians, one of the defining characteristics of Jesus Christ is that he is embodied. Here it defines him as a spirit, thus proving that it's not mutually exclusive."[^3]

> "You can't pick and choose when God only has a spirit and when God has a body."[^7]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
**The strongest evangelical reply.** The parallel arguably equivocates on "spirit." In context (1 Corinthians 15:44), Paul contrasts a "natural body" with a "spiritual body" (*sōma pneumatikon*) and calls the risen Christ a "life-giving spirit" (*pneuma zōopoioun*) — language most exegetes read as the resurrection body *animated and empowered by the Spirit*, and Christ's *life-giving role* as the eschatological Adam, not a claim that Christ is immaterial. Verse 44 explicitly affirms a resurrection *body*. So 1 Corinthians 15:45 does not put the risen Christ on all fours with the (allegedly bodiless) God of John 4:24; the two "spirit" statements may carry different senses.

**Why the LDS point still lands.** Note that this very reply *concedes the core claim*: "spirit" applied to Christ in 15:45 plainly does **not** entail bodilessness — it coexists with a resurrection body. That is exactly the inference LDS resist in John 4:24. The consistency argument therefore succeeds at its modest job — showing that biblical "spirit"-predication is not a proof of bodilessness — even if it overreaches when pressed as a strict equation of the two verses. The reverse-direction tension (an embodied Son within a bodiless divine essence) is developed in [[Embodied Risen Christ Strains the Trinity]].

**Candour (from the panel).** The hosts admit the defense is wordier than the attack — "any jackass can kick down a door, but it takes a builder to build a barn" — and name soundbite-refinement as unfinished work.[^8]
