# Apologists Claimed the Biblical God Was the Same God as the Greek Philosophers

## The Claim
Thoughtful Faith argues that the decisive wrong turn was not merely borrowing Greek *vocabulary* but the apologists' explicit *identification* of the God of the Bible with the God of the philosophers. Facing Greek intellectuals who mocked a God who changes, dies on a cross, and points to a Father greater than himself, the second-century apologists — instead of "rejecting the very assumptions and frameworks of Greek metaphysics" — "granted them" and "started to try and argue that their God, the God of the Bible, was the same God as the God of the Greek philosophers."[^1] That identification, the video says, is what pulled the immaterial, timeless, impassible Absolute into the center of Christian theology.

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. The Greek objections that set the agenda (rating: moderate; intellectual history)
As Christianity spread into the Greek-speaking intellectual centers (Athens, Alexandria), "the Greek intellectuals mocked Christianity because they felt it could not be rationally defended" against their notions of God: "The Christian God seemed to change. The Christian God died on a cross," and since only one thing could be the ultimate cause, "Jesus could not be God" because he "clearly pointed to his father as more ultimate than him" (cf. [[John 14.28|John 14:28]]).[^2] These pressures — not scripture — framed the questions the apologists set out to answer.

### 2. Apologists raised inside Greco-Roman culture (rating: strong; historical)
"Apologists like [[Tertullian]], [[Irenaeus of Lyons|Irenaeus]], and [[Justin Martyr]] who were born and raised in the Greco-Roman culture" sought "to provide a defense against Greek objections to Christianity," shifting the center of gravity from the Jewish thought-world of Jesus and the apostles to Greek philosophical frameworks.[^3] The same audience-shift is documented from an academic angle in [[Trinity Built to Satisfy Greco-Roman Philosophy]].

### 3. The "hammer and nail" diagnosis (rating: moderate; interpretive)
The video grants the apologists "had good intentions. They weren't trying to corrupt Christianity"; but "if all you have is a hammer, everything eventually starts looking like a nail." Having accepted Greek metaphysics as the tool, they read the Bible's God as the philosophers' God.[^1] This is the mechanism behind [[Unmoved Mover Reshaped God the Father]] and [[Greek Philosophy Corrupted the Christian Concept of God]].

### 4. Theophilus of Antioch's litany of negations (rating: strong; primary exhibit)
A scholarly source quoted in the video states that since the apologists first addressed pagan intellectuals, "Christian thinkers had claimed to worship the same God honored by the Greeks and Romans," adopting "precisely the same language to describe God as did pagan intellectuals." The exhibit: [[Theophilus of Antioch]] "described God as ineffable, inexpressible, uncontainable, incomprehensible, inconceivable, incomparable, unteachable, immutable." The quoted source concludes that this immaterial, timeless, impassible God "became the keystone of Christian apologetics, for it served to establish a decisive link to the Greek spiritual and intellectual tradition."[^4] *Argument weight: primary — the identification is stated in the apologists' own idiom.*

### 5. The endpoint: an impassible God the Bible must be bent to fit (rating: moderate; downstream)
Once the philosophers' God is granted, impassibility follows — later crystallized in the Westminster Confession's "without body, parts, or passions," quoted in the video as the mature form of the doctrine.[^5] On this footing "you start reading the Bible through that lens," reinterpreting the grieving, relenting, covenant-making Yahweh until he fits. See [[Without Body Parts or Passions Is a 1530 Protestant Creed]] and [[The Creedal God Is the Platonic One Not the God of the Bible]].

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
**The strongest opposing case.** Defenders argue the apologists *translated* rather than *replaced*. (a) Claiming the philosophers unknowingly honored the true God is a biblical move, not a capitulation — Paul at the Areopagus tells the Athenians the "unknown God" they already worship is the Creator (Acts 17:23), and Justin's *logos spermatikos* holds that whatever truth the Greeks grasped was Christ's own seed. (b) Theophilus's negations are apophatic guardrails — ways of denying that God is a creature (finite, corruptible, teachable) — with deep roots in Hebrew transcendence ("to whom will you liken me?" Isaiah 40), not pagan imports. (c) Adopting an interlocutor's *vocabulary* to contest his *conclusions* is standard missionary contextualization; the apologists used Greek terms to argue *for* the crucified, embodied Christ the Greeks found absurd — the opposite of surrender. The disciplined verdict: that the apologists engaged Greek categories is undeniable; whether that engagement *corrupted* the doctrine of God or *clarified* it is the contested question (see [[Hellenization Was Not Corruption of an Original Pure Church]]).

**The LDS response.** Contextualization is legitimate until it inverts the authority order — and the video's charge is that the apologists let the Greek *definition* of God become the fixed point to which scripture had to conform, rather than the reverse. The tell is the impassible, partless God of Theophilus's litany: a description assembled from negations congenial to Aristotle, then defended by re-reading the Bible's relational God as mere appearance. Where Paul's Areopagus speech ends at a God who "raised him from the dead" — bodily, in history — the apologetic trajectory ends at a God "without body, parts, or passions," which is the very reversal the Restoration undoes.
