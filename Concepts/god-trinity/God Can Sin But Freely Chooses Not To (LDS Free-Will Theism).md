# God Can Sin But Freely Chooses Not To (LDS Free-Will Theism)

## The Claim
[[Hayden Carroll]] argues that God has **genuine libertarian free will**, which entails that God *can* sin — He simply, freely, and always chooses not to. This, he says, *preserves* rather than undermines God as the moral standard, and makes God's goodness praiseworthy (a free choice against a live alternative rather than the output of a nature that "cannot do otherwise"). A striking corollary he affirms from LDS doctrine: a god "can cease to be god" — falling from exaltation is theoretically possible.[^1]

## Supporting Evidence

1. **Free will requires the real possibility of doing otherwise.** Asked point-blank "can God sin?" Carroll answers: "He can, but he chooses not to. Our God has free will." Pressed that this breaks God's status as the standard, he replies, "He can, but he doesn't. So he's still the standard."[^2] *Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate* — internally coherent given a libertarian account of freedom.

2. **A being that "cannot do otherwise" merits no praise — the robot charge.** Carroll turns [[Greg (Collierville Pastor)]]'s immutable God into the robot: "if a being cannot do otherwise, is it worthy of any praise at all?... God is only doing that which is in his nature. It's like a robot."[^3] Praiseworthiness, on this view, presupposes that the good was freely chosen. *Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate* — a genuine intuition about moral praise, though it assumes libertarianism.

3. **Practical equivalence.** Carroll grants the outcome is the same as Greg's: God "won't" fall, so "practically it's the same" — indeed the whole plan "cannot be orchestrated without a perfect, sinless God," so a fall would be immediately evident. The possibility is real but never actualizes.[^4]

4. **Doctrinal backing and a key nuance.** LDS scripture has each kingdom governed by a law (D&C 88), and teaches a god "can cease to be god."[^1] *Important nuance / steelman of his own view:* Carroll distinguishes *metaphysically able to sin* from *morally sinless in fact*. He is willing to say "God the Father has been morally perfect forever — I don't have a problem saying that." So the claim is not that God sinned, only that God's sinlessness is a free achievement, not a metaphysical incapacity.[^5]

## Best Quotes
> "He can, but he chooses not to. Yeah, our God has free will, is what we would say."[^2]

> "If a being cannot do otherwise... is it worthy of any praise at all?... God is only doing that which is in his nature. It's like a robot."[^3]

> "God can cease to be god. So, theoretically, could you fall from your exaltation? Theoretically, absolutely."[^1]

> "I don't have a problem saying that God the Father has been morally perfect forever."[^5]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
[[Greg (Collierville Pastor)]] defends the classical-theist view that God is *unable* to sin, and his responses are strong:

- **Inability to sin is a perfection, not a limitation.** "To be only of truth is actually a superpower... like you only have the ability to hit home runs. That's not a weakness, that's a strength." Moral law is grounded in God's very nature (1 John 1:5, "God is light and in him is no darkness at all"), so sin is not a *possibility* God declines but a contradiction of who He is.[^6] *This directly answers the robot charge:* acting infallibly from a perfect nature is the height of freedom, not mechanism — the blessed in heaven are free yet *non posse peccare* ("not able to sin").

- **A God who could fall is not the biblical "rock."** Greg's worship-argument: scripture presents God as "a steadfast, immovable rock... from everlasting to everlasting" (Malachi 3:6, "I the Lord change not"). "If that light can fall and go out... it's not the steadfast rock I thought it was — that's really scary and depressing."[^7] A defeasible God cannot ground ultimate hope. See [[Malachi 3.6|Malachi 3:6]] and [[Divine Immutability - God Cannot Change]].

- **A deviable standard is no standard.** "If he can sin... he's no longer the standard." A rule that can itself err cannot be the measure of right and wrong.[^2]

- **The dialectical mirror.** Both men deploy the robot analogy: earlier Greg uses it to *force* Carroll to grant God has free will (if God simply "can only do according to his nature," that sounds robotic), then Carroll throws it back at Greg's *non posse peccare* God. The disagreement is really about **what freedom is** — the power to do evil (Carroll) versus unconstrained self-determination *toward* the good (Greg). Greg also notes Christ was "like us in every way but without sin" (Hebrews 4:15), so sinlessness and full personhood are compatible.[^8]

**Fair concession to Carroll:** his "morally perfect forever" nuance blunts the crudest version of the objection — he is *not* committed to a God who ever sinned, only to a God whose goodness is chosen. The remaining dispute is whether a God who *could* have done otherwise is more praiseworthy (Carroll) or less secure as the ground of moral order and hope (Greg). This connects to the [[Infinite and Eternal Are Not the Same - The Regress Objection Rebutted|infinite-regress vs. monarchical]] models of God's origin and the [[Lorenzo Snow]] couplet debated alongside it.
