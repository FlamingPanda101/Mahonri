# A Feeling With No Sexual Content Is Not Homosexual

## The Claim
[[Jeff Robinson]] presses a diagnostic test on his clients: *"if a thought or a feeling
you have about another man has no sexual content, it is not homosexual. Period."* Male
admiration, a "man crush," a bromance, envy, hurt that someone wasn't a better friend,
longing to belong — all of these can occur in men who have never had a homosexual feeling,
so having them is not evidence of homosexuality. The self-check: *"could a man who's never
experienced homosexual feelings have the feelings I'm having? If they could, it's not
homosexual."* [[Timber Harvard|Timber Harward]] applies the same point to the men he
mentors — a "pull to bond with other guys" is platonic, not homosexual; often "you just
probably need to make better friends."[^1][^2]

## Supporting Evidence (as presented)

### 1. The analytic core: no sexual content → not homosexual
Robinson: a non-sexual feeling toward a man "is not homosexual. Period. End of story."
Homosexual requires sexual content; a feeling without it does not qualify, whatever its
intensity.[^1]
> **Evidence rating:** Strong as a definitional/analytic point; **Argument weight:** Primary.

### 2. Two guardrails Robinson attaches
He immediately qualifies the claim against misuse: (a) *not sexual* does not mean *not a
problem* — "If I'm so jealous of another man I hate my life, that's a problem. It's just
not a homosexual problem"; and (b) the non-sexual can *become* sexual quickly once arousal
patterns are established, "especially if I interpret it that way." The point is to stop
**over-interpreting** ordinary feelings as proof of being gay, not to deny that feelings
can be sexualized.[^3]
> **Evidence rating:** Moderate; **Argument weight:** Primary (these guardrails are what keep the claim from becoming denial).

### 3. Reported clinical payoff
Robinson: when a client genuinely internalizes that non-sexual feelings are not
homosexual, the reported relief is large — "I never hear less than 60%... 60, 70, 80, 90%
of it" of the struggle, because the culture had trained him to read every tender trait as
evidence.[^4]
> **Evidence rating:** Weak-to-moderate (self-reported, uncontrolled); **Argument weight:** Secondary.

### 4. Timber: unmet needs mistaken for orientation
[[Timber Harvard|Timber Harward]]: "being attracted to men is not homosexual. We can have
a platonic pull to want to bond with other men," but "society even on the left and right
spins that as something homosexual [so] we internalize it." Often the real issue is
"deeper unmet needs there that maybe are becoming sexualized" — sometimes simply the need
for friends.[^2]
> **Evidence rating:** Moderate (lived-experience mentoring); **Argument weight:** Secondary.

## Best Quotes
> "If a thought or a feeling you have about another man has no sexual content, it is not homosexual. Period. End of story... So ask yourself, could a man who's never experienced homosexual feelings have the feelings I'm having? If they could, it's not homosexual."[^1]

> "That doesn't mean it might not be a problem. If I'm so jealous of another man I hate my life, that's a problem. It's just not a homosexual problem — unless I sexualize it."[^3]

> "Being attracted to men is not homosexual. We can have a platonic pull to want to bond with other men... it just means, unfortunately, you just probably need to make better friends."[^2]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
The obvious worry is motivated redefinition: relabeling same-sex desire as "just unmet
needs" or "just needing better friends" can slide into **denial and repression** — telling
someone their genuine orientation is really a friendship deficit risks dismissing a real
experience and echoes reparative-therapy framing (see [[Joseph Nicolosi]]) that major
professional bodies reject as unsupported and potentially harmful. Suppression-style
strategies are, in the clinical literature, associated with *worse* not better mental
health, so "stop interpreting it as homosexual" can misfire for someone whose attractions
are, in fact, sexual and stable.

The claim's defense, on Robinson's own terms, is that it is (a) genuinely **analytic** — a
feeling with no sexual content is, by definition, not a *sexual* feeling — and (b) explicitly
**bounded**: he affirms the feeling may still be a real problem, and warns that the
non-sexual can be sexualized, so he is not denying that anyone has genuine same-sex sexual
attraction. Read narrowly, the claim only forbids treating *non-sexual* traits (music,
sports, tenderness) as evidence of orientation — a point even critics can grant. Read
broadly, as a blanket reinterpretation of *sexual* attraction into mere friendship-hunger,
it overreaches and is where the objection lands. The note keeps both readings visible on
purpose.
