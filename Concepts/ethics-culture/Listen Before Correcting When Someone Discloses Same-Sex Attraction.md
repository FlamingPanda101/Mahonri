# Listen Before Correcting When Someone Discloses Same-Sex Attraction

## The Claim
Asked how a lay leader or teacher should respond when a youth confides "I'm gay," the panel
is unanimous: **the first move is to listen and validate, never to correct.** [[Jeff Robinson]] models the opening — gratitude and empathy, an invitation to say more — and says
the one thing you do *not* do is "pull out your research and say, 'you're wrong.'"
Assumption-questioning and reframing come later, and only inside a relationship of trust;
[[Timber Harvard|Timber Harward]] adds that once trust exists, it is loving to gently push
back.[^1][^2]

## Supporting Evidence (as presented)

### 1. Robinson's actual opening lines
"I wouldn't immediately try to correct them. I'd probably say, 'Thank you for sharing that.
That must have been very difficult for you. I'm so glad you would trust me. Can you tell me
some more about that?'" — "because first we have to hear where they're at... and be grateful
that they're talking."[^1]
> **Evidence rating:** Strong (concrete, professional counsel); **Argument weight:** Primary.

### 2. What *not* to do first
When the host restates it — "the first thing you don't do is pull out your research and say,
'well, you're wrong'" — Robinson: "Absolutely not. Absolutely not." Leading with correction
severs the trust that just made disclosure possible.[^3]
> **Evidence rating:** Strong; **Argument weight:** Primary.

### 3. Hearing precedes reframing (and must, because affirmation gave real relief)
Robinson ties the pastoral order to the shame dynamic: because people "found relief from
their anguish in those labels," you cannot open by attacking the label — "you can't just
come in and say... the labels are wrong." You "establish a relationship, hear their
experience where they are," *then* help them find relief in "something better" (see
[[Pride Is Not the Antidote to Shame]]).[^4]
> **Evidence rating:** Moderate; **Argument weight:** Primary.

### 4. Timber: listen first, then it can be loving to push back
[[Timber Harvard|Timber Harward]], from running a support group and answering personal
emails, "tries to just hear their experience" first; but once he understands where someone
is coming from, "it's okay to push back sometimes in a loving way" — e.g., gently
questioning whether a trait a man reads as "gay" is actually so (see [[A Feeling With No Sexual Content Is Not Homosexual]]).[^2]
> **Evidence rating:** Moderate (lived ministry); **Argument weight:** Secondary.

## Best Quotes
> "I wouldn't immediately try to correct them. I'd probably say, 'Thank you for sharing that. That must have been very difficult for you. I'm so glad you would trust me. Can you tell me some more about that?'"[^1]

> Host: "So the first thing you don't do is pull out your research and say, 'well, you're wrong.'" — Robinson: "Absolutely not. Absolutely not."[^3]

> "It's okay to push back sometimes in a loving way and be like, 'Hey, you're saying this is gay and I don't really think it is.'"[^2]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
A fully affirming clinician would applaud the *first* move — validate, thank, hear — as
exactly best practice, but object to the *second*: framing the eventual goal as reframing
the person *out of* a gay identity is, on the affirming view, a soft-sell of a change
agenda, and "I'll listen, then gently question your assumptions" can function as
relationship-building in service of an outcome the person did not seek. Professional bodies
that reject orientation-change efforts would draw the line at the point where "listening"
turns into steering. There is also a real risk of the leader overstepping — the host himself
flags that these conversations should not happen without parents and that lay leaders are
"not therapists."

The convergence, though, is larger than the dispute: everyone in the debate agrees that
**leading with correction is wrong** and that validation, safety, and hearing come first —
the divergence is only over the eventual *direction* of care, which is the same
underlying disagreement mapped in [[Pride Is Not the Antidote to Shame]] and
[[Mixed-Orientation Marriages and LGBTQ Latter-day Saints Show Higher Wellbeing]]. On the
narrow pastoral question this note answers — *what do you say in the moment someone
confides in you?* — the counsel (gratitude, empathy, listen, do not correct) is sound and
broadly shared.

## LDS Connection
This is the applied, "what do I actually say" companion to the panel's conceptual notes; it
presupposes the shame analysis of [[Pride Is Not the Antidote to Shame]] (you cannot open by
attacking a label that is currently relieving someone's anguish) and points toward the
gentle assumption-questioning of [[A Feeling With No Sexual Content Is Not Homosexual]] and
[[Adopting the Gay Label Hinders Discipleship]] — but *only* after trust exists. Tier C, with
Robinson's clinical counsel leaning B.
