# Consent Answers What Is Permissible Not What Marriage Is For

#type/claim

## The Claim

[[Jacob Hansen]] argues that modern secular culture "has largely reduced sexual morality to one principle — consent. If everyone consents, everything is fine."[^1] His critique is not that consent is *bad* but that it is **the wrong kind of criterion**: consent is a test of what is *permissible*, and is structurally silent about *purpose*. "Consent doesn't tell us what marriage is for. Consent doesn't tell us what family is for. Consent doesn't tell us what gender is for."[^2]

The diagnosis is a missing category. In the secular frame "nobody is talking about purpose — only permission,"[^2] but (per [[What Is Gender For - Sexual Ethics Need a Telos Not Just a Rule]]) "morality is not about what is permissible. It's about what is ideal."[^3] A consent-only ethic can rule an act *allowed*, yet it cannot even *pose* the question of the ideal sexual order — because the moment purpose is off the table, every consensual arrangement is flattened to equivalence ("I think they're both equally valid choices").[^1]

Hansen is careful to concede the liberal point that gives consent its force: the ideal "cannot be imposed by force," and "all mankind should be free to seek the ideal that they believe in according to the dictates of their own conscience so long as they are not violating the rights of others."[^3] So the claim is deliberately narrow — **consent under-describes morality**, leaving the question of the *ideal* unanswered; it is not the claim that consent is worthless or that non-ideal arrangements should be banned.

## Supporting Evidence

1. **The reduction is real and self-described.** The video juxtaposes the thesis with an interview in which participants describe polyamory and monogamy as "both equally valid choices," with "the most important thing" being "each being committed to empowering the other's autonomy."[^1] The autonomy-and-consent frame is quoted from its own advocates, not caricatured. *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Strong as an accurate description of consent-liberalism's stated commitments.*

2. **Consent is silent on three distinct purpose-questions.** Marriage, family, and gender each have a "what is it *for*" question that a permission-criterion cannot address.[^2] The point is conceptual, not empirical: consent ranges over *acts between parties*, whereas purpose ranges over *institutions and ends*. *Argument weight: Primary.*

3. **Permissible ≠ ideal.** Even a fully consensual order can be non-ideal; conflating "allowed" with "good" is the error. This is the same permissible-vs-ideal distinction that anchors the parent thesis.[^3] *Argument weight: Secondary.*

4. **The concession keeps the argument honest.** By explicitly refusing to *impose* the ideal, Hansen separates "what should be permitted" (where consent rightly governs) from "what is ideal and why" (where consent is mute), so the critique doesn't collapse into authoritarianism.[^3] *Argument weight: Secondary — this is what blunts the strongest objection.*

## Best Quotes

> "Consent doesn't tell us what marriage is for. Consent doesn't tell us what family is for. Consent doesn't tell us what gender is for." [^2]

> "Do you notice what's missing? Purpose. Nobody is talking about purpose. Only permission." [^2]

> "I'm not here advocating for forcing anyone to live according to any ideal… so long as they are not violating the rights of others." [^3]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

**Consent encodes a real and hard-won moral insight.** The autonomy ethic is not empty content-free permission; it protects against coercion, rape, and paternalism, and liberals from Mill onward *deliberately* restrict publicly enforceable morality to harm precisely to safeguard pluralism. Asking society to enforce an "ideal sexual order" is exactly what a consent-liberal fears — and history gives them reasons. On this reading, consent's "silence about purpose" is a *virtue*: it leaves purpose to individuals rather than imposing one.

**Autonomy can have purposes — chosen ones.** An autonomy ethicist can answer "what is marriage for?" with "for whatever the partners covenant it to be." To call self-authored purposes "no purpose" arguably *begs the question* by assuming purpose must be given from outside (by nature or God) to count. A committed same-sex or polyamorous couple can articulate rich purposes (mutual flourishing, child-rearing, lifelong fidelity) without any cosmic telos.

**The move risks a bait-and-switch.** A critic will note that "morality is about the ideal, not the permissible" can smuggle a *particular* ideal in through the back door: everyone agrees some orders are better than others, but that concession does not select Hansen's ideal over, say, an egalitarian-autonomy ideal. Naming the missing category (purpose) does not yet establish *which* purpose is correct.

**Response.** Hansen grants the freedom point outright — he is not proposing to legislate the ideal — so the "you'll coerce us" worry misfires against his actual position. And he can accept that autonomy answers "what is *permitted*" while still pressing that it cannot answer "what is *ideal*," because chosen purposes are precisely what is *in question* when we ask whether one order is better than another. His claim is modest here — consent is the wrong tool for the ideal-question — and the *substantive* answer to which ideal is true is deferred to the eternal-purpose argument (see [[If Marriage Ends at Death Sexual Ethics Reduce to Arbitrary Command]] and [[What Is Gender For - Sexual Ethics Need a Telos Not Just a Rule]]). The steelman's strongest form — that leaving purpose to individuals is itself the ideal — is a real rival position, not a refutation.
