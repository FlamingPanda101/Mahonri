# Accepting Same-Sex Marriage Would Not Improve Church Retention (Objection Rebutted)

## The Claim (Objection Rebutted)
A frequent argument — raised by critics and by sympathetic reformers alike — holds that the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints is bleeding young members over its stance on same-sex marriage, and that accepting same-sex marriage would stanch the loss and retain the rising generation. One of the report's own peer reviewers pressed this point, prompting the authors to model it directly.

## The Rebuttal

1. **The counterfactual loses more than it keeps (rated: strong, modeled).** In the FFYD data, the authors ran the numbers on a hypothetical policy change. Even under the *generous* assumption that 100% of youth who agreed the Church should accept same-sex marriage were thereby retained (~45 individuals), a merely 15% defection among the much larger group who *disagreed* would cost 58 individuals — a net loss.[^1] The authors note the 100%-retention assumption is "unrealistically high" and the 15%-loss assumption "likely conservative," so the real trade would be worse.[^1]

2. **The disagreeing base dwarfs the agreeing base (rated: strong).** Agreeing the Church should accept same-sex marriage was itself a significant predictor of leaving (28% of those who "strongly agree" deidentified vs. 5% of those who "strongly disagree"). But in the sample only 129 youth agreed while 408 disagreed — so a policy tuned to retain the smaller, agreeing group risks alienating a base three-to-four times its size.[^2]

3. **The real driver is politics, not orientation (rated: moderate).** When political ideology was controlled, sexual orientation "dropped from significance" as a predictor of leaving: non-heterosexual youth were more likely to be politically liberal and therefore more likely to be in the "Faith Leaving" group, but "conservative heterosexuals and conservative bisexuals were just as likely to be Faith Staying."[^3] This reframes the dispute as political alignment rather than orientation per se, and suggests a doctrinal concession would not resolve the underlying political tension. See [[Political Identity Now Supersedes Religious Identity]].

## Best Quotes

> "if only 15% of those who disagreed with the Church accepting same-sex marriage (somewhat or strongly) deidentified in response to the change, the Church would lose 58 individuals—more than the number retained."[^1]

> "Numerically, if retention alone were the goal, such a change would likely not meet that goal."[^1]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

- **Retention is not the real argument.** The strongest form of the reformer's case is moral, not numerical — that the Church *should* change because the teaching is wrong or harmful, regardless of membership math. The report explicitly brackets this: it answers only the empirical "would it improve retention?" claim, not the underlying moral question. The rebuttal therefore defeats a retention-based argument, not a conscience-based one.
- **A modeled scenario on a small sample.** The counterfactual rests on a modest FFYD subsample (129 agreeing, 408 disagreeing; only ~22 disagreers actually deidentified), and the 15% defection figure is an assumption, not an observed outcome. Different assumptions yield different balances.
- **Attitudes are not fixed.** Cross-sectional views on same-sex marriage measured at one wave may shift over time, and the model assumes preferences and their salience stay put.
- **Silent on member well-being.** The analysis counts identification, not the lived well-being of LGBTQ Latter-day Saints under either policy; that distinct question is treated elsewhere. See [[Mixed-Orientation Marriages and LGBTQ Latter-day Saints Show Higher Wellbeing]].
