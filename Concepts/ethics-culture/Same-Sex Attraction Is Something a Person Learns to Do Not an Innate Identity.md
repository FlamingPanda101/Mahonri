#type/claim

# Same-Sex Attraction Is Something a Person Learns to Do Not an Innate Identity

## The Claim
[[Jeff Robinson]] argues that same-sex attraction is best understood not as a fixed,
innate identity but as **"something a person knows how to do"** — a learned capacity.
The reframe originated in his doctoral research: every man he interviewed who claimed to
have "changed" meant the same thing — *"I could still do it. I just don't do it."* From
that he catalogues five folk-explanations of what causes SSA and proposes the fifth as
the most useful: (1) born that way, (2) chose it, (3) a symptom of deep psychological
problem or mental illness, (4) the cause doesn't matter, only the response — and (5) it
is simply *something you know how to do*. He grants partial truth to the first four but
argues the fifth alone restores meaningful agency: if SSA is innate and "happening to
me," my agency is minimal; if it is something I learned to do, I have some choice over
whether, when, and how I act on it.[^1][^2] Crucially, "learned" is **not** "chosen" —
Robinson explicitly rejects the choice framing.[^3]

## Supporting Evidence (as presented)

### 1. The clinical observation that seeded the model
In his dissertation interviews, men who said they had changed uniformly reported they had
not lost the capacity — *"I could still do it. I just don't do it. I just don't need to
do it,"* including feelings, thoughts, behaviors, and fantasies. This suggested to
Robinson that SSA is a thing a person *knows how to do* rather than a fixed state.[^1]
> **Evidence rating:** Moderate as clinical observation from a credentialed therapist; **Argument weight:** Primary (the load-bearing premise of the whole talk).

### 2. "Learned" is distinguished from "chosen" — the English analogy
To the old "born that way or chose it?" dichotomy Robinson answers: *"Were you born
speaking English or did you choose it?"* Some of the most dominant features of our lives
were neither innate nor chosen but developed through participation — as one participates,
unchosen, in learning a first language.[^3]
> **Evidence rating:** Moderate (analogy, not proof); **Argument weight:** Primary.

### 3. All five explanations hold "some truth"
Robinson concedes a predisposing vulnerability (a partial nod to "born that way"),
non-chosen participation in its development, the role of unmet social-emotional needs
(the "symptom" view), and that one must often act without full understanding of cause
(the "doesn't matter" view). The fifth simply names what the other four circle: a learned
competence.[^2]
> **Evidence rating:** Moderate; **Argument weight:** Secondary.

### 4. "No amnesia" — the honest downside
The model's bad news is symmetrical with its good news: *"you don't get amnesia."* Once
learned, it is not un-learned; there is "no magic cure" that scrubs it from the brain.
Robinson illustrates with a mundane learned behavior: *"When I was a kid, I learned to
cuss. I still know how to do it. But I have some agency over when I do it and how I do
it."*[^4]
> **Evidence rating:** Moderate; **Argument weight:** Secondary (guards the claim against a "promise of cure" reading).

## Best Quotes
> "It is only something you know how to do. It is simply something you know how to do. It is merely something you know how to do... Well, that's a pretty big thing."[^2]

> "Were you born speaking English or did you choose it? There are things that are very important in our lives and really dominate our experiences that we didn't choose and we weren't born that way."[^3]

> "The bad news about it is you don't get amnesia. Once you know how to do it, you know how to do it. And so there's not some magic cure."[^4]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
The mainstream psychological position (e.g., the American Psychological Association) is
that sexual orientation is **not a choice**, is generally stable across the lifespan, and
that efforts to change orientation (SOCE / "conversion therapy") are ineffective and carry
documented risk of harm. On that view, "born this way" is not a naïve slogan but a
faithful report of how attraction is actually experienced by many gay people — as
unchosen and unresponsive to effort — and recasting SSA as "a behavior you know how to
do" can sound like precisely the premise behind discredited change therapies, and can
feel invalidating to the person living it. A sharper form of the objection: the phrase
blurs **behavior** (genuinely chosen) with **attraction** (typically not) — one can abstain
from conduct while the orientation persists, which is exactly what [[Timber Harvard|Timber
Harward]] reports of himself.

The steelman's honest residue, on the source's own terms: Robinson (a) explicitly denies
"chosen," limiting his claim to agency over *response*, not the erasure of attraction;
(b) builds in "no amnesia" precisely to forbid a cure narrative; and (c) concedes the
model is clinically derived and **not** research-confirmed (see
[[The Born-Gay Assumption Is Unsupported by Science and LDS Prophets]]). The distinction
that *survives* the objection is the modest, even mainstream one — orientation, conduct,
and self-label are three different things, and agency lives at the level of conduct and
self-understanding. The stronger claim, that SSA is "basically a learned skill," outruns
the available evidence and is contested by the clinical consensus.

## LDS Connection
This is the conceptual keystone of the panel's pastoral case: because agency is the
center of the Latter-day Saint plan of salvation, a frame that *preserves* agency for
members who experience SSA is treated as spiritually and practically liberating, where a
deterministic "born gay" frame is treated as agency-denying (and, the panel argues, as
warping one's view of a fair God). It underwrites the label critique in
[[Adopting the Gay Label Hinders Discipleship]] and the therapeutic move in
[[Overfocus and Overinterpretation Entrench Same-Sex Attraction]]. Tier C overall, with
Robinson's in-field observations leaning B; the empirical ceiling is set by his own
concession that the model is unproven.
