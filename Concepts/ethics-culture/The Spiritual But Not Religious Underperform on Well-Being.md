# The Spiritual But Not Religious Underperform on Well-Being

## The Claim
A popular modern move is to keep "spirituality" while dropping organized religion — "spiritual but not religious" (SBNR). The claim examined here is that this substitute does not deliver the goods: in nationally representative data, the SBNR and the religiously unaffiliated report the *lowest* well-being of any group, and the effect is worst in the youngest generation — the very cohort most likely to have made the switch.

## Supporting Evidence

1. **SBNR Millennials are the least happy group measured (rated: strong, Pew).** Among the "spiritual but not religious," 33% of earlier generations say they are "very happy" with life, but only 10% of Millennial+ do — "the lowest level of any religious group" in the Pew Religious Landscape Study.[^1]

2. **Same collapse in family life (rated: strong, Pew).** 61% of earlier-generation SBNR call their family life "very good" or "excellent," versus only 36% of Millennial+ SBNR — "again the lowest of any religious group."[^2] For contrast, Latter-day Saint Millennials are the *most* likely of any religion to say they are "very happy."[^3]

3. **The unaffiliated bottom out on spiritual peace (rated: strong, Pew).** On feeling "a deep sense of spiritual peace and well-being" at least weekly, Atheists/Agnostics sit at 14–19% and "nothing in particular" at 23–26%, versus 62–67% for Latter-day Saints — roughly a threefold gap.[^4]

4. **Worse mental health among the non-religious (rated: moderate, Healthy Minds).** In the Healthy Minds college dataset, those of no faith have *significantly higher* rates of severe depression, anxiety, and past-year suicide attempts than Latter-day Saints and most other faiths.[^5]

5. **Secularism has not actually "won" the young (rated: moderate).** Contrary to the secularization narrative, Millennial+ are *slightly more* likely than older generations to believe people have a soul or spirit (98.2% vs. 96.8%); the report concludes "pure, nonspiritual secularism has not won them over."[^6] The problem for SBNR is not too much spirituality but the loss of the *community and practice* that convert belief into well-being. See [[High-Demand Religion Improves Retention and Well-being]].

## Best Quotes

> "33% of earlier generations of those who are 'Spiritual but not Religious' indicate they are very happy with their lives compared to just 10% of those who are Millennial+, the lowest level of any religious group."[^1]

> "While traditional religious beliefs have declined, Millennial+ may be more likely to subscribe to nontraditional ideas that deviate from Western mainstream beliefs."[^6]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

- **Correlational and cross-sectional.** The Pew data are a one-time snapshot; they cannot show that *leaving* religion lowered well-being. Reverse causation is live — people who are already unhappy, isolated, or in crisis may be *more* likely to drift into the SBNR/unaffiliated category, so the low scores could be a cause rather than an effect of that status.
- **Self-report and framing.** "Very happy" and "spiritual peace" are self-rated and susceptible to social desirability and in-group framing; committed believers may narrate their lives more positively without a difference in underlying state.
- **A heterogeneous catch-all.** "Spiritual but not religious" bundles very different people (seekers, drifters, the disillusioned), so a low average may hide wide variation. The youngest SBNR cohort is also comparatively small, and the report notes smaller samples produce greater variability.
- **What the report does control.** For the well-being measures the authors reran the analysis controlling for gender and race and found "controlling for these made little difference," which blunts (but does not eliminate) the demographic-confound objection.[^7] Selection into SBNR remains uncontrolled.
