# Overfocus and Overinterpretation Entrench Same-Sex Attraction

## The Claim
[[Jeff Robinson]] argues that what turns adolescent same-sex arousal into an entrenched,
identity-defining struggle is not the arousal itself but **five reinforcing responses to
it**, chief among them **overfocus and overinterpretation**. The governing principle: what
you focus on intensely grows larger. The therapeutic corollary is that reducing the focus
and ceasing to over-interpret lowers the *salience* of the attraction — which
[[Timber Harvard|Timber Harward]] reports from his own life, while being explicit that this
is diminished salience, **not** a claim that the attraction is gone.[^1][^2]

## Supporting Evidence (as presented)

### 1. The five factors that "lock it in"
After a boy recognizes he is being aroused by other males, Robinson says five things
follow and entrench it: **(1) overfocus and overinterpretation** — it becomes "the biggest
thing in my life," outweighing school, family, friends, and church; **(2) self-loathing**
— "I wanted to be good... and I've got the worst possible thing... I'm disgusting";
**(3) isolation and secrecy** — becoming "world-class fakers" no one suspects; **(4)
compulsive sexuality** — fantasy, pornography, masturbation used to medicate the pain (a
pattern he notes is shared with heterosexual men); and **(5) spiritual/religious turmoil**
— "Why haven't my prayers been answered?"[^1]
> **Evidence rating:** Moderate as clinical description; **Argument weight:** Primary.

### 2. Focus enlarges what it lands on
The linchpin is attention. Robinson asks clients, "When you focus on something that much,
does it get larger or smaller?" — "Larger." The more the one fact ("I get aroused by men")
is worried over, the more it swells until it "outweighs everything else."[^3]
> **Evidence rating:** Moderate; **Argument weight:** Primary.

### 3. De-focusing lowers salience — Timber's report
[[Timber Harvard|Timber Harward]]: his SSA "has diminished, but it's because I'm just not
focusing on it" — the result of distancing from "the labels and the behavior" and focusing
on "God's direction for our lives." He is careful: it diminished, he "still definitely
experience[s] same-sex attraction," just "not to the same degree."[^2] Elsewhere he locates
present triggers in "personal insecurities... body issues... male relationships," not a
standing preoccupation.[^4]
> **Evidence rating:** Moderate (firsthand, and self-limiting); **Argument weight:** Primary.

## Best Quotes
> "Over here is the fact I get aroused by men, and over here is every other thing in my life — school, family, friends, church... this one thing outweighs everything else... When you focus on something that much, does it get larger or smaller? Larger."[^3]

> "Most become what I would call world-class fakers... nobody knows the hell this kid walks around with."[^1]

> "It has diminished, but it's because I'm just not focusing on it... as we distance ourselves from the labels and the behavior and focus on God's direction for our lives, it diminishes over time."[^2]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
"Just stop focusing on it" is exactly the kind of counsel critics warn against as
**thought-suppression / repression**, which the clinical literature associates with *worse*,
not better, mental-health outcomes and with rebound preoccupation. Telling a distressed
person that the intensity of their struggle is "self-created" by their own attention can
also induce guilt and gaslight a genuine experience — and lowered *salience* is not the same
as a changed orientation, so framing de-focusing as progress risks moving the goalposts.

The steelman's force is blunted, though not erased, by what the note's own subjects
concede. Robinson and Timber claim reduced **salience and distress**, explicitly *not*
orientation change — Timber says outright that the attraction persists and that knowing its
cause never made it "go away." The mechanism they describe is closer to **attentional
redirection toward valued goals** (covenant life, relationships, service) than to raw
suppression, and Timber locates his relief in redirected focus and charity rather than in
gritted-teeth avoidance (see [[Pride Is Not the Antidote to Shame]]). Whether that
redirection is durable and non-harmful for a given person, versus a socially-pressured
suppression, is the live empirical question — and the note flags it rather than settling it.
