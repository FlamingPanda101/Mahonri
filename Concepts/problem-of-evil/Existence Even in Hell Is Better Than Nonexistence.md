# Existence Even in Hell Is Better Than Nonexistence

## The Claim (Catholic rebuttal to the Creation Dilemma)

This is the Catholic answer to [[The Creation Dilemma]] — Jacob Hansen's charge (borrowing his friend [[Hayden Carroll]]'s book *The Creation Dilemma*) that "the Catholic God is evil" because God creates people he foreknows will be damned when he could refrain. The ten Catholics do not dodge to mystery; they give a **metaphysical** answer in two prongs: (1) **being is convertible with good**, so to exist at all — even ending in hell — is a genuine good and therefore *does* will the creature's good; and (2) God is **pure act**, so his creative act cannot be conditioned by creatures' foreseen free choices. Hell results from the creature's free rejection of grace, which God permits but does not will.[^1]

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. Good is convertible with being (rating: the load-bearing Thomist premise; argument weight: primary)
"When we say good... we mean good as convertible with being. A person is more good than a table because they have a soul — they have more being than a table does. And it's better to have a person with a soul, even if they go to hell, than for them to not exist at all."[^2] Pressed directly — "it's better to exist in hell forever than to not exist at all?" — the Catholic answers "Yes."[^3] So creating a person, *as such*, wills a real good (existence), satisfying "God wills the good of every individual." See [[Thomas Aquinas]] (the definition of love as "willing the good of another" is invoked explicitly).[^4]

### 2. Creation is good because it reflects God's glory (rating: coherent within the system; argument weight: primary)
Applied to Judas: "was it good for Judas to be created? Yes... because to be created in itself is good, because it is a pure act of God" — everything that comes into existence does so as "a reflection of the goodness and the glory of God." The Catholics distinguish two questions: *was Judas's creation good?* (yes — being is good) from *was Judas's final end good for Judas?* (no — hence Jesus's lament), and deny that the second cancels the first.[^5]

### 3. God is pure act and cannot be conditioned by creatures (rating: the deepest prong; argument weight: primary)
If God decided whether to create based on whether a creature would freely love him, then "there is something external to God that is acting on the divine essence in his pure act of creating" — which would make God *not* pure act, "a metaphysical impossibility." Since "if he's not pure act then we're not talking about God," God's creative act cannot be governed by foreseen outcomes; it proceeds from his own goodness, not from a cost-benefit calculation over creatures' futures.[^6] Cf. [[Divine Attributes Follow From Pure Actuality]].

### 4. Synergism, not Calvinism (rating: important clarification; argument weight: secondary)
The Catholics insist this is *not* Calvinism: God does not *actively will* evil, he *permits* it. God gives real grace to all and genuine libertarian freedom (they cite 1 Timothy 2:4, "he desires all to be saved," and Acts 7:48-50, "I called, no one answered"). "Love doesn't force itself on anybody"; God "is a gentleman" who allows his creatures to reject him. Hell is the creature's self-chosen refusal of grace, not God's design for them. See [[An Omnipotent God Cannot Force a Free Creature to Choose Good]].[^7]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

Hansen's replies (the LDS/critic side):

1. **Jesus ranks non-existence above damnation.** [[Matthew 26.24|Matthew 26:24]] — "it had been good for that man if he had not been born" — has *Jesus* asserting that for at least Judas, non-existence is preferable to his fate. That directly denies "existence is always a net good." "For me personally... I would rather not exist at all than be in a state of eternal torment forever."[^8] See [[The Judas Argument - Better Never to Have Been Born]].

2. **The button intuition.** Shown a button that will create a person whose certain outcome is eternal torment, "why would he ever push that button... when he didn't have to?" Most people would *not* press it — the reluctance tracks the moral judgment that creating the foreknown-damned is not willing their good.[^9]

3. **The free-will defense misfires on the *prior* question.** Quoting Carroll's book: "the free will defense focuses entirely on what happens *after* a person already exists... but it doesn't explain why that person was created in the first place, which is the missing piece." The first morally decisive act is *creation*, upstream of any creaturely choice. See [[The First Morally Decisive Moment Is Creation Not Rebellion]].[^10]

4. **"Pure act" just restates the problem.** To protect pure act, the Catholic must say God "pushes the button" regardless of the foreseen hell — which is precisely the act Hansen calls immoral, now defended by metaphysical necessity rather than answered. Hansen's proposed resolution is to *examine the metaphysics itself*: the pure-act/impassibility framework may be a post-apostolic Greek import rather than a biblical revelation, and revising it (as LDS metaphysics does) dissolves the dilemma at its root.[^11]

**The LDS exit.** Because Latter-day Saints reject creation *ex nihilo* — human "intelligences" are uncreated and co-eternal with God — God never "pushes a button" that brings the damned into being from nothing; he organizes and enters relationship with agents who already exist. This is why Hansen frames the dilemma as a *comparative* test that his own metaphysics passes and the creedal one does not. See [[Why Doesnt God Only Create the Saved]] and [[The Mary Problem for Catholic Theism]].
