# The Regeneration Rejoinder - Calvinist Evangelism Is Self-Defeating

## The Claim (Objection)
[[Hayden Carroll]] presses a distinct objection to Reformed theology — not (primarily) that Calvinism makes God's *goodness* incoherent (that is [[Even If the Trinity Is True Your God Is Evil|the sibling argument]]), but that it makes the **evangelistic conversation itself self-defeating**. If God unconditionally regenerates the elect and passes over the rest, then Carroll's being a Latter-day Saint — and his possible damnation — is *God's* choice, not his. So the debate cannot change a predestined outcome, and the only honest question left for Carroll to ask God at judgment is: **"Why didn't you regenerate me?"** He claims Calvinism has no non-question-begging answer, so "I can stump God with a question."[^1]

## Supporting Evidence

1. **The performative self-defeat.** "If God wanted latter-day saints to worship the true Jesus Christ, he would regenerate them and lead them." On [[Greg (Collierville Pastor)]]'s own premises, "he predestined me to be a Latter-day Saint... If I remain a Latter-day Saint under your view, he predestined me to burn in hell forever. So this conversation... doesn't make a difference."[^2] *Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Strong as an internal critique* — it targets the coherence of *Reformed evangelism*, not Calvinism's truth as such. Carroll even quips this is "the reason I'm not a Calvinist — because if God wanted me to be a Calvinist, then eventually I will."[^3]

2. **The "stump God" move.** At judgment, when Greg says God "will expose your heart" and show "you willingly loved the darkness," Carroll's reply is to keep asking: "You didn't change my heart from stone to flesh — *why didn't you change my heart?*" He argues the only Calvinist answer is Romans 9's "who are you, O man?" — which he caricatures as God saying "Shut up, mortal. You can't ask that question."[^4] *Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate* — it presses a real tension (the compatibilist asymmetry between election and reprobation), though it assumes libertarian free will as the standard of a "fair" answer.

3. **The omnibenevolence cost.** A God "able to save everybody" who "chooses not to," and creates the reprobate anyway, cannot be all-good: "how can you be all good if you can prevent people from going to hell but you don't want to?"[^5] See [[Even If the Trinity Is True Your God Is Evil]].

4. **The aborted-infants hard case.** Carroll cites the [[James White]]–Jacob Hansen debate: a consistent Calvinist will affirm God *could* justly send an aborted baby to hell. "How is that fair and just... other than just saying it's good because he does it?" He notes MacArthur wrote a whole book (*Safe in the Arms of God*) precisely because the system gives no clear answer.[^6] *Argument weight: Secondary; Evidence rating: Moderate.*

## Best Quotes
> "And I'm going to say, 'Why didn't you regenerate me?'"[^4]

> "I can stump God with a question. That's wild."[^1]

> "He predestined me to be a Latter-day Saint. He predestined you to be a believer... this conversation about interpretation and about the text and all these things... it doesn't make a difference... And I think that makes him to be an evil God."[^2]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
[[Greg (Collierville Pastor)]] answers as a **compatibilist**, and his responses are the strongest available on the Reformed side:

- **Scripture teaches both sides of the coin.** God is sovereign "from beginning to end" *and* humans are "morally volitional creatures... responsible for our sin." Greg's paradigm cases are Judas and the crucifixion (Acts 2, 4): the Jews did what "God predestined" yet are "cut to the heart" and held responsible. "How those two things fit... they fit together in the mind of God better than they fit in the mind of Greg."[^7] See [[Calvinism Is Consistent but Built on a Single Bad Premise]]. *He concedes it is a mystery he "would just surrender to" — honest, but it does not dissolve Carroll's asymmetry worry.*

- **At judgment God exposes genuine rejection.** Greg insists the reprobate "won't be able to kick the curb": God will pinpoint "where the truth confronted you head-on and you willingly loved the darkness rather than the light" (John 1/3), so the responsibility is really the sinner's (Romans 9's potter). Carroll's rejoinder is that this still leaves "why didn't you regenerate me?" unanswered.[^4]

- **The transcendent-author analogy.** Greg's central illustration: as J.R.R. Tolkien authors suffering into Middle-earth without being "charged with murder," God authors the story from "a transcendentally different level," so ordained suffering (his own 7-year infertility; a rape-conceived child who became "pride and joy"; Joseph in Egypt; *felix culpa*) is not evidence of an evil God.[^8] *Steelman weakness Carroll exploits:* if the author had the power to make the characters *real* and *conscious*, we *would* hold him culpable — so the analogy breaks exactly where eternal conscious torment enters.[^9]

- **Election grounds witness, not fatalism.** Greg (with Paul Washer) argues sovereignty is not a license to write anyone off — "treat everyone like a potential brother or sister"; we don't know the elect, so we preach and mourn those who reject.[^10]

**Fair note (both directions):** the vault records that *many* Reformed thinkers reject the crude "hell glorifies God" framing and double predestination, so Carroll's target is *consistent* Calvinism, not every Reformed position (see [[Even If the Trinity Is True Your God Is Evil]]). Conversely, the LDS alternative — [[Free Will - Atheist Determinism vs LDS Co-Eternality|co-eternal, uncreated agents]] — blocks the reductio at its root but relocates the problem (divine foreknowledge vs. agency; a God "bound by eternal law" who sometimes cannot prevent evil).
