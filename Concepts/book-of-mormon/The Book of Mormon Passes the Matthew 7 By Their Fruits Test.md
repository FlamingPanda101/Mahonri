# The Book of Mormon Passes the Matthew 7 By Their Fruits Test

#type/claim

## The Claim
[[The Bald Coder]] frames his whole analysis around Jesus's test in [[Matthew 7.15-20|Matthew 7]] — "every good tree produces good fruit, and every bad tree produces bad fruit" — as a way to judge a book without reading every page. Having found (by embedding-model analysis) that the Book of Mormon is Christ-centered and teaches universal moral principles, he concludes it registers as **"good fruit"** and so passes the test. Crucially, he applies the *identical* verdict to the Old Testament, New Testament and Quran.[^1]

## Supporting Evidence
1. **The interpretive frame is scriptural, and self-flagged as interpretation.** The project began with [[Matthew 7.15-20|Matthew 7]]: rather than read every page, the creator used natural-language processing to ask "what are the major themes and invitations of this book" to get "a more objective answer to know if this is a good or a bad fruit." He explicitly labels the concluding step as "conclusions and interpretations based on the data," inviting disagreement.[^2] *(Evidence rating: n/a — this is the argument's structure, not a data point. Argument weight: Primary.)*
2. **Data → verdict.** Because the two prior findings hold — Christ-centered ([[The Book of Mormon Scores Higher Than the Bible on Christ-Centeredness]]) and morally universal ([[The Book of Mormon Teaches Universal Moral Principles Shared Across Scripture]]) — he answers his own question: "does that make the Book of Mormon a good fruit? In my humble opinion, undoubtedly, yes."[^3] *(Evidence rating: Weak — a value judgment resting on model-generated scores. Argument weight: Primary.)*
3. **The ecumenical extension (the double-edged twist).** He immediately extends the same verdict: "I have absolutely no problem saying the same thing about the Old Testament, the New Testament, and the Quran... regardless of faith or lack thereof, we have so much more in common than we do differences."[^3] *(Evidence rating: n/a — interpretive. Argument weight: Primary — this is what distinguishes the claim from a simple pro-Book-of-Mormon result.)*

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
**Steelman of the critic.** The strongest objections are structural, and several are sharp. (1) *Category mismatch.* [[Matthew 7.15-20|Matthew 7:15-20]] is a test for discerning **false prophets** by the *fruit of their conduct and its results*, not a rubric for scoring a text's thematic vocabulary; recasting "fruit" as embedding-similarity to moral probes is a genre shift Jesus did not make. (2) *The test, as applied, proves too much.* By passing the Quran on the very same criterion, the method demonstrably **cannot distinguish true scripture from false** — so it can establish, at most, "morally admirable," never "divinely inspired" or "historically true," and certainly not the Book of Mormon's *unique* authority. A traditional reading takes "fruits" to include doctrinal truth and fulfilled prophecy, on which these texts do **not** agree. (3) *Necessary, not sufficient.* A devout 19th-century author steeped in the Bible could produce a Christ-centered, morally elevated book; good fruit in this thin sense is fully compatible with human authorship. (4) *Self-limited source.* The creator opens by disclaiming the work as "by no means academic," not peer-reviewed and not "scientific in any way," and the guardrail [[A Data Analysis of Scripture Is Only as Objective as Its Model]] applies — so the verdict is an honest personal opinion, not a demonstration.

**Response (the note's own weighting).** Taken at the author's own modest altitude, the argument is fair: as a *good-faith invitation* to a secular or interfaith audience, "read it and judge the fruit for yourself" is legitimate and disarming. But it must not be inflated into an authenticity proof. Its LDS value is rhetorical framing for [[Book of Mormon Evidences]], and the honest resting point is the creator's companion admission that spiritual experience, not statistics, is finally why people read scripture — see [[Spiritual Confirmation Not Data Is Why People Keep Reading Scripture]].
