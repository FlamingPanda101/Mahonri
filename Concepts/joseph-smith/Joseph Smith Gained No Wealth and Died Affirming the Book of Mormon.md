# Joseph Smith Gained No Wealth and Died Affirming the Book of Mormon

## The Claim (Objection Rebutted)

The most durable *motive* for the fraud thesis is money: a man produces a "fantastical" book to sell copies and get rich. The interviewing Evangelical pastor names this as his own best steelman — "why would they put together this fantastical book with all of these claims... Was this an attempt to sell books to make money?"[^1] [[Casey Griffiths]]'s answer is that the profit motive is exactly the part of the con-man theory the documentary record most flatly contradicts: no one got rich, the principals were financially ruined and physically hunted, and Joseph died still bound to the book — the opposite of what a swindler cashes out on.[^2]

## The Rebuttal

### 1. Nobody got rich from the Book of Mormon — rate: strong
Griffiths: "they don't get rich off the Book of Mormon. Nobody does."[^2] Far from profiting, [[Martin Harris]] **mortgaged his farm** to fund the first printing — 5,000 copies for roughly $3,000 — surrendering "a lot of his livelihood."[^3] A scheme to sell books does not begin by having the financier pawn his land; the money flows the wrong way for fraud.
> **Evidence rating:** Strong — the Harris mortgage is documented and uncontested; the "get rich" thesis predicts the reverse cash flow.

### 2. They were pursued and impoverished for life, and Joseph died a prisoner — rate: strong
"If that was their plan, they had no idea what they were getting themselves into, because they are pursued the rest of their life."[^4] Griffiths points to [[D&C 135]]: on the day they were killed the men were still devoted to the record, and Joseph "was preaching to the guard in Carthage Jail" — "that sounds like a little bit more than a" con.[^4] The martyrdom seals the point: [[Hyrum Smith]] "dies alongside of him in Carthage jail,"[^5] and the family's early commitment ran just as deep — [[Alvin Smith]] on his deathbed charged Joseph, "Be a good boy. Do everything you can to obtain the record."[^6] Liars-for-gain do not, as a rule, die in jail beside their brother rather than recant.
> **Evidence rating:** Strong — the martyrdom is a fixed historical datum; a profit motive predicts recantation or flight, not death in custody.

### 3. A lifelong, interlocking cast paid a cost and never cashed in — rate: moderate-to-strong
Griffiths: if Joseph is a con man, it is "Academy Awards all around for his mom and his brothers and sisters and the Whitmer family and Emma's family... their stories interlock very well."[^7] The point sharpens once the estrangements are added: [[Oliver Cowdery]] and [[Sydney Rigdon]] were **excommunicated** and the entire Whitmer family **left the church for good**, "but all of them tell a consistent story... to the last day of their lives." Griffiths has stood at [[David Whitmer]]'s grave in Missouri, whose headstone reads, "The record of the Jews and the record of the Nephites are one. Truth is eternal."[^8] People who have lost the movement, the money, and the friendship — yet still refuse to recant — are not being paid off. (Developed at length in [[Witnesses Never Recanted Despite Estrangement]] and [[Witnesses Paid a Real Cost With No Payoff]].)
> **Evidence rating:** Moderate-to-strong — the never-recant-after-estrangement pattern is well documented; "everyone in the cast is acting" is a plausibility argument, not a proof.

### 4. There was no time and no qualified collaborator to manufacture the product — rate: moderate
The profit thesis needs a *means*, and Griffiths argues the means is missing. The 531-page text was produced in "April, May, and June of 1829,"[^9] is "internally consistent... an internal theology... an internal geography," and "adds more names to the English language than Shakespeare does."[^10] The only educated helper, Oliver Cowdery, "is only there, according to the documents, in those three spring months," and invoking a ghostwriter like Sydney Rigdon "showing up early" is "mental gymnastics" against the timeline — "asking someone to produce a book that is as complex as the Book of Mormon, even if you're being generous with the time frame, is a lot."[^11] With no motive-serving payoff *and* no plausible manufacturing pathway, the fraud model runs out of both reasons and means. (The production-speed case is carried in detail by [[Dictation Speed and Method Argue Against Fabrication]] and [[No Naturalistic Source Accounts for the Whole Book of Mormon]].)
> **Evidence rating:** Moderate — the timeline is well sourced; "too complex to fake in three months" is a strong-but-contestable inference (see Steelman).

## Best Quotes

> "They don't get rich off the Book of Mormon. Nobody does. In fact, Martin Harris mortgages his farm and gives up a lot of his livelihood to pay for this."[^2]

> "If that was their plan, they had no idea what they were getting themselves into, because they are pursued the rest of their life."[^4]

> "That's the thing — if he's a con man, I mean, Academy Awards all around for his mom and his brothers and sisters and the Whitmer family and Emma's family... their stories interlock very well."[^7]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

A careful critic answers on three fronts. **(1) Absence of profit is not sincerity.** A *sincere but self-deceived* founder (the pious-fraud / visionary model) equally predicts poverty and martyrdom — Joseph could have believed his own claims and still have been mistaken; dying for a belief proves it was *held*, not that it was *true*. This rebuts the cynical grifter caricature, not the serious naturalistic case. **(2) Non-monetary payoffs exist.** Prophetic authority, status, followers, and marriages are gains a purely financial ledger misses; "he didn't get rich" does not mean "he gained nothing." **(3) The means argument is probabilistic.** Rapid composition by an unusually fluent, immersed dictator is not physically impossible, and the "more names than Shakespeare / internally consistent" claims are apologetic framings a critic will discount. **Reply:** the note's target is specifically the *profit motive* the pastor himself advanced, and on that narrow question the record is lopsided — the financier mortgages his farm, the principals are hunted to death, and the estranged witnesses never cash in. Griffiths grants the larger point that the story is "fantastic" and that "there are weak points" (see [[If You Look for a Reason to Reject Joseph Smith You Will Find One]]); the claim here is modest and comparative — a swindle is a poor explanation for a life that ended poorer and in jail. Whether Joseph was *sincere-and-mistaken* rather than *true* is the live debate, and it belongs to [[The Fuller Picture of Joseph Smith Undercuts the Self-Conscious-Fraud Model]] and [[Debate - Joseph Smith Prophet or Fraud]].
