# If You Look for a Reason to Reject Joseph Smith You Will Find One

#type/claim

## The Claim
Having read [[Joseph Smith]]'s personal papers for fifteen years, [[Ryan Wood]] says he can "completely understand why somebody wouldn't" and "how somebody very much would" still believe Joseph was a prophet — because the *same corpus* yields either verdict depending on the disposition the reader brings. The honest method is a **faithful approach: read with your eyes wide open** — refusing both hostile debunking and hagiographic whitewash. "If you're looking for a reason to hate the guy, you're probably going to find it, but that doesn't mean you're taking a truly dispassionate and faithful approach."[^1]

## Supporting Evidence

1. **The evidence underdetermines the verdict.** The reader who wants a villain and the reader who wants "the holiest of holy prophets" will *each* find confirmation in the papers; Wood's stated goal was "to show all of that at once" rather than curate one side.[^2] *Evidence rating: Strong — the core epistemic point, and a candid one.*

2. **Concrete "ick" data he does not hide.** Joseph once got angry, **dictated a revelation, crumpled it up, and threw it** at the man: "Take that" — which, Wood grants, "is not very prophetic."[^3] He also names the discomfort around [[Fanny Alger]] and things Joseph asked people not to tell Emma "because she wouldn't understand."[^4] *Evidence rating: Strong — primary-source specifics offered against interest.*

3. **Discomfort is not disqualification.** As the host presses, the crumpled-revelation story "just shows he's human and gets angry"; uncomfortable material is "not disqualifying of a prophet." "Well, if you're looking for a reason, then you'll find plenty of reasons" — the reasons track the *searching disposition*, not the prophetic question.[^5] *Evidence rating: Strong.*

4. **Intellectual vs. spiritual testimony.** Wood frames the split as the difference between an **intellectual** testimony (which the mixed data can leave genuinely undecided) and a **spiritual** one — so the papers neither compel nor preclude belief.[^6] *Evidence rating: Moderate — a useful distinction, though a critic will call it an escape hatch (see Steelmanning).*

## Best Quotes

> "If you're looking for a reason to hate the guy, you're probably going to find it, but that doesn't mean you're taking a truly dispassionate and faithful approach to the work."[^1]

> "If you're really obsessed about making this man into the holiest of holy prophets, you're going to find that too. My hope was to show all of that at once."[^2]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
The strongest objection is **unfalsifiability**: if favorable data prove a prophet and unfavorable data are excused as "you were looking for a reason," then no evidence could ever count against him — the claim is insulated. A fair reply distinguishes two things. (a) Wood is not saying evidence is irrelevant; he is making the ordinary hermeneutical point that *motivated reasoning in both directions* distorts historical judgment, and that a "faithful approach" is defined precisely as reading with eyes open, granting the ugly parts. (b) The believer still shoulders a burden: the affirmative case must rest on positive data (see [[Joseph Smith's Restraint Is an Intellectual Testimony]] and the translation witnesses), not merely on discounting the negative. The critic is right that "you'll find what you look for" cuts *both ways* — which is Wood's actual claim, not special pleading for one side. Compare the ontological version of this move in [[Prophets Are Fallible Not Divine]] and the presentism caution in [[Presentism in Folk Belief Judgment]].
