# Joseph Smith's Restraint Is an Intellectual Testimony

#type/claim

## The Claim
[[Ryan Wood]]'s affirmative case does not rest on any single miracle but on a pattern of **restraint**: "I am convinced Joseph Smith was a prophet not because of anything recorded that he did, but because of what he did *not* do." A power-seeker would have grasped; Joseph — and after him [[Brigham Young]] — repeatedly declined justifiable revenge and deliberately limited his own authority. That forbearance, Wood argues, is an "intellectual testimony" that they were who they claimed to be.[^1]

## Supporting Evidence

1. **He did not rush to found a religion.** A man who claimed God and Christ appeared to him in the woods would ordinarily "start their own religion right away." Joseph instead waited about **ten years** — until the Book of Mormon — and moved incrementally (Aaronic priesthood, baptizing, still no church; then Melchizedek priesthood, still no church), as if acting only when instructed. Wood ties the pattern to Joseph "learning his lesson" after the loss of the 116 pages.[^2] *Evidence rating: Moderate-to-strong — a genuine feature of the chronology.*

2. **He built opposition to himself into the structure.** The revelations set up a [[Joseph Smith|First Presidency]] with power, but also a Quorum of the Twelve that can act against it and a Seventy with equal authority — a system of checks Wood likens to a leader "giving back the war powers to Congress." "Who does that?"[^3] *Evidence rating: Moderate — the structural claim is real; its motive is interpretive.*

3. **Brigham Young declined revenge after the martyrdom.** With [[Hyrum Smith]] and Joseph murdered and towns around Nauvoo fleeing in fear of a religious-zealot militia, Brigham had the means — including [[Orrin Porter Rockwell]] — to retaliate massively, yet did not. Wood compares it to Abraham Lincoln forgiving the South.[^4] *Evidence rating: Moderate-to-strong.*

4. **The restraint is a recurring theme, not a one-off.** "The amount of restraint that these guys had, and the … justifiable things that they could have done and didn't, to me is an intellectual testimony that they're actually who they were." It runs "throughout his life up until the end."[^5] *Evidence rating: N/A — the thesis.*

## Best Quotes

> "I am convinced Joseph Smith was a prophet not because of anything recorded that he did, but because of what he did not do."[^1]

> "Any other person back then would have started their own religion right away. He waits 10 years … It's weird. Either he was restrained … or the logical thing would be that he was instructed not to until he was told to."[^2]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
Two serious objections. **(1) Restraint may be prudence, not virtue.** After the martyrdom the Saints were outnumbered and fleeing; large-scale retaliation against the United States would have been suicidal, so non-retaliation could be strategic calculation rather than prophetic forbearance. And a decade-long ramp-up to 1830 is exactly what ordinary movement-building looks like — no divine patience required. **(2) The "checks on his own power" reading is selective.** From the *same* Joseph Smith Papers, Wood elsewhere notes the Council of Fifty minutes in which Brigham Young is styled the "Theocratic King" of Deseret; Joseph also organized the Nauvoo Legion, led the Council of Fifty, and ran for U.S. president — hardly the profile of a man only *diffusing* authority. A fair reply keeps the claim modest and comparative: Wood explicitly offers this as an **intellectual** testimony — a probabilistic consideration, not a proof (see [[If You Look for a Reason to Reject Joseph Smith You Will Find One]]) — and the comparison class matters. Figures with far weaker theophany claims and far more provocation have seized far more and forgiven far less; the incremental founding, the structural opposition, and Brigham's forbearance with a loaded [[Orrin Porter Rockwell]] available are real data points that a purely cynical "power-grab" model does not predict.
