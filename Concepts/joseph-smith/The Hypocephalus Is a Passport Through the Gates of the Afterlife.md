# The Hypocephalus Is a Passport Through the Gates of the Afterlife

## The Claim
Egyptologist [[John Gee]] — who publishes on this exact document type in mainstream peer-reviewed venues — explains that [[Facsimile 2]] is a **hypocephalus**: a class of Egyptian funerary amulet placed at the head of a mummy so the deceased can "pass through any gate" in the next life. Knowing what the object *is* and *does* reframes the popular "just translate the glyphs and it's game over" critique: a hypocephalus is a separate, esoteric ritual amulet with a temple-like function, not a page of the Abraham narrative, and its genre is passage-through-gates — the very idea Latter-day Saints associate with temple worth­iness.[^1]

## Supporting Evidence

1. **The name literally means "at the head" (rate: strong; B-tier Egyptologist, philology).** "Hypocephalus" comes from a Greek phrase *hypo tēn kephalēn*, "under the head," itself based on a mis-rendering of an Egyptian text reading *ẖr tp* — literally "under the head" but idiomatically "**at** the head." The amulets are found on top of, and behind, mummies' heads; they are not literally under the head (standing a mummy on its head was a curse), so "at the head" is the accurate sense.[^1]

2. **They are a well-attested, catalogued class (rate: strong; B-tier, published catalogue).** About **100 to 120** hypocephali are known and catalogued (Gee references the standard photographic catalogue, Mékis, *The Hypocephalus*). This is a real Egyptian genre with a large comparative corpus — the basis on which any figure can be checked against parallels.[^2]

3. **They are customizable — no two are exactly alike (rate: strong; B-tier, corpus survey).** Ranging "everything from... just like a cow in the middle" to elaborate examples with three ankhs, hypocephali were personalized. There are recurring **general types and common motifs**, but "no two hypocephali really are alike." This variability is decisive for judging Joseph Smith's restored figures (see [[Joseph Smith's Facsimile 2 Restorations Fall Within the Attested Hypocephalus Range]]).[^3]

4. **Its function (per Book of the Dead 162) is passage and protection (rate: strong; B-tier, primary Egyptian text).** The associated text — Book of the Dead spell **162** — says you place it "at the head or around the neck" to do one of two things: for a pharaoh, "destroy his enemies in the present life"; for the deceased, so "they can pass through any gate that they encounter in the next life." Gee's memorable gloss: "it's almost like your yarmulke serves as your temple recommend... a passport of sorts."[^4]

5. **The spell is explicitly esoteric (rate: moderate; B-tier, primary text).** BoD 162 places a flame under the sun-god Ra's head so "he is in the divine afterlife... he is resurrected," then commands: "This is a great and secret book. Do not allow anyone's eyes to see it... He who knows it and keeps it secret, he will continue to exist." It also transmits secret names to recite for passage. Secrecy, sacred names, and gated passage are the object's own native categories — not later LDS overlay.[^5]

6. **Facsimile 2 is a separate artifact from Facsimiles 1 and 3 (rate: strong; B-tier, prosopography).** Facsimile 1 is part of a roll bearing its owner's name and genealogy, and Facsimile 3 carries that *same* name; Facsimile 2 carries a **different** owner's name — **Sheshonq**. The 19th-century dealers "separated out the papyri from the mummies... and when they sold them in lots, they would just grab some of this and grab some of that," so Facsimile 2 traveled with the others by accident of sale, not because it is part of the same document.[^6]

**Implication.** The critique "the hieroglyphs can just be translated, game over" treats Facsimile 2 as if it were a paragraph of Abraham awaiting decoding. It is instead a distinct amulet from a customizable genre whose whole purpose is gated, secret, temple-like passage — which is exactly the register in which Joseph Smith read it.

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
A critic can grant every point here and still press the real objection: *describing* the genre does not rescue Joseph Smith's specific **interpretations**. That a hypocephalus is about afterlife passage does not license Joseph's readings of Kolob, the four quarters of the earth, or God sitting on his throne; the standard Egyptological glosses for these figures (Amun-Re, the four sons of Horus, Mehet-weret/Hathor) are not what Joseph's captions say. The genre-and-function framing is therefore best read modestly — it shows the object is esoteric and temple-adjacent, and that "just translate it" is naive, but it does not by itself vindicate any caption. Those are met on their own ground in [[Facsimile 2's Temple-Gate Inscriptions Vindicate Joseph Smith's Temple Reading]] and [[Facsimile 2 Is an Astronomical Document Matching the Book of Abraham's Cosmology]], and the interpretive-humility point is developed in [[Egyptologists Disagree Across Time and Place on What a Drawing Means]].
