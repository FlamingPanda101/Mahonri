# The Restoration Repeats Christianitys Own Break From Judaism

## The Claim
[[Devin Barnes|Devin]] presses a **parallel-cases** argument: first-century Christianity broke from Judaism on the very terms the Restoration broke from historic Christianity, so any rule that legitimizes the first must legitimize the second — or be exposed as special pleading.[^1] Early Christians claimed to be the true heirs of a corrupted Judaism, keeping its scriptures while announcing new revelation and new authority; Joseph Smith claimed to recover a corrupted Christianity on the same pattern. Devin therefore demands a *non-circular* criterion that vindicates the New Testament rupture from Judaism while rejecting the Latter-day Saint rupture from creedal Christianity.

## Evidence
- The structural parallel — a reforming movement calling its parent tradition apostate while claiming continuity with it — is the same one developed in [[Christianity and Judaism Were Meant to Be One Religion]] and, for the Catholic case, [[Catholic Doctrinal Development Is the Same Double Standard]].
- It reframes "Are Latter-day Saints Christians?" ([[Are Latter-day Saints Christians]]) as a question about *consistency of criteria*: the critic who accepts the Jewish-to-Christian break but rejects the Christian-to-LDS break owes a principled, non-question-begging difference between them.
- The argument is defensive rather than evidential — it neutralizes the "you left the real tradition" charge without itself establishing Joseph Smith's claims, which is why it sits opposite the negative's [[Every Testable Joseph Smith Claim Fails Only Subjective Evidence Survives]].

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
Daniel (the negative) **rebuts** the symmetry on two grounds. (1) *Fulfillment vs. correction*: Christians read the new covenant as the promised fulfillment of the Hebrew scriptures — an internal completion the prophets themselves foretell — whereas, he argues, no comparable prophecy foretells a Restoration through Joseph Smith. (2) *Testability*: the apostolic break rested on the resurrection and public apostolic witness, while Joseph Smith's distinctive claims (the Book of Abraham, manuscript-less prophecy inserted by the Joseph Smith Translation) are *testable and fail*, so the cases are not on a par. Devin's rejoinder is that "fulfillment" is itself a post-hoc theological reading the first-century Jew rejected for the same reasons the modern Christian rejects the Restoration — which is exactly the circularity his argument targets.
