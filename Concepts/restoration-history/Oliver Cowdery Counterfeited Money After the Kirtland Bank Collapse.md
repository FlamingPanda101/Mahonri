# Oliver Cowdery Counterfeited Money After the Kirtland Bank Collapse

#type/claim

## The Claim
One of the reasons [[Oliver Cowdery]] was excommunicated (1838) is that after the Kirtland Safety Society collapsed he kept **printing bank notes without authorization** — which, [[Ryan Wood]] notes, is by definition counterfeiting. Reading the whole Joseph Smith Papers, Wood treats it not as the lurid crime the anti-Mormon trope implies but as a mundane, sloppy 1800s money affair: "nothing is as elaborate or cool as it is in the 20th or 21st century."[^1]

## Supporting Evidence

1. **The charter problem forced an "anti-banking" workaround.** When authorities would not allow new banks to incorporate, the Society could not operate as a chartered bank; so, on already-printed plates, they scratched out the wording by hand and wrote "anti-banking society" on the notes to avoid breaking the law.[^2] *Evidence rating: Moderate — Wood's compressed retelling of the Kirtland Safety Society Anti-Banking Company episode; the legal specifics are loose.*

2. **Cowdery fetched the plates and printed past his stop order.** Cowdery went (Wood recalls to Philadelphia) to obtain the printing plates and bring them back, then continued printing money even after he "wasn't allowed to."[^3] *Evidence rating: Moderate.*

3. **The notes were hand-signed documents.** Every bill was signed by hand — [[Joseph Smith]], and sometimes Sidney Rigdon — because in the 1800s "a bank note wasn't a bank note until it was signed by the banker"; the JSP images show the handwritten bills.[^4] *Evidence rating: Strong — documentary (JSP holographs).*

4. **He never apologized for it at his church trial.** In the minutes of his excommunication proceedings, "the one thing he never apologized for was counterfeiting money." He wanted his money back (like others, he had deposits in the Society), took the printing plates with him, and left without really defending himself.[^5] *Evidence rating: Strong — court minutes preserved in the JSP.*

5. **The label is technically accurate but far less severe than the myth.** "Printing money without permission … is the definition of a counterfeiter," so Cowdery was "a counterfeiter technically speaking" — yet the reality is a failed community bank, not a criminal forging another sovereign's currency.[^6] *Evidence rating: Strong (as a framing point).*

## Best Quotes

> "The one thing he never apologized for was counterfeiting money."[^5]

> "Oliver Cowdery was a counterfeiter technically speaking cuz he was printing money without permission, which is the definition of a counterfeiter. So it's a little less severe."[^6]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
A critic reads this as confirmation of the "money-digging con-man" narrative: a founding Book of Mormon witness was a literal counterfeiter, so the whole enterprise is tainted. The faithful reply has three parts. (a) The act was **unauthorized reprinting of the failed Society's own notes** by a depositor trying to recover his money — not fraudulent forgery of someone else's currency. (b) The **church itself disciplined him** for it; concealment is the opposite of what happened. (c) Cowdery's misconduct never touched his testimony: even while estranged and under mob threat he reaffirmed the Book of Mormon (see [[Oliver Cowdery]] and [[William McLellin Affirmed the Book of Mormon While Rejecting Church Leadership]]), and he later returned to the church. The episode shows a flawed man inside a real historical economy, which is exactly Wood's point about resetting expectations for the 1830s.
