# Multiple Independent Witnesses Attest the Kirtland Temple Pentecost

#type/claim

## The Claim
Asked whether reading the Joseph Smith Papers ever made something seem *more* miraculous than he had thought, [[Ryan Wood]] answered: "the entire Kirtland Temple dedication experience." The Pentecostal phenomena of 1836 — angels, fire, a rushing wind, tongues — are recorded by **several independent witnesses, writing in different ways across different periods, who describe the same events**. Applying a historian's standard of multiple primary attestation, Wood says the convergence "could not be explained by any other phenomenon other than miracle."[^1]

## Supporting Evidence

1. **Independent accounts of the same theophany.** During patriarchal blessings connected to the dedication (Joseph Smith Sr. blessing his family, e.g. at the John Johnson home), "several people who through different experiences describe the same thing happening" — angels appearing and, in Joseph Smith's identification, God the Father and Jesus Christ.[^2] *Evidence rating: Strong (as multiple attestation) — the core of the argument.*

2. **Concrete, unusual sensory detail.** Witnesses reported "a man walk by who was covered in fire" and another "covered in angels … so brilliant we couldn't hardly look at him," with someone passing out; Joseph explained, "that was Jesus Christ walking by and the other one was God the Father."[^3] *Evidence rating: Moderate-to-strong — vivid, specific, and cross-reported.*

3. **The dedication itself was multiply documented.** "Several people writing in several different ways through several periods of time … reaffirm that yes, there were angels in the room, yes, there was a rushing mighty wind," and people spoke in tongues.[^4] *Evidence rating: Strong.*

4. **The "language of Adam" song was written down.** A song was sung in tongues (the "language of Adam"), recorded in the facsimile edition of the Revelation books — the manuscript source of the Doctrine and Covenants — with its English interpretation also written; a large "X" marks that it was *not* published in the D&C. That the participants "took the time to record this stuff" argues against mere emotional theatrics.[^5] *Evidence rating: Moderate — documentary, resting on Wood's reading of the Revelation manuscripts.*

5. **A historian's-method framing.** Wood casts it as: primary sources, eyewitnesses at the location, multiple attestations, "uncorroborated witnesses all said the exact same thing" — so "through the scientific method of history I'm forced to say this is … the data."[^6] *Evidence rating: N/A — methodological claim.*

## Best Quotes

> "There were several people writing in several different ways through several periods of time that reaffirm that yes, there were angels in the room."[^4]

> "Uncorroborated witnesses all said the exact same thing … It could not be explained by any other phenomenon other than miracle."[^6]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
A skeptic offers naturalistic deflation: (a) the Kirtland dedication was an **emotionally primed revival** in which contagious enthusiasm, fasting, and suggestion generate shared ecstatic experience — tongues and visions recur in many such 19th-century meetings without implying the supernatural. (b) The accounts are not truly "uncorroborated" in the sense Wood wants: many were written **later**, by committed insiders, and may have harmonized over time. (c) Calling a sung glossolalia "the language of Adam" assumes what is to be proved. The faithful reply is that Wood is making the modest historian's point, not a proof: *by the ordinary standard applied to any ancient event*, independent multiple attestation of specific, unusual detail is strong evidence that *something* extraordinary was experienced — and dismissing it purely because it is religious is itself a bias, not a finding. This pairs with the typological case in [[The Kirtland Dedication Recapitulates Christ's Final Week]] and the vision of Christ accepting the house in [[D&C 110]].
