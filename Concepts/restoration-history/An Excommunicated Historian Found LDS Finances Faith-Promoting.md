# An Excommunicated Historian Found LDS Finances Faith-Promoting

## The Claim
The critics' insinuation is that Church leaders personally benefit from the Church's wealth — that "their hedge fund is their ring and they just can't give it up."[^1] [[Jacob Hansen]] calls this "one of the dumbest arguments out there" and answers it with a hostile witness: the late [[D. Michael Quinn]], an academic historian excommunicated in the 1990s and "at times a very critical person towards the church," who was given "essentially unfettered access to the church's historical financial records" and wrote the 600-page *The Mormon Hierarchy: Wealth and Corporate Power* — concluding that the subject "was not an area worthy of serious criticism."[^2][^3]

## Supporting Evidence
1. **A critic-historian judged the finances "faith-promoting."** Quinn, though excommunicated, said the book "could be viewed as faith-promoting" because, once you get past the staggering totals ("billions, tens of billions... maybe hundreds of billions"), the expenditures show "this money is overwhelmingly going for the benefit of the rank and file."[^3][^4] *Argument weight: primary. Evidence rating: strong — a hostile witness's conclusion carries extra evidential force, and Quinn is a B-tier credentialed historian.*
2. **Leaders are paid modestly and by their own vote.** Quinn: a 2014 document set the general authorities' living allowance at "$120,000 a year" on an equal basis — and the First Presidency and Quorum of the Twelve "are the ones who set those salaries."[^5] *Weight: primary. Rating: strong.*
3. **The Church pays its own employees more than its President.** A 2010 report from Canada showed the Church's highest-paid employee there received "more than double what the president of the church was receiving" — i.e., leaders "allow rank and file Mormons to be paid double what they receive."[^6] *Weight: secondary. Rating: strong — a striking, specific inversion of the greed narrative.*
4. **The nonprofit comparison is lopsided.** The president of the Red Cross "receives 1.2 million dollars annually" — roughly ten times the LDS Church president's allowance, for a far smaller organization.[^7] And [[Russell M. Nelson]] left "his lucrative career as a world-renowned heart surgeon" for a $120,000 living allowance "in a job that you can never retire from."[^8] *Weight: secondary. Rating: moderate-to-strong (the Red Cross figure is second-hand in the source; Nelson's career change is well documented).*
5. **A living allowance is not a paid ministry.** The brethren manage "massive real estate holdings," investment portfolios, universities, and relief operations; a secular manager of "billions in real estate" or "billions in stocks and bonds" would earn far more than $120,000. The allowance compensates a genuine sacrifice of career and retirement, not a windfall.[^9] *Weight: secondary. Rating: moderate.*

## Best Quotes
> "He was actually excommunicated from the church in the 1990s... But Quinn was a good faith actor, and he realized that the church's financial history was not an area worthy of serious criticism." [^2]

> "This money is overwhelmingly going for the benefit of the rank and file." — [[D. Michael Quinn]] [^4]

> "Do people really think that President Nelson left his lucrative career as a world-renowned heart surgeon to make a hundred and twenty thousand dollars a year...?" [^8]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
Steelman: Quinn remained a believer in the Restoration even after his excommunication, so "hostile witness" somewhat overstates it; and a $120,000 allowance plus housing and benefits is still real compensation, not volunteer poverty — the "unpaid lay ministry" claim is strictly true only of local leaders, since general authorities are supported. Fairly granted. But the argument does not need Quinn to be an atheist — only to be a rigorous critic with access and a motive to expose wrongdoing, which he was; and the compensation point is answered on its own terms: roughly 100 supported leaders out of a 17-million-member church, none enriched relative to comparable secular or nonprofit roles (see [[The SEC Settlement Does Not Prove the Church Hid Its Wealth]], "99% of church leaders are unpaid," and [[Church Wealth and Welfare Are Evidence Not Scandal]] on the absent yachts and jets). The follow-the-money test finds no individual getting rich.
