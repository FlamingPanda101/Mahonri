# The Apostles Were Martyred to Become Missionaries in the Spirit World

#type/claim

## The Claim
[[Jonah Barnes]], on *Latter Daily Saints* (Ep. 186), advances a thesis from his book *The Missing
Ministry of the Risen Christ*: during the **40 days** between the Resurrection and Ascension, Christ
told the apostles that the **real missionary work is in the spirit world**, and deliberately
commissioned them to martyrdom for that end. Christ's descent (see [[1 Peter 3.18-20|1 Peter
3:18-20]]) had just "opened the gates," leaving "billions and billions" of the dead who could now
hear the gospel but had **no missionaries** — so the apostles were sent to their deaths to become
those missionaries. Barnes reads the Restoration as echoing the pattern: the martyred apostle
[[David W. Patten]], and the English convert [[Anne Booth]]'s vision of him preaching to the
spirits in prison, are offered as a modern instance of a slain apostle continuing his ministry
among the dead.[^1][^2][^5]

## Supporting Evidence
1. **The doctrinal substrate is solid and independently attested.** Postmortem preaching to the
   "spirits in prison" ([[1 Peter 3.18-20|1 Peter 3:18-20]]), the intermediate state
   ([[Alma 40.11|Alma 40:11]]), and the redemption of the dead ([[D&C 138]]) are established
   Restoration doctrine with real ancient pedigree — see [[The Descent of Christ to the Underworld Was Universally Attested]] and [[D&C 138 Restores the Harrowing of Hell Recorded in the Gospel of Nicodemus]]. *Evidence rating: Strong (the underlying doctrine); Argument weight: Primary.*[^1]
2. **David W. Patten's martyrdom is historical.** Patten, an apostle of the early Restoration, was
   mortally wounded leading a rescue at the **Battle of Crooked River** (Missouri, 1838); the account
   has him charging the hill at the front, and dying after telling his wife, "Stay true to your
   testimony." *Evidence rating: Strong on the fact of the martyrdom (details relayed loosely here);
   Argument weight: Secondary.*[^3]
3. **The Ann Booth vision as a Restoration echo.** As recounted, an Englishwoman ([[Anne Booth]]),
   relating her experience to [[Parley P. Pratt]], saw "a vision of the spirits in prison" in which "a
   man from America" with "a mustache and dark features" arrived to teach the waiting dead — a figure
   she later identified as the deceased Patten. Barnes reads this as a martyred apostle taking up the
   spirit-world mission Christ described in the 40 days. *Evidence rating: Weak (a 19th-century lay
   visionary account, relayed third-hand and loosely here — the hosts fumble the name mid-story);
   Argument weight: Tertiary.*[^4][^5]

## Best Quotes
> "Christ makes it very clear that this church is not going to last, and instead, 'I need you
> apostles to die,' because the real missionary work is in the afterlife. The real missionary work is
> in the spirit world." [^1]

> "The gates had just been opened. There were billions and billions of people who now could hear the
> gospel, and no missionaries." [^2]

> "She says, 'I saw a vision of the spirits in prison and they were all waiting around, and there was
> a man from America who came to them, and he had a mustache and dark features'... that's the guy who
> I saw come to the spirits of the dead." [^4]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
- **The strongest objection targets the *intent* Barnes reads in.** The New Testament presents the
  apostles as martyred **for** their witness (John 21:18-19 foretells Peter's death), not commissioned
  **in order to** die as a spirit-world missionary strategy; "God let them be killed so they could
  preach to the dead" is a devotional harmonization layered onto the martyrdoms, not a stated apostolic
  teaching. A critic grants the descent doctrine while denying this specific teleology.
- **"This church is not going to last" is the contested apostasy premise.** Barnes's claim that Christ
  predicted the primitive church would fail is the LDS Great-Apostasy thesis, which mainstream
  Christianity rejects (Matthew 16:18, "the gates of hell shall not prevail"); the thesis cannot be
  assumed as neutral ground.
- **The Booth vision is not primary evidence.** It is a 19th-century visionary's self-report, here
  relayed third-hand by comedic hosts who cannot recall her name; visionary identifications of a
  spirit are unfalsifiable, and nothing in the retelling is verifiable against a primary record. It can
  *illustrate* the doctrine for believers but cannot *establish* Barnes's thesis.
- **Sourcing caution.** The 40-days framework, the apocryphal parallels, and the Booth account are all
  relayed from Barnes's self-published books at a comedy show's altitude; each should be checked against
  primary sources and the higher-tier vault notes before being leaned on.

**What survives:** an evocative synthesis — the spirit-world mission is genuine Restoration doctrine
with ancient roots, and Patten's martyrdom is historical — but the load-bearing claim (that Christ
*commissioned the apostles to die* specifically to staff a spirit-world mission) is Barnes's
interpretive thesis, resting on a contested apostasy premise and an unverifiable vision, not on
demonstrable exegesis.
