# The Robber Council Refutes Holy-Spirit-Guided Councils

#type/rebuttal

## The Objection Being Answered
A standard defense of creedal authority holds that **the Holy Spirit guided the ecumenical councils**, so their definitions are protected from error. [[Alex Arnold]] treats this as "the entire authority structure that creedal Christianity rests on" and declines to straw-man it — but argues the history creates a problem the theological claim must account for.[^1]

## The Rebuttal
The **Second Council of Ephesus (449 AD)**, convened by the emperor Theodosius II, formally adopted a Christology the church at the time recognized as legitimate. Just **two years later** the **Council of Chalcedon (451)** reversed it, and — in [[Leo the Great|Pope Leo I]]'s own phrase — branded Ephesus 449 a *latrocinium*, a "**robber council**." Both councils claimed the guidance of the Holy Spirit; one of them was wrong.[^2] That is the structural problem: if two Spirit-invoking councils reach opposite verdicts, then "the Holy Spirit guided the councils" is functioning as a **post-hoc ratification of whatever the winning side actually decided**, not as an actual mechanism of divine disclosure operating *before* the decision is made.[^3]

## Supporting Evidence
1. **The reversal is documented church history, not LDS polemic.** Ephesus II (449) and its overturning at Chalcedon (451), and Leo's "robber synod" verdict, are standard in every history of the Christological controversies. *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Strong.*[^2]
2. **The mechanism is exposed by the timing.** Two years is too short for the underlying facts to have changed; what changed was the political and ecclesiastical balance of power. Spirit-guidance invoked equally by both sides cannot discriminate between them, so it cannot be the thing doing the deciding. *Argument weight: Primary.*[^3]
3. **It generalizes.** The same council apparatus produced other reversals and coercions (e.g., [[Constantinople II Excommunicated Pope Vigilius]]; the imperial enforcement traced in [[Trinity Survived Only Through Imperial Coercion]]), so Ephesus/Chalcedon is a representative case, not a lone anomaly. *Argument weight: Secondary.*

## Why It Matters
If Spirit-guidance only *ratifies* the victor after the fact, it cannot serve as the neutral guarantee that underwrites using the creeds to disqualify others. And the "the Spirit guided the imperfect councils anyway" move is **symmetric**: it applies with equal force to [[Joseph Smith]] and any other claimant, so it cannot be wielded selectively against the Restoration. This is the same authority-mechanism problem that runs through [[What the Deuteronomists Cut Is What the Creeds Replaced]].

## Best Quote
> "Both councils claimed the guidance of the Holy Spirit but one of them was wrong. That's the whole structural problem right there... the Holy Spirit guided the council's claim is functioning as a post hoc ratification of whatever the winning side actually decided."[^3]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
A creedal defender can reply that a **"robber council" is precisely one the church later judged *not* ecumenical** — reception by the wider church, not the bare claim of a synod, is the criterion, so Chalcedon's overturning of Ephesus 449 is the system *working*, not failing. The force of Arnold's point then narrows: the "Holy Spirit guided it" assurance is only available *retrospectively*, once you already know which council won — which is exactly his charge that it ratifies rather than guides. A second reply distinguishes *infallible* ecumenical councils from failed synods, but that simply relocates the question to how one identifies an ecumenical council *before* the outcome is known, without appeal to the very authority in dispute. The honest residue on both sides: reception-history is a real criterion, but it is a *human historical* criterion, which is the Latter-day Saint's point.
