# No Bishop Held Universal Jurisdiction in the First Three Centuries

## The Claim

[[Jacob Hansen]] argues, again "as a historian," that there was **no pope in the early church** — defining "pope" minimally as a single bishop with *immediate universal jurisdiction over all believers* and an *infallible teaching office*. Testing the window ~100–300 AD, he asks which of two models the data fits: (A) a centralized bishop-of-bishops over all others, or (B) independent bishops governing their own churches as collegial equals. His verdict: the record "seems to be pointing in the opposite direction" from a Roman monarch — model B — and the centralized papacy is a **fourth-century development** that hardened once the church was absorbed into the Roman Empire.[^1]

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. Cyprian: "no bishop of bishops" (rating: strong; argument weight: primary)
[[Cyprian of Carthage]] (mid-3rd c.), in the very act of resisting Rome, writes: "For none of us bishops sets himself up as a bishop of bishops or forces his colleagues to obedience... Each bishop has his own right of judgment." Hansen's inference is about *why* Cyprian could say this: "he wouldn't have made that argument" unless the contemporaries actually understood the bishops as equals.[^2] (The Catholic complication — that Cyprian *also* wrote on the "Chair of Peter" — is treated in [[The Apostles Held Equal Power With Peter]].)

### 2. Victor I could not enforce an excommunication (rating: moderate–strong; argument weight: primary)
When [[Pope Victor I|Victor of Rome]] tried to cut off the Asian churches over the date of Easter, "the other bishops didn't comply" and Irenaeus rebuked him. If Victor held supreme jurisdiction, his sentence would have bound; that it was resisted points to independent jurisdictions.[^3]

### 3. Stephen I never forced Cyprian (rating: moderate; argument weight: primary)
In the rebaptism controversy, [[Pope Stephen I|Stephen of Rome]] "never asserts his authority over Cyprian and excommunicate[s]" him — on Hansen's reading, "because he doesn't have that authority." The disagreement ran on for years unresolved, which a true monarchical primacy would have foreclosed.[^4]

### 4. Clement on electing (not top-down appointing) bishops (rating: moderate; argument weight: secondary)
Hansen reads Clement of Rome as describing bishops *elected* by their congregations rather than appointed "from the top down," consistent with local self-governance.[^5]

### 5. The Chieti Document (rating: moderate; argument weight: primary framing)
Hansen leans on the 2016 Catholic–Orthodox dialogue statement (the **Chieti Document**, rendered "Kieti" in the audio): "In the West, the primacy of Rome was understood *from the fourth century onward*... and this understanding was not adopted in the East." He grants it is not a binding magisterial text, but takes it as a fair summary of where the historical consensus lands "without Catholic presuppositions" — Roman primacy is a *later, gradually interpreted* prerogative.[^6]

### 6. The de-facto-leader model (rating: moderate; argument weight: primary synthesis)
Hansen's positive reconstruction (his "strong hiker" analogy): Rome was a prominent, faithful, well-resourced church whose bishop led *de facto* — like the strongest hiker a group naturally follows — but who *later* "turns around and begins to tell people they need to do things," provoking conflict (Cyprian). By the fourth century, once "Rome adopts the church into its empire," the prerogatives of the Roman pontiff are *gradually increased* because they are tied to imperial power.[^7]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

The Catholic case, stated fully:

1. **Vatican I's two dogmas — and a shared starting point.** The Catholics define the two dogmas precisely: papal *primacy of jurisdiction* (supreme, full, immediate, ordinary, episcopal jurisdiction over the whole church) and papal *infallibility* — the latter a *negative, protective* charism ("not so much about the pope as... about the church" being protected from binding error). Stripped to essentials: "Christ gave authority to St. Peter alone to be the chief shepherd... in an office that is singular." Notably, **Hansen concedes he agrees with *that* minimal claim** ("I actually would") — his dispute is only whether it existed as *jurisdiction* in the early centuries.[^8]

2. **The Chieti Document is a working dialogue text, not magisterial.** It is produced by a joint theological commission and "doesn't tell us which contributions were made by which side," so an Orthodox-favoring paragraph may be the Orthodox contribution. Its paragraph 19 ("the bishop of Rome never exercised canonical authority over the churches of the East") means Rome did not *micromanage* Eastern internal affairs — not that it lacked primacy. Vatican II's *Lumen Gentium* explicitly affirms the bishops are true bishops in their own right; "the pope does not usurp the authority of the bishops for himself."[^9]

3. **Holding an authority ≠ always exercising it (and excommunication is a separate question).** Victor's failure to force compliance, and Stephen's not excommunicating Cyprian, are read by Catholics as prudential or as concerning the *distinct* matter of excommunication, not as proof the primacy was absent.[^10]

4. **The councils appealed *backward*.** At Sardica (343) the bishops said "Rome is the head" and that judgments should go through him (canon four); Hosius of Cordova (who had presided at Nicaea) presided. Stephen's local ruling against rebaptism ultimately *prevailed* and was ratified at later councils. Catholics argue the fourth-century councils were *appealing back* to earlier teaching, not inventing a new claim — evidence the authority was recognized as pre-existing.[^11]

5. **Clement, Ignatius, and consultation of Rome.** Clement of Rome's letter *intervenes* in Corinth's affairs (an outside jurisdiction); Ignatius of Antioch says the church at Rome "presides." The Catholics read these as first- and early-second-century seeds of Roman primacy. (Hansen's counter — that "presides in love" means presiding over its own jurisdiction, and that Irenaeus's "preeminent authority" means prominence — is developed in [[Irenaeus's Preeminent Authority Means Prominence Not Jurisdiction]].)[^12]

**Where the tension sits.** Both sides agree the *sharp Vatican I definition* is later; the dispute is whether that definition unfolds a genuine apostolic primacy (Catholic development) or retrojects imperial-era centralization onto a collegial early church (Hansen). Hansen's "neutral historian" posture is itself contestable, and his key witnesses (Cyprian, Victor) are *hostile* witnesses whose polemics a Catholic reads as the exception that proves an emerging rule.

## LDS Relevance

Hansen's negative conclusion — no supreme Roman office in the early church — is congenial to the Latter-day Saint account, but the LDS *constructive* picture differs from both Rome and Protestantism. Where the Protestant [[The Rock of Matthew 16 Is Peters Confession Not a Papal Office|Gavin Ortlund reads Matthew 16]] as founding no transferable office at all, Latter-day Saints affirm Peter as the *presiding* senior apostle who, with the Twelve, held real keys — keys later *lost* in the Great Apostasy and *restored* through Joseph Smith. Hansen's collegial-equals data thus supports the shared negative (no Roman monarch) while the LDS retain a structured presiding order that the Protestant reading would also pressure. See [[Refusing the Papacy Is Protest Not Schism]].
