# Irenaeus's Preeminent Authority Means Prominence Not Jurisdiction

## The Claim

The single most-cited patristic proof-text for early Roman primacy is [[Irenaeus of Lyons|Irenaeus]], *Against Heresies* 3.3.2, where every church "must agree" with the church of Rome "on account of its **preeminent authority**" (Latin: *propter potentiorem principalitatem*). [[Jacob Hansen]] argues the verse establishes Rome's *prominence*, not a *jurisdictional* supremacy — and that when read in context Irenaeus makes Rome an **outstanding example of the class of apostolic churches**, not a monarch over them.[^1]

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. The Latin does not say "authority" (rating: moderate–strong; argument weight: primary)
Hansen parses the phrase: *potentiorem* ("greater, stronger, more important") and *principalitatem* ("primacy, preeminence, principality"). Crucially, "the word authority isn't used" — Latin *has* a word for authority, **auctoritas**, and Irenaeus does not use it. So translating the phrase as "preeminent *authority*" already imports the conclusion; "prominence / preeminence / primacy" is the more careful rendering.[^1]

### 2. The context is anti-heresy, and the principle is orthodoxy (rating: strong; argument weight: primary)
Irenaeus is refuting Gnostic heretics: the question is *which churches preserve orthodox teaching?* His answer is to go to churches of *apostolic succession*. In his own words, "it is within the power of all in every church... to reckon up those who were instituted bishops," but rather than list every church's succession, he gives Rome's "for brevity's sake." So Rome functions as the *most convenient outstanding example* of the apostolic class, not the sole seat of authority.[^2]

### 3. Peter *and* Paul — not Peter alone (rating: moderate; argument weight: secondary)
Irenaeus grounds Rome's prominence in its founding "by the two most glorious apostles, **Peter and Paul**." Hansen stresses the conjunction: the prestige is apostolic-and-doctrinal (two great apostles), not a transmission of a uniquely *Petrine* office.[^3]

### 4. Catholic scholars concede the "example of a class" reading (rating: strong; argument weight: primary)
Hansen quotes two Catholic historians against the maximal reading. **Robert Eno** (Catholic University of America): "the context of Irenaeus's argument does not claim that the Roman church is literally unique, the only one in its class; rather he argues that the Roman church is the *outstanding example of its class*... before finishing he also refers to Ephesus and Smyrna." **William La Due** (*The Chair of Peter*): "for Irenaeus it is those churches of apostolic foundation that have the greater claim to authentic teaching... *all* of the apostolic churches enjoy what he terms preeminent authority in doctrinal matters."[^4]

### 5. Ignatius: "presides in love" over its own sphere (rating: moderate; argument weight: secondary)
The Catholics also cite [[Ignatius of Antioch]], who calls the Roman church the one that "presides in love." Hansen answers that presiding "in love" is presiding over *its own jurisdiction* — "I can be president over my jurisdiction" — not over all churches.[^5]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

The Catholic reply, at full strength:

1. **"Must agree" is stronger than "good example."** Irenaeus does not merely commend Rome; he says every church *necessarily* agrees with it (*necesse est*), and grounds that necessity in Rome's *potentiorem principalitatem*. A mere convenient example would not carry a *necessity* of agreement. Even granting "preeminence" over "authority," a *preeminent principality* with which all must agree is a real primacy.[^6]

2. **Ignatius's language is leadership language.** The Greek behind "presides" (*prokathēmenē*) is a term of headship, and — tellingly — of the seven letters Ignatius writes, he *corrects and exhorts* Smyrna, the Trallians, and the Magnesians, but issues *no correction* to Rome, only asks that they not impede his martyrdom. Catholics read the asymmetry as deference to Rome's standing.[^7]

3. **Clement already acted beyond Rome's borders.** Clement of Rome's letter intervening in the Corinthian church's internal dispute shows Rome exercising a concern that reaches *outside* its own diocese — an early instance of the very supra-local role Hansen denies.[^8]

4. **La Due's phrase still contains "preeminent authority."** Even the scholar Hansen quotes uses "preeminent authority in doctrinal matters" — so the disagreement narrows to whether *doctrinal* preeminence implies *jurisdictional* supremacy, which the Catholic says is the seed the later definition unfolds.

**Where the tension sits.** Everyone agrees Irenaeus prizes Rome; the dispute is whether "preeminent prominence among apostolic churches" is (Hansen) merely first-among-doctrinal-equals or (Catholic) a genuine primacy that later councils rightly formalized. The scholarly quotations show even Catholic historians read Irenaeus as ranking Rome *within* a class of apostolic churches rather than *above* the whole church by jurisdiction — which is the load Hansen needs.

## Best Quotes

> "It's *potiorem*, which means greater, stronger, more important... *principalitatem*, which means primacy, preeminence, principality. It doesn't say authority. There actually is a word in Latin for authority — *auctoritas* — he doesn't use that word." — Hansen [^1]

> "The context of Irenaeus's argument does not claim that the Roman church is literally unique... rather he argues that the Roman church is the outstanding example of its class." — Robert Eno, quoted by Hansen [^4]

> "Notice he says Peter *and* Paul, not just Peter." — Hansen [^3]
