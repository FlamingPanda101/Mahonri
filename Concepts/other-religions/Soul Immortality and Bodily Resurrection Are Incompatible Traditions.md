# Soul Immortality and Bodily Resurrection Are Incompatible Traditions

## The Claim

Soul-immortality and bodily resurrection are "two fundamentally different and incompatible doctrines," and mainstream Christianity has for nearly 2,000 years affirmed **both simultaneously** without ever reconciling them. The [[Apostles Creed|Apostles' Creed]] and [[Nicene Creed]] formally confess "the resurrection of the body," while the working pastoral theology — the funeral sermon — treats the body as incidental, death as the soul's liberation, and the interval before resurrection as the soul "already dwelling fully in heavenly bliss."[^1] The Swiss Reformed scholar [[Oscar Cullmann]] named this displacement in 1955: Christianity adopted "Plato rather than Paul" on the specific question of what death actually is.[^2]

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. The creed and the funeral contradict each other
"You end up with a creed affirming resurrection of the body sitting alongside a working theology that treats the body as incidental to personal identity, treats death as the soul's liberation from that same body, and then treats the period between death and resurrection as the soul already dwelling fully in heavenly bliss." The two fragments "were layered on top of each other and the tradition simply never required itself to sort out which framework was actually in charge."[^1]
*Evidence rating: Strong · Argument weight: Primary.*

### 2. Cullmann named it in 1955
[[Oscar Cullmann]], a mainstream Swiss Reformed scholar who taught at Basel and the Sorbonne, argued in a 1955 lecture at Harvard Divinity School (published 1958 as *Immortality of the Soul or Resurrection of the Dead?*) that the two are incompatible and that "Christianity adopted Plato rather than Paul on the specific question of what death actually is." His argument "was controversial among his peers exactly because it named the displacement so clearly."[^2]
*Evidence rating: Strong · Argument weight: Primary — a credentialed, non-LDS, mid-century mainstream witness.*

### 3. Paul makes resurrection the center, not a supplement
In [[1 Corinthians 15.14-19|1 Corinthians 15]] Paul spends an entire chapter arguing that "if there's no resurrection, the entire Christian faith collapses" — "if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile." That sentence "only makes theological sense if the resurrection of the body is the central claim, not... a supplement to something that the soul has already achieved on its own."[^3]
*Evidence rating: Strong · Argument weight: Primary.*

### 4. The double-judgment problem
Press the popular framework and it cannot sequence its own events. If grandma's soul is *already* fully in heaven, what does the resurrection add — does she get her body back, and why would she want it? Is she "more in heaven" afterward? And is God going to sentence at the final judgment people who have already been in heaven or hell for thousands of years — did the judgment already happen, or is this a second one? "Most Christians have never been asked these questions and when they are, their actual framework turns out to not have good answers."[^4]
*Evidence rating: Moderate · Argument weight: Primary — the internal-coherence pressure test.*

### 5. Wright's resolution: "life after life after death"
[[N.T. Wright]] — a conservative Anglican bishop and one of the most credentialed living resurrection scholars — reaches the same critique: the New Testament promises "not life after death, but life after life after death." There is a real **intermediate state** (what LDS theology calls the spirit world, what Paul calls being "with Christ"), but it is "not the final destination. It's like the foyer." Death → intermediate state → resurrection → judgment is a sequence you can actually order and explain; the popular framework cannot.[^5]
*Evidence rating: Strong · Argument weight: Primary.*

## Best Quotes

> "Soul immortality and resurrection are two fundamentally different and incompatible doctrines and... Christianity adopted Plato rather than Paul on the specific question of what death actually is." [^2]

> "What the New Testament promises is not life after death, but life after life after death... the intermediate state is not the final destination. It's like the foyer." [^5]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

The strongest reply is that the two are *not* incompatible but sequential, and mature theology has always held them together: the soul consciously survives death (an intermediate state) *and* is later reunited with a raised body — 1 Corinthians 15 itself pairs "asleep in Christ" with the future resurrection, and Philippians 1:23 ("to depart and be with Christ") sits beside 1 Thessalonians 4's bodily rising. On this reading Cullmann overstated a genuine tension into a contradiction, and his thesis was contested precisely because most Christian theologians *do* subordinate the interim state to the resurrection. The video partly concedes this: [[N.T. Wright]] himself resolves the sequence without abandoning an intermediate state, and Catholic and Orthodox theology formally distinguishes particular judgment, intermediate state, resurrection, and final judgment. So the claim is best scoped to the **popular pastoral framework** — "grandma is fully in heaven now, watching over us" — which does treat the interim as final and thereby renders the resurrection redundant. Against that popular framework the incompatibility bites; against a carefully sequenced theology it is a charge of emphasis, not contradiction.
