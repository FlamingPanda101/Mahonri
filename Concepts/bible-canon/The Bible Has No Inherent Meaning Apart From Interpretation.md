# The Bible Has No Inherent Meaning Apart From Interpretation

#type/claim

## The Claim
Dan McClellan's recurring thesis, restated here, is that scripture **"has no independent inherent meaning of its own... the meaning itself is something that we create in negotiation with it, and we frequently overrule it."**[^1] Because no one can access "what God says" apart from their own understanding — there is no chip to swap in "God's understanding" for yours — appealing to "the word of God" over "your own understanding" is incoherent: you have nothing *but* your own understanding to read with. The proof is that communities routinely "overrule" the text using "interpretive lenses and hermeneutic maneuvers," and **slavery is the parade example**: on a plain "lean not on your own understanding" reading, "slavery should be a-okay."[^2]

## Supporting Evidence
1. **No unmediated access to divine meaning.** "We don't have any access to what God says... independent of our own understanding" — the *Big Hero 6* chip that swaps your understanding for God's does not exist, so "the understanding of the Lord" can only ever reach you *through* your understanding.[^3] *(Evidence rating: Strong on the narrow point that all reading is cognitively mediated; Argument weight: Primary.)*
2. **The "meaning is negotiated" thesis.** McClellan: the text "has no independent inherent meaning of its own... something that we create in negotiation with it and we frequently overrule."[^1] *(Evidence rating: Weak as stated — a strong anti-realist claim asserted, not argued; Argument weight: Primary.)*
3. **Texts are demoted to "non grata."** Passages the community now rejects (he gestures at slavery, other "parts... we don't like") are set aside by "rationalizations... interpretive lenses and hermeneutic maneuvers to decide that we're past it."[^1] *(Evidence rating: Moderate — the phenomenon of selective application is undeniable; Argument weight: Secondary.)*
4. **The slavery parade case.** "If we're supposed to lean on the Lord and not unto our own understanding, slavery should be a-okay," and abolitionists were "frequently told to trust in the Lord and lean not on their own understanding."[^2] *(Evidence rating: Moderate — historically plausible, asserted without citation; Argument weight: Secondary.)*
5. **Even revelation must be interpreted.** Beecher's corollary: "even if the spirit told me something, I would still have to lean on my own understanding to know what the heck it means" — there is no understanding-free reception of any message.[^4] *(Evidence rating: Strong on the mediation point; Argument weight: Primary.)*

## Best Quotes

## Apologetic Engagement
**Steelman the hosts.** The *descriptive* core is correct and worth conceding: all reading is mediated by human cognition, there is no interpretation-free access to a text's meaning, and communities demonstrably do apply scripture selectively. The slavery example is the strongest form — no one today defends chattel slavery by quoting "lean not on your own understanding," which shows moral judgment overruling a wooden reading. Beecher's point that even a revelation must still be *understood* is simply true.

**The informed response.** The argument smuggles a sweeping anti-realist conclusion out of a modest premise, and it is self-undermining. (1) **Non-sequitur.** "All understanding is mediated" does not entail "the text has *no* inherent meaning." That a message must be decoded by a mind does not make the message contentless — otherwise no communication, McClellan's included, could succeed. (2) **Self-refutation.** McClellan's own career presupposes determinate meaning: he argues, often fiercely, that particular texts *do* mean specific things their authors intended (that Genesis 1 and 2 are two sources, that John 1:1 does not say Jesus is God, that a mistranslation has *hidden* a text's real sense). One cannot correct a "wrong" reading while holding that texts have no inherent meaning to get right; the very concept of mistranslation requires an original meaning. (3) **The slavery case cuts the other way.** Abolition did not triumph by *overruling* scripture but by appealing to its *deeper* trajectory — the image of God (Gen 1:27), the Exodus liberation, "there is neither bond nor free... in Christ Jesus" (Gal 3:28), Philemon. The historian Tom Holland's cited account is that abolition spread as "Pentecostal fire" precisely from a Spirit-illumined reading of scripture *against* the proof-texts — the text's own moral logic driving reform, not an arbitrary community veto. See [[Abolition Spread as Pentecostal Fire from Spirit-Read Scripture]]. That is meaning *constraining* interpretation, the opposite of "no inherent meaning." (4) **A principled alternative to "overruling" exists.** The choice is not between wooden literalism and free negotiation; a text can have a determinate sense that later revelation *develops* rather than negates ("the letter killeth, but the spirit giveth life," 2 Cor 3:6).

## LDS Relevance
Latter-day Saints have a built-in, non-arbitrary answer to "who gets to overrule the text": **continuing revelation through living prophets** (Articles of Faith 1:9 — "we believe... that He will yet reveal many great and important things"). Where McClellan sees only a community inventing "hermeneutic maneuvers," the LDS model supplies a disciplined mechanism for reading scripture forward in continuity rather than by preference — the 1978 revelation extending the priesthood is developed as revelation resolving a prior practice, not a committee "overruling" a text. This also reframes the slavery example: the moral advance the hosts credit to human conscience overruling scripture, Latter-day Saints locate in the same divine source that gave the scripture, working through revelation and the "light of Christ" given to every person (Moroni 7:16). The LDS reader should still accept the honest half of McClellan's point — proof-texting a single verse as a self-interpreting oracle ignores genre, context, and the whole counsel of scripture.
