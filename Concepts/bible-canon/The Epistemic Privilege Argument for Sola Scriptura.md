# The Epistemic Privilege Argument for Sola Scriptura

## The Claim
The flagship argument of [[In Philosophers Garb - Best Argument for Sola Scriptura|In Philosopher's Garb]]'s case, where the host "put[s] the majority of the weight of my position."[^1] It defends *sola scriptura* — recast as the principle that the Bible gets **veto power** over later tradition (see [[Sola Scriptura as the Bibles Veto Power Over Tradition]]) — **without** any appeal to inspiration, infallibility, or a defined canon. The engine is a purely historical-epistemic claim: **it is rational to prioritize the testimony of sources we know to be *epistemically privileged* over sources that are not.** The New Testament authors were close to Jesus and the apostles; later church councils, sitting centuries downstream, were not — so where the two conflict, the earlier witnesses win.[^2]

## Supporting Evidence

1. **The detective analogy — privilege is ordinary reasoning.** Investigating a fatal home invasion, a detective most wants to interview **Bob**, who was in the house, over **Grace**, who lives thousands of miles away. Bob is "epistemically privileged" — positioned to know the truth about the event — so his testimony outweighs Grace's where they conflict. This is how courts and historians already reason: a source written a few years after an event is prioritized over one written 500 years later.[^3] *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Strong — the intuition is uncontroversial as stated.*

2. **We already apply it to reject late testimony about Jesus.** Christians don't take Muhammad's seventh-century claims about Jesus as overturning the Gospels precisely because he "lived roughly 600 years after the life of Jesus. He wasn't there."[^4] The same logic is simply carried into the scripture-vs-tradition question. *Argument weight: Secondary. Evidence rating: Strong — a live parallel most Christians already grant.*

3. **The New Testament authors are epistemically privileged — even prescinding from inspiration.** Jesus wrote nothing, but his friends and their followers did: Peter's teaching recorded by Mark, the Gospel of John from "the beloved disciple," Paul (who encountered the risen Jesus and was vetted by the other apostles). One can "make no assumptions about them being inspired or infallible" and still say these are excellent sources "because they're so close to the events at hand."[^5] Even disputed **Hebrews** survives the test: every proposed author (Apollos, Priscilla, Timothy, Luke, Barnabas) sits in the Pauline circle, and its early acceptance by privileged readers counts in its favor.[^6] *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Moderate — proximity establishes historical value, though "privileged about the events" does not by itself establish "privileged about theology."*

4. **Later councils lack the privilege — and there is no evidence the Spirit supplies it.** The fathers of the **seventh ecumenical council** (Nicaea II, 787) lived ~750 years after Christ, were not eyewitnesses, and had no access to eyewitnesses; when a theological question arose "there is no evidence that they're in an epistemically privileged position." The Catholic/Orthodox reply — the Holy Spirit guides them into truth — has "simply no evidence biblical or otherwise" behind it, whereas the privilege of the apostolic authors is well evidenced.[^7] *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Moderate — turns on an absence-of-evidence claim the other side contests directly (see counter).*

5. **The "infallible only in council" move is ad hoc gerrymandering.** Individually the fathers taught error: [[Augustine]] on unbaptized infants, several fathers on the inferiority of women, Ambrose entertaining Arian interpolation of texts. To say the Spirit lets all this pass yet becomes protective only when the same fallible men gather in a room is "clearly pretty improbable and simply implausible… pretty contrived and ad hoc."[^8] *Argument weight: Secondary. Evidence rating: Moderate — rhetorically strong; the fathers' errors are real, though the conciliar claim is about protection in defined dogmatic acts, not personal impeccability.*

6. **The burden of proof falls on the objector.** The only way to defeat the argument is to show the later teaching **goes back to the apostles** or that the later teachers "had the same epistemic privilege as the apostles." That, not proof-texting, "is the pressure point" the Protestant should press.[^9] Defensively, the standard ongoing-authority proof texts miss: **John 16:13** ("guide you into all truth") and **Matthew 28:20** ("I am with you always") are addressed to *the disciples*, and say nothing about officeholders 500 years later — so they don't transfer the privilege forward.[^10] *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Moderate.*

7. **Trusting tradition on authorship does not commit you to trusting it on everything (the tu quoque disarmed).** The objection "you rely on church tradition to know who wrote the Gospels, so be consistent and accept its doctrine too" fails because epistemic privilege is **topic-specific**. You trust a doctor's testimony about medicine but not a stranger's at a bus stop; likewise [[Papias]] and [[Justin Martyr]] are privileged on the historical question of *who wrote* the documents without being privileged on every doctrine. "Just because I think a person has epistemic privilege about one topic doesn't mean I think they have epistemic privilege about every topic."[^11] *Argument weight: Secondary. Evidence rating: Moderate — a real disanalogy is pressed in the counter below.*

## Best Quotes
> "It is rationally justified to prioritize testimony from people who we know to be epistemically privileged over the testimony of people who don't share the same degree of epistemic privilege."[^12]

> "Even if I make no assumptions about inspiration or what the biblical canon is or isn't, I'm justified in prioritizing the testimony of the New Testament authors over that of later writers… because they are in a position which is epistemically privileged while the guys writing 750 years later aren't."[^2]

> "The burden of proof is, I think, on our Catholic and Orthodox friends to show that their proposed teachings either go back to the apostles or that the teachers had the same epistemic privilege as the apostles."[^9]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

**Catholic / Orthodox response — the Spirit-guided church *is* the promised privilege.** The argument's load-bearing premise is that "there is no evidence" the Holy Spirit secures the later teaching church. But that is precisely what the New Testament is read to assert: the church is "the pillar and ground of the truth" ([[1 Timothy 3.15|1 Timothy 3:15]]), the Spirit is promised to guide (John 16:13), Christ is present "to the end of the age" (Matthew 28:20), and apostolic succession is the ordinary channel by which apostolic teaching is transmitted, not invented. See [[The Church Is the Pillar of Truth and Scripture Is Its Byproduct]], [[A Living Magisterium Settles What Sola Scriptura Cannot]], and [[The Early Church Did Not Operate Under Sola Scriptura]] (the ante-Nicene church shows no "Scripture alone" method). The host's own reading of these texts as addressed only to the first disciples is contestable exegesis, not a neutral datum.

**The authorship concession re-opens the canon problem.** The host admits he needs church tradition — [[Papias]], [[Justin Martyr]], the fathers — to know *who wrote* the Gospels and therefore *which* documents are apostolic. But that is the whole game: the criterion that sorts "epistemically privileged" from "unprivileged" sources is itself supplied by the later church whose authority he is otherwise discounting. To decide the New Testament is privileged, he has already trusted post-apostolic tradition to identify it. See [[Knowing Scripture Is the Word of God Requires an Authority Outside Scripture]] and [[The Three Unjustified Protestant Canon Presuppositions]]. The "topic-specific privilege" reply is double-edged: if Papias's proximity certifies his memory of *who wrote Mark*, the same proximity certifies the early fathers' near-unanimous memory of *what the apostles taught and practiced* (episcopacy, the Eucharist, baptism) — doctrines the host wants to reject as late. He cannot cordon the fathers' historical reliability off from their doctrinal reporting when both rest on the same closeness to the events.

**LDS response — epistemic privilege argues for the Restoration, not for a closed book.** If proximity to revelation is what confers authority, then the Latter-day Saint agrees entirely — and draws the opposite conclusion. The Protestant reader in 2026 is himself "Grace on the other side of the world," two millennia and countless interpretive filters removed from the events, adjudicating with no privileged *living* witness. Preserving a distant echo in a fallible, humanly transmitted text is a weaker remedy for the distance than *re-opening the channel*: living apostles and prophets who restore epistemic privilege rather than merely reading about it. See [[The Restoration Answers an Authority Problem Scholarship Cannot Solve]] and [[God Himself Not a Book Is the Ultimate Authority]]. On this view the argument proves too little for the Protestant: it establishes that the New Testament is a superior historical *source*, not that it is the *sole* or *sufficient* rule (a "veto power" that grants tradition real if lesser weight is not the Reformation's *sola*; see [[Sola Scriptura as the Bibles Veto Power Over Tradition]]).
