# Sola Scriptura as the Bible's Veto Power Over Tradition

## The Claim
[[In Philosophers Garb - Best Argument for Sola Scriptura|In Philosopher's Garb]] argues most *sola scriptura* debates fail because the definition is bad, and proposes a deliberately **modest** replacement: *sola scriptura* is the methodological principle that, "when doing theology, we should give the Bible **veto power** over later traditions and teachings" — the Bible carries more weight than later church teaching.[^1] The strategic payoff is that this thin definition **sidesteps** the two most popular Catholic/Orthodox objections — the self-refutation charge and the canon problem — because it never asserts the premises those objections attack.[^2]

## Supporting Evidence

1. **The definition is minimal by design.** It says nothing about *how much* authority tradition retains (Protestants may disagree), nothing about whether the Bible is inspired, infallible, or inerrant, and it does not deny that God may have spoken infallibly to someone in a way not recorded in the text. "It's simply a methodological principle that we should employ when reasoning about theology."[^3] *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Strong as a description of his own position.*

2. **It sidesteps the self-refutation objection.** The classic Catholic syllogism ([[Jimmy Akin]] vs [[James White]]): (1) *sola scriptura* holds all teachings must be taught in Scripture; (2) *sola scriptura* is a teaching; (3) it is not taught in Scripture; (4) therefore it is self-refuting. The host simply denies premise 1 — "I never said that all teachings must be taught in scripture" — and can grant that some teachings come from reason or tradition, dissolving the objection. He notes [[Gavin Ortlund]] makes the same move against [[Trent Horn]].[^4] *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Moderate — valid against premise 1, though see counter on what it costs.*

3. **It sidesteps the canon problem.** The gotcha "you say the canon is infallibly inspired, but you need the Catholic Church to know *what's in* the canon" only bites a definition built on a special inspired-and-infallible category. Since his argument rests on **epistemic privilege** (see [[The Epistemic Privilege Argument for Sola Scriptura]]), not on canon-inspiration, he claims he "can completely set aside debates about the canon and still be fine" — Mark and John outweigh a 7th-century council on proximity grounds whether or not the canon's boundary is settled.[^5] *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Moderate — contested by the counter below.*

4. **The label is negotiable; the substance is the point.** Told "that's not really *sola scriptura*," the host shrugs: call it "*sola scriptura* 2.0" if you like — "a theme of Protestant theology is this idea that the church is always reforming," so reforming the definition is in-bounds. The challenge is to engage the *arguments*, not the terminology.[^6] *Argument weight: Secondary. Evidence rating: n/a (a framing move).*

## Best Quotes
> "When doing theology, we should give the Bible veto power over later traditions and teachings… This definition, to put it simply, is very modest."[^1]

> "Notice that in my definition of solo scriptura, I never said that all teachings must be taught in scripture… I can just grant that some teachings come from reason or maybe other church tradition. I can just easily sidestep this."[^4]

> "My argument wasn't that scripture is a special set of inspired and infallible texts… nothing about that argument requires me to draw a firm boundary line around some books and deem them infallible while everything else isn't. I can completely set aside debates about the canon and still be fine."[^5]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

**"This wins by retreat — it is no longer *sola scriptura*."** The historic doctrine (Westminster; the magisterial Reformers) is specifically that Scripture is the church's **sole infallible rule of faith**. A principle that merely gives the Bible *more weight* while granting later tradition real (if lesser) authority is closer to *prima scriptura* or the Anglican "three-legged stool" than to the Reformation's *sola*. [[Gavin Ortlund]]'s own careful version still keeps "only infallible *rule*" as its core (see [[The 80 Percent Rule - Sola Scriptura Is Caricatured Into the Excluded Middle]]); dropping infallibility and canon concedes exactly the ground the debate is fought over. On the Catholic side, once tradition has genuine weight the question becomes *who adjudicates* Bible-vs-tradition tensions — which reintroduces the need the magisterium fills ([[A Living Magisterium Settles What Sola Scriptura Cannot]]).

**The canon problem does not actually disappear.** To grant "the Bible" veto power you must first know *which documents* are "the Bible" carrying that authority — the veto presupposes a **bounded, identified collection**. The host concedes he relies on post-apostolic tradition (Papias, the fathers) to identify which writings are apostolic (see [[The Epistemic Privilege Argument for Sola Scriptura]]), so the authority that fixes the privileged set is again the later church. The canon question is logically prior to any canon-based veto — see [[The Canon Question Is Logically Prior to Canon Authority]] and [[The Three Unjustified Protestant Canon Presuppositions]]. Renaming the collection "the epistemically privileged documents" does not tell you where its edges are.

**The modest principle is itself an extra-biblical human posit.** By the host's own admission the veto rule is "a methodological principle" derived from reason, not from Scripture and not infallible. But then it has no more intrinsic authority than the tradition it presumes to judge: a reasoned human standard sitting *over* the church's teaching. That is the [[Conscience Is the Protestants True Ultimate Authority|functional-conscience]] critique — the real final court is the individual's philosophical judgment about what counts as "privileged," not the text.

**LDS note — a "modest" sola scriptura is hospitable to continuing revelation.** Because the definition explicitly leaves open that "God has spoken to some person directly and infallibly in a way that's not recorded," it does not close the canon — it only ranks sources. Latter-day Saints agree the Bible carries great weight; the live dispute is the *closed, sufficient* canon, which this modesty quietly abandons. If uninspired distance is the problem, the answer is renewed revelation, not a thinner rule (see [[God Himself Not a Book Is the Ultimate Authority]]).
