# Jesus Treated Scripture as Authoritative Without Calling It the Word of God

#type/claim

## The Claim
[[Dan McClellan]] concedes the apologist's premise that Jesus treated the scriptures as true and authoritative — but insists this is a **category distinct** from calling them "the word of God," a label "which Jesus never identifies the scriptures as."[^1] When the pastor cites Jesus' "Your word is truth" ([[John 17.17|John 17:17]]) and "Scripture cannot be broken" ([[John 10.33-35|John 10:35]]) as if they equate scripture with God's word, McClellan replies that neither verse does so: Jesus "does not say that that word is the scriptures," and "cannot be broken" is a statement of scripture's *reliability*, not an identification of the text *as* God's word.[^1] The underlying lexical claim: across the Bible "word of God" refers to Jesus or to God's message "independent of the medium," most often orally, "but never specifically to the textual transmission itself."[^2]

## Supporting Evidence
1. **"Word of God" never denotes the text itself.** The phrase "is always used either to refer to Jesus or to the message that God shares independent of the medium... but never specifically to the textual transmission itself."[^2] This is the empirical backbone of the whole argument. *(Evidence rating: Strong on the distributional point — it converges with the independently-argued [[No Verse Calls a Human Writing the Word of God]]; Argument weight: Primary.)*
2. **"Authoritative/true" ≠ "God's word."** Treating scripture as true and authoritative "is distinct from treating it as God's word, which Jesus never identifies the scriptures as."[^1] The two properties can come apart: a reliable text need not be ontologically "the direct speech of God." *(Evidence rating: Moderate — a real conceptual distinction, though it turns on how strictly one reads Jesus' usage; Argument weight: Primary.)*
3. **[[John 17.17|John 17:17]] — "your word is truth."** In Jesus' high-priestly prayer, "he does not say that that word (λόγος) is the scriptures."[^1] The referent is God's communicated truth/message, not a table of contents. *(Evidence rating: Moderate; Argument weight: Secondary.)*
4. **[[John 10.33-35|John 10:35]] — "scripture cannot be broken."** That scripture "cannot be broken" (οὐ δύναται λυθῆναι) asserts it cannot be nullified — "which is also not identifying the scriptures as God's word."[^1] Notably the same verse says "the word of God came" *to* the judges of Psalm 82 — a spoken oracle, not the scroll. *(Evidence rating: Moderate; Argument weight: Secondary.)*
5. **The inference the pastor needs is unproven.** If no verse — not even from Jesus — squarely calls the written text "the word of God," then the inerrantist syllogism ("Scripture is God's direct speech; God cannot err; therefore Scripture cannot err") rests on an unproven first premise. See [[The Word of God Fallacy - The Load-Bearing Premise Is Unproven]]. *(Evidence rating: Strong as a logical point; Argument weight: Primary.)*

## Apologetic Engagement
**Steelman McClellan.** The lexical observation is sound and is not idiosyncratic — the same "word of God never denotes a human writing" data is argued at length by the Catholic apologist [[Trent Horn]] (see [[No Verse Calls a Human Writing the Word of God]]), and the point that the inerrantist argument smuggles in its key premise is a live one (see [[The Word of God Fallacy - The Load-Bearing Premise Is Unproven]]). "Your word is truth" and "scripture cannot be broken" are indeed statements about God's message and about scripture's reliability; neither is a formula equating the physical text with divine speech. Reliability and "being the word of God" are conceptually separable, and McClellan is right that inerrantists often slide from one to the other without argument.

**The informed response.** (1) *Functional identification is still identification.* Jesus routinely settles disputes with "it is written" and treats a written text's assertion as God's own binding word (Matt 4:4–10; Matt 19:4–5, where he attributes the *narrator's* words of Genesis 2:24 to "he who made them... said"). One need not find the exact string "the scriptures are the word of God" to establish that Jesus treated written scripture as carrying God's authority — an argument from the *absence* of a precise phrase is weak where the *function* is pervasive. (2) *John 10:35 juxtaposes the two.* The clause pairs "the word of God came" with "the scripture cannot be broken" in a single breath, using the unbreakability of *the writing* to secure the force of *the word* — the text at minimum treats them as inseparable, not as strangers. (3) *The distinction proves less than advertised.* Granting that "word of God" primarily names God's message rather than ink on a page does not show the message is *absent* from or *unreliable* in the text; it shows the text is the message's medium — which is all a fallibilist doctrine of scripture needs. Here McClellan's fallibility point is congenial rather than threatening to the Latter-day Saint (below): one can deny wooden inerrancy without denying that God speaks through scripture.

## LDS Relevance
The Latter-day Saint position already grants McClellan's wedge between the *message* and the *medium*. The eighth Article of Faith affirms the Bible as the word of God "**as far as it is translated correctly**" — an explicitly *fallibilist* view of the text that never rested inerrancy on the medium in the first place. Where inerrantist Protestantism must locate God's word *in the text itself* (and so is vulnerable to McClellan's "the text is never called God's word" thrust), LDS theology locates the "word of God" primarily in **living communication** — Christ the Word ([[John 1.1|John 1:1]]), the spoken oracles of prophets, and continuing revelation — with scripture as its inspired but humanly-mediated record (see [[Translated Correctly Is Not an Escape Hatch but Vindicated Honesty]]). McClellan's observation that "word of God" denotes an ongoing divine message "independent of the medium" maps almost exactly onto the Restoration claim that revelation did not cease with a closed canon. The LDS reader can therefore accept the honest half of the point — the written text is not a self-identifying, self-interpreting oracle — while still confessing scripture as scripture and answering the "who decides?" question with a named, accountable authority (living prophets), which is precisely what [[Knowing Scripture Is the Word of God Requires an Authority Outside Scripture]] presses.
