# The Make Her a Man Saying Is a Late Insertion Into Thomas

## The Claim
The Gospel of Thomas is routinely dismissed over its bizarre closing saying (logion 114): Peter says "get Mary out of here, she doesn't belong," and Jesus replies, "I will make her a man… for every woman who makes herself male will enter the kingdom of heaven." [[Jonah Barnes]] argues this is a **single, late scribal insertion** — added a century or two after the sayings core — and that throwing out the whole gospel because of it is a double standard critics never apply to the corruptions they keep in the canonical text. [^1]

## Supporting Evidence

1. **It sits in the narrative frame, not the old core.** *Argument weight: Primary.* The awkward saying is the very last line, part of the beginning-and-end "sandwich bread" that reads differently from the middle (see [[The Gospel of Thomas Has an Early Sayings Core]]). "Scholars who've looked at this… say the core is written very differently than the sandwiched bread," and adding a favorite "flavor of the month" to the end of a text is exactly how such corruption happens. [^2]
2. **It is one attestation, not a pattern.** *Argument weight: Secondary.* "It's literally the last one that says something kind of funky… It's only one attestation. Like, calm down." A lone anomalous saying does not discredit 113 others. [^3]
3. **Apostasy corrupted non-canonical texts too.** *Argument weight: Primary.* Barnes frames it with the Restoration's own model: "just like there was an apostasy that affected the plain and precious truths of the books in our canon, there was an apostasy that affected the non-canonized ones, too." A scribal insertion in Thomas is the same phenomenon Latter-day Saints already attribute to the biblical text (cf. [[The Great Forsaken - A Pre-Christian Apostasy of the Scribes]]). [^1]
4. **A possible innocent reading: an ancient marriage idiom.** *Argument weight: Tertiary.* Barnes notes that in some ancient languages a woman who marries is said to be "manned" (e.g. Old Slavonic *zamuzh*), so "I will make her a man" may be an idiom for claiming/marrying, or an honorific — not the transphobic absurdity modern readers hear. He holds this loosely. [^4]
5. **Critics keep worse and don't bin the book.** *Argument weight: Primary.* The same scholars who see corruption in Matthew, in the letters of John, or in the disputed longer ending of Mark ([[Mark 16.9-20|Mark 16:9-20]]) "don't rip that out." Consistency requires treating one odd saying in Thomas the same way. [^5]

## Best Quotes
> "So there was a little corruption in that — big whoop. I'm not going to dismiss the entire thing… They see a small corruption in Matthew, they see it in the letters of John, and they go, 'Well, you can't throw out everything.'" [^5]

> "Just like there was an apostasy that affected the plain and precious truths of the books in our canon, there was an apostasy that affected the non-canonized ones, too." [^1]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
- **The manuscript evidence does not show an insertion.** Logion 114 is present in the complete Coptic Nag Hammadi copy, and there is no textual witness *lacking* it; "it was added later" is a stylistic conjecture, not a documented variant. The honest form of the claim is *it may be secondary*, not *it demonstrably is*.
- **Evangelical critics have a principled reason to weight it.** [[Wes Huff]] and others dismiss Thomas partly because its distinctive material (esoteric "secret sayings," logion 114) diverges theologically from the apostolic gospels — a content criterion, not mere squeamishness. The steelman is that logion 114 is a *symptom* of Thomas's overall character, not an isolated blemish.
- **The double-standard charge can rebound.** If corruption in a text does not discredit it, that principle protects the *canonical* gospels at least as much as Thomas — and Latter-day Saint arguments elsewhere lean hard on biblical corruption. The panel wants corruption to be fatal for the Bible's *sufficiency* but harmless for Thomas's *credibility*; the asymmetry needs justifying.
- **"One attestation" understates it.** Thomas's frame also carries the "secret/hidden words" prologue and other distinctives; treating only logion 114 as late, while treating the equally framed prologue as primitive evidence for the LDS reading, is selective.
