# Biblical Precedent for Testing Teaching Against Scripture

## The Claim
The host's second argument: the New Testament itself models the very method *sola scriptura* prescribes — taking any new teaching, even an apostle's, and holding it up to the previously received deposit as the benchmark.[^1] If the biblical writers command and commend "using what we have already been given… as the litmus test for all other teachings," then the methodological principle has scriptural warrant.

## Supporting Evidence

1. **Galatians 1:6-9 — even an apostle or angel is subject to the deposit.** Paul, a genuine apostle personally commissioned by Christ, tells the Galatians that if he "or an angel from heaven" preaches a gospel contrary to the one they already received, "let him be accursed" ([[Galatians 1.8|Galatians 1:8]]). He wants the church to *compare* new teaching against the prior apostolic deposit and reject the new if it conflicts — "exactly what sola scriptura is all about." Paul has no notion that he, or future leaders, are immune from teaching error.[^2] *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Moderate — the verse commands testing, but against *which* standard is contested (see counter).*

2. **Galatians 2:11-14 — Paul rebukes Peter to his face.** Peter, "arguably the rock" Catholics say Christ built the church on, acted hypocritically at Antioch and led others astray, so Paul "opposed him to his face" ([[Galatians 2.14|Galatians 2:11-14]]). If the chief apostle could err and needed public correction, "why would anybody take seriously the idea that later church leaders who don't hold a candle to Peter are infallible or above question?"[^3] *Argument weight: Secondary. Evidence rating: Moderate — establishes correctability; the Catholic conduct-vs-doctrine distinction is pressed below.*

3. **Acts 17:10-11 — the Bereans are praised for the practice.** The Bereans were "more noble" because "they received the word with all eagerness, examining the scriptures daily to see if these things were so" ([[Acts 17.10-11|Acts 17:11]]). They tested Paul's *new* preaching against the scriptures they already trusted, and Luke *praises* them for it — so the practice the host defends is the one Scripture calls noble.[^4] *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Moderate.*

4. **The Berean objection is answered by the grammar (and Keener).** Catholics respond that the Bereans were noble for *open-mindedness* (hearing Paul out), not for scripture-checking. But there is no basis to split Acts 17:11: Luke says they were noble and then lists *two* things — they listened eagerly *and* they examined the scriptures. [[Craig Keener]]'s *Bible Background Commentary*: "Judaism regarded as noble those who checked everything against the scriptures and diligently listened to teachers." It is a packaged concept, not one-or-the-other.[^5] *Argument weight: Secondary. Evidence rating: Moderate — a fair grammatical reading, though it does not by itself specify the canon.*

5. **1 Thessalonians 5:20-21 — test prophecy against the privileged standard.** "Do not despise prophecies, but test everything; hold fast what is good." A claimed word from the Holy Spirit is to be *tested* — and the test is conducted against what already has epistemic privilege (see [[The Epistemic Privilege Argument for Sola Scriptura]]).[^6] *Argument weight: Secondary. Evidence rating: Moderate.*

## Best Quotes
> "This idea of taking any new teaching and holding it up to the apostolic deposit we've already been given is exactly what sola scriptura is all about. It's using what we have already been given… as the benchmark, the litmus test for all other teachings."[^2]

> "If even the blessed Apostle Peter made mistakes and needed to be rebuked, why in the world would anybody take seriously the idea that later church leaders who don't hold a candle to Peter are infallible or above question?"[^3]

> "These Jews were more noble… examining the scriptures daily to see if these things were so… What these Jews were doing is exactly what my definition of sola scriptura suggests."[^4]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

**The precedent presupposes an already-received deposit — which is what tradition is.** Each text tells believers to test new claims against a standard they *already possess by prior reception*. The Bereans checked Paul against the **Old Testament**, a body of writings already recognized as authoritative — by tradition — before any New Testament existed. So the verses argue for measuring novelty against the *prior apostolic deposit*, not for a *closed 66-book canon as the sole rule*; the deposit in the apostolic age plainly included oral apostolic teaching the host himself grants is authoritative. See the counter-reading built into [[Acts 17.10-11|Acts 17:11]] and the begging-the-question analysis in [[Galatians 1-8 Begs the Question - The Swiss Army Verse]]: citing Galatians 1:8 presupposes you already possess the correct gospel-standard, which is the contested point.

**These texts are equally at home in the Catholic/Orthodox model.** Catholics and Orthodox *also* test alleged private revelations and doctrinal novelties against the apostolic deposit — Scripture *and* Tradition together. "Test everything against the deposit" is common ground; whether the deposit is **Scripture alone** is the disputed claim these verses do not settle. They therefore under-determine *sola scriptura*.

**Paul's rebuke of Peter is about conduct, not conciliar teaching.** Galatians 2 concerns Peter's *hypocrisy at table*, not a doctrinal definition. Catholics freely grant that popes and bishops sin personally and can be rebuked, while distinguishing that from a council's or *ex cathedra* dogmatic act — so the rebuke leaves the infallibility claim (which is narrow and conditional) untouched.

**LDS response — the same precedent licenses testing creedal Christianity against living revelation.** If Scripture models testing every teaching — even an apostle's — against the received revelation, the Latter-day Saint applies it consistently: post-apostolic creeds and traditions are themselves to be tested, and the test includes fresh revelation and personal confirmation (Moroni's promise), not a distant closed text alone. Galatians 1:8's "even an angel" is not a bar against Moroni but a call to test the message — see [[Scripture as the Test of Prophets and Revelation]] and [[Galatians 1-8 Begs the Question - The Swiss Army Verse]].
