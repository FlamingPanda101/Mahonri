# What the Deuteronomists Cut Is What the Creeds Replaced

#type/claim

## The Claim
[[Alex Arnold]] argues that two events usually studied in separate academic silos are really **one event seen from two angles**. In the **7th century BC** the Deuteronomistic scribes *removed* specific theological content from the Hebrew record; in the **4th century AD** the creedal councils *installed* Greek philosophical categories to fill the interpretive space that exact removal had opened. "What got cut from the text is almost exactly what got replaced by the creeds."[^1] The distinctive Book of Mormon claim is not the generic "the Bible has errors" but the *specific* one in [[1 Nephi 13.26-29|1 Nephi 13]] — a **deliberate removal**, by an **identifiable institution**, producing **specific doctrinal consequences** — which Arnold frames as "a falsifiable three-part claim with a mechanism, an actor, and a consequence,"[^2] published in **1830**, before either half of the process had been confirmed by scholarship.[^1]

## Supporting Evidence
1. **The edit is documented and mainstream.** [[Julius Wellhausen]] (1878), [[Martin Noth]] (1943), and [[Frank Moore Cross]] at Harvard establish that Deuteronomy–Kings were substantially reshaped by a school of scribes working during and after [[Josiah]]'s reign, with a specific agenda: centralized worship, Yahweh-only monotheism, and the delegitimization of older traditions.[^3] *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Strong (the Deuteronomistic redaction is consensus; the "deliberate deletion of a specific doctrine" gloss is Arnold's synthesis).*
2. **The textual fingerprint of the edit.** [[Michael Heiser]] (*The Unseen Realm*; PhD Hebrew Bible, Wisconsin; a Protestant "no lover of LDS theology") shows the older Hebrew cosmos has Yahweh presiding over the *bene elohim*, a **divine council** running through Psalm 82, Job 1, and Genesis 6, "systematically suppressed in the standardization of the text."[^4] The clearest single case is [[Deuteronomy 32.8-9|Deuteronomy 32:8]]: the Masoretic "sons of Israel" versus the Dead Sea Scrolls' older "**sons of God**" — a variant also documented by [[Emanuel Tov]], the Orthodox Jewish scholar who ran the DSS publication project.[^5] *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Strong on the variant.*
3. **The same mechanism in the New Testament.** The [[1 John 5.7|Comma Johanneum]] — the most explicitly Trinitarian verse in the NT — appears in no Greek manuscript before the 15th century; [[Erasmus]] refused it until handed the (widely-judged-fabricated) Codex Montfortianus. "Same mechanism, different century": an institution shaping a text to support a doctrine it needed textual support for. See [[Erasmus Was Handed a Fabricated Manuscript to Restore the Comma]].[^6] *Argument weight: Secondary. Evidence rating: Strong (the Comma's lateness is conceded across the spectrum).*
4. **What was cut is a specific list.** [[Margaret Barker]] (former president, Society for Old Testament Study), [[Mark Smith]] (Princeton), and [[William Dever]] (Arizona) reconstruct a pre-reform religion containing a **second divine figure** alongside Yahweh (Son of God / Angel of the Lord / Melchizedek), **deification** through priestly ritual, the **Asherah** physically present in the temple, and the **divine council**. Dever's excavations at Kuntillet Ajrud found storage jars (~800 BC) inscribed "Yahweh and his Asherah," ~180 years before Josiah.[^7] [[Alan Segal]]'s *Two Powers in Heaven* independently shows binitarianism was a live option in Judaism *before* Christianity.[^8] See [[No Pre-Josiah Text Vilifies Asherah]] and [[The Deuteronomists Edited Out the Pre-Existent Divine Messiah]]. *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Moderate–Strong on the reform; the full LDS-endowment correspondence is Barker's non-consensus extension.*
5. **What was installed is a specific list — and it is Greek, not Hebrew.** [[Jaroslav Pelikan]] (Yale) traces the move from biblical to Greek philosophical categories as "a development, not a recovery"; [[Adolf von Harnack]] named it the **Hellenization of Christianity** — impassibility, divine simplicity, incorporeality — sourced from [[Plato]]'s *Timaeus*, the Stoics, and the middle Platonists, applied to the Hebrew God by [[Justin Martyr]], [[Clement of Alexandria]], and [[Origen]], and codified at [[Council of Nicaea|Nicaea]] (325) in the non-biblical term *homoousios*.[^9] [[Gerhard May]] (1978) dates *creatio ex nihilo* to a 2nd-century response to Gnostic/Platonic cosmology — not a reading of [[Genesis 1.1|Genesis 1:1]] (whose *bara* the Brown-Driver-Briggs lexicon does not define as creation from nothing, with a formless void already present in [[Genesis 1.2|Gen 1:2]]).[^10] See [[Creation Ex Nihilo Originated Late 2nd Century CE]] and [[The Creedal God Is the Platonic One Not the God of the Bible]]. *Argument weight: Primary. Evidence rating: Strong that the categories are Greek and late.*
6. **The convergence is independent and post-dates 1830.** A German Protestant on Deuteronomistic editing (1878), an Orthodox Jewish manuscript scholar on the Scrolls, and American archaeologists in the Sinai were "not collaborating toward a shared conclusion"; "almost none of them are LDS and a significant number aren't even Christian."[^2] Every corroborating strand — Wellhausen 1878, Harnack in the 1880s–90s, the Scrolls in 1947, May in 1978, Barker across the later 20th century — arrived *after* Joseph Smith.[^11] *Argument weight: Primary (this is the load-bearing move). Evidence rating: the dates are solid; the inference to prophetic foreknowledge is the faith-adjacent step Arnold flags as such.*

## Best Quotes
> "The Deuteronomistic scribes in the 7th century BC removed specific theological content from the Hebrew record, and then the creedal councils of the 4th century AD installed Greek philosophical categories to fill the space that exact removal created. What got cut from the text is almost exactly what got replaced by the creeds. And Joseph Smith just happened to describe both sides of that event in 1830 before either of those things had been confirmed by scholarship."[^1]

> "Not 'the Bible has errors,' but a specific institutional process removed specific content producing specific wrong conclusions in anyone who read the result."[^2]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
- **"You're cherry-picking scholars."** The honest reply is to distinguish framework from detail: the documentary hypothesis, the Deuteronomistic-history thesis (Noth 1943, refined by Cross), the manuscript diversity of the Scrolls (Tov), and the Hellenization of doctrine (Harnack, Pelikan) are each *consensus at the level of whether they occurred*; only the details (how many Deuteronomistic editions, exactly which passages) are debated.[^12] The move fails only if a critic can show the *framework* is disputed, which it is not.
- **"Barker is fringe."** Conceded in part: Barker's full first-temple Christology and her LDS-endowment correspondence are *not* mainstream. But her *basic* claim — that the reform suppressed earlier theological content and that first-temple religion differed substantially from what followed — is exactly what Noth, Cross, and Smith established from other angles; she pushes an accepted framework further than most colleagues.[^13] The question is whether the correspondences she identifies "require explanation," not whether every conclusion is accepted.
- **"The councils were guided by the Holy Spirit."** Answered separately at [[The Robber Council Refutes Holy-Spirit-Guided Councils]]: two councils (Ephesus 449, Chalcedon 451) both claimed the Spirit and reached opposite verdicts, so Spirit-guidance functions as *post-hoc ratification* of the winning side, not a mechanism operating *before* the decision.[^14]
- **"Anthropomorphism."** The claim that the walking, wrestling, face-to-face God is "just anthropomorphism" is a coherent position, but it "requires overriding the actual plain reading of the text with a commitment the text itself never makes."[^15]
- **The load-bearing concession.** Arnold grants the historical case "doesn't prove the restoration"; whether Joseph Smith was the *instrument* of recovery "is a different question that requires faith." What it establishes is narrower and stronger: "the loss and the replacement are historical fact, not faith."[^16] Critics can accept every historical premise without conceding LDS truth-claims — the argument's honesty is that it says so.
