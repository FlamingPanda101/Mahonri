# The Gospel of Thomas Has an Early Sayings Core

## The Claim
The Gospel of Thomas is not a narrative gospel but a **"sayings gospel"** — 114 bare *logia* ("Jesus said…"). Only its opening and closing lines are narrative "sandwich bread"; the sayings in the middle read as far older than the 3rd-century framing scholars assign to the whole. [[Jonah Barnes]] argues the honest conclusion is "we actually have no clue when this was dated" for the core — and that biblical scholars resist an early core precisely because it "shakes the infallibility of two institutions": the Bible and the academy. [^1]

## Supporting Evidence

1. **Thomas is formally different from the four gospels.** *Argument weight: Primary.* Mark, Matthew, Luke, and John are narrative — birth, ministry, cross, resurrection, in chronological order. Thomas is "just a sayings gospel… like a list of proverbs," 114 of them in the Nag Hammadi copy, strung "Jesus said this, Jesus said this." [^2]
2. **Only the frame is narrative; the middle is not.** *Argument weight: Primary.* "Notice the very beginning and the very end are narrative… That's just the beginning and just the end. And in the middle is not that." A composite text can carry an old core inside a later frame. [^3]
3. **Scholars date the frame and the core differently.** *Argument weight: Primary.* Barnes reports that many scholars — "unpopularly" — judge "the core of Thomas is far, far older than people give it credit for": the "sandwich bread" may look 3rd-century, "but the middle of it looks like it's from something far, far older than that." [^1]
4. **An old core dissolves the neat heresy timeline.** *Argument weight: Secondary.* If sayings with "secret"/esoteric flavor predate the canonical gospels, the tidy story that "the Gnostics came a hundred years later and messed things up" no longer holds — the ideas were already in circulation. [^4]

## Best Quotes
> "So many scholars believe, unpopularly, they believe that the core of Thomas is far, far older than people give it credit for." [^1]

> "Whenever Bible scholars find something that talks about weird stuff, they give it a special name… Gnostic… aka heretical. They say, 'Oh, this doesn't fit with our paradigm, therefore it must be Gnostic.'" [^5]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
- **"Sayings gospel" does not entail "early."** Thomas being a *genre* cousin of the hypothetical Q source is a literary observation, not a date. A collection can be assembled late out of both old and new material — which is exactly the composite picture Barnes concedes.
- **The mainstream reads the dependence the opposite way.** Text critics who have worked the parallels closely (Goodacre, Gathercole) argue Thomas reflects the *redactional* wording of Matthew and Luke — i.e. it presupposes the finished gospels — pushing its composition into the early-to-mid 2nd century, not before them. An old "core" is asserted here but not demonstrated from the transcript.
- **"They just call it Gnostic to dismiss it" cuts both ways.** The label "Gnostic" is contested and often too blunt (Thomas lacks a full demiurge cosmology) — but the panel's mirror-image move, treating every esoteric saying as primitive and every awkward one as a "late insertion" (see [[The Make Her a Man Saying Is a Late Insertion Into Thomas]]), is the same selective dating it complains about.
- **The candid part is genuinely candid.** The strongest form of the claim is modest and defensible: *we do not securely know when the core sayings originated.* That agnosticism, not "older than the gospels," is what the evidence in the video actually supports.
