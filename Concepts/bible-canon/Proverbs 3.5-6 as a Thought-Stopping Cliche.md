# Proverbs 3.5-6 as a Thought-Stopping Cliche

#type/claim

## The Claim
Dan McClellan and Dan Beecher of *Data Over Dogma* argue that **[[Proverbs 3.5-6|Proverbs 3:5-6]]** ("Trust in the LORD... lean not unto thine own understanding") is routinely weaponized as a **thought-stopping cliché** — pulled out as a "trump card" to forbid questioning and end a conversation, functionally saying "I'm right and you're wrong, the end." A listener reported that whenever the Bible "didn't seem right," his pastor "would just whip this one out" and rule that the questioner "doesn't get to have an opinion about it."[^1] The hosts' sharper point is a concession an apologist can share: read as a blanket ban on the intellect, the verse is *misread*. They cite the Hermeneia Proverbs commentary that it "should not [be interpreted] as a general disparagement of intellect and understanding."[^2]

## Supporting Evidence
1. **The lived deployment is real.** The originating anecdote is a pastor using the verse to shut down every difficulty: the questioner is told he "doesn't get to have an opinion." This is the verse functioning not as wisdom but as an authority-stop.[^1] *(Evidence rating: Strong — a widely recognized pastoral pattern; Argument weight: Primary.)*
2. **The hosts name the mechanism precisely.** They classify it as a "thought-stopping cliché" — a rhetorical move that ends inquiry rather than answering it, equivalent to "I'm right and you're wrong, the end."[^3] *(Evidence rating: Moderate — an apt description, not a data claim; Argument weight: Primary.)*
3. **Scholarship agrees the verse is not anti-intellectual.** McClellan quotes the Hermeneia series: Proverbs 3:5 poses "a fundamental question. Is human insight the point of departure for sapiential reflection or does wisdom ultimately come from Adonai?" — a question about the *source* of wisdom, kin to [[Job]] and Ecclesiastes, dating to the Persian or Hellenistic period.[^2] *(Evidence rating: Strong — mainstream wisdom-literature scholarship; Argument weight: Secondary.)*
4. **The abuse has an ugly pedigree.** McClellan observes that on a wooden "lean not on your own understanding" reading, "slavery should be a-okay," and that abolitionists were "beyond a shadow of a doubt... frequently told to trust in the Lord and lean not on their own understanding" when they called slavery evil.[^4] *(Evidence rating: Moderate — historically plausible, asserted without citation; Argument weight: Secondary.)* See [[The Bible Has No Inherent Meaning Apart From Interpretation]].

## Best Quotes
> "Trust in the Lord with all your heart and lean not on your own understanding. In all your ways acknowledge him and he shall direct your paths." *(KJV, as read on the episode)*[^5]

> "One should not interpret Proverbs 3:5 as a general disparagement of intellect and understanding. Rather this verse has to do with a fundamental question. Is human insight the point of departure for sapiential reflection or does wisdom ultimately come from Adonai?" *(Hermeneia commentary, quoted by McClellan)*[^2]

> "It's a thought-stopping cliché... It's I'm not having this discussion... And it's also I'm right and you're wrong, the end."[^3]

## Apologetic Engagement
**Steelman the hosts.** The critique lands squarely on a genuine abuse. Using a proverb to *pre-empt* every question is intellectually dishonest, and Latter-day Saints have independent reason to reject it: the faith's founding narrative is a fourteen-year-old who *did not* accept "lean not on your own understanding" but asked God directly (James 1:5; JS–History 1:10-13). Notably, the hosts and the apologist actually agree on the exegesis — even the Hermeneia commentary McClellan cites says the verse is not a disparagement of intellect. The target is therefore a *deployment*, not the text.

**The informed response.** Because the hosts have conceded the verse is not anti-intellectual, the argument reduces to "some people misuse this verse" — which is true of every text and defeats no doctrine. Three points sharpen the reply. (1) **Genre.** Proverbs are calibrated observations, not absolute algorithms (Prov 26:4-5 sets two proverbs in deliberate tension); reading 3:5 as a rule forbidding thought misreads the genre the way reading "answer not a fool" as a universal law would. (2) **What "lean not" actually asks.** The couplet contrasts two *starting points* — autonomous self-sufficiency vs. trust in Adonai — counseling epistemic humility when one's own reasoning collides with revealed wisdom, not the suspension of reasoning. (3) **The LDS tradition holds study and faith together**, which is the decisive rebuttal to the anti-intellectual caricature: "seek learning, even by study and also by faith" (D&C 88:118), and the revelation formula "study it out in your mind; then... ask me if it be right" (D&C 9:8) makes reasoning the *prerequisite* of confirmation. The verse, rightly read, is on the same side as the scholarship the hosts cite; the abuser is on neither.

## LDS Relevance
McClellan is himself a Latter-day Saint, so this is an in-house critique as much as an external one. The LDS canon is unusually well-armed against thought-stopping proof-texting: 2 Nephi 9:28-29 warns that "to be learned is good if they hearken unto the counsels of God" — condemning not learning but learning unmoored from God, exactly the balance the Hermeneia commentary describes. And D&C 9:7-9 institutionalizes "study it out in your mind" *before* seeking a spiritual confirmation, so an LDS reader who uses Proverbs 3:5 to forbid inquiry is contradicting the Restoration's own epistemology. The honest LDS position concedes the abuse and keeps the verse — trust plus inquiry, not a trump card.
