# The Book of Enoch Was Not Taken Out of the Bible — It Predates the Canon

#type/claim

## The Claim

[[Dan McClellan]] argues that the viral claim "the [[1 Enoch|Book of Enoch]] was taken out of the Bible" is a **category error**. 1 Enoch fell out of favor with all Jewish authorities and almost all Christian authorities *before* a bound biblical canon existed — before "inspired scripture" as an unbound category hardened into "the Bible" as a delineated collection.[^1] You cannot remove a book from a collection that had not yet been formed. The historically accurate statement is a *stronger* one: 1 Enoch was widely regarded as inspired scripture in early Judaism and early Christianity, yet it was never part of a "Bible" from which it could be excised.[^2] McClellan grants that the pro-Enoch speaker "has a point" about its early authority, but says both parties in the debate misunderstand the timeline — and that the mirror claim ("so it was added") is equally confused.[^3]

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. "Scripture" was an unbound category before the fourth century — Argument weight: Primary
Before a canon was formalized, McClellan says, one "could speak of inspired scripture as an unbound category, as something that was not delineated" — "a much broader category than what would later become the Bible."[^1] The phrase "taken out of the Bible" presupposes a bound Bible that did not yet exist when 1 Enoch lost favor; it "flatten[s] the distinction between inspired scripture as an unbound category and the Bible as a bound collection of canonical scripture."[^4] This is the canon-specific application of the vault's broader principle in [[Scripture Was a Broader Category Than the Bible - The Anachronism of a Closed Canon]].

### 2. 1 Enoch fell out of favor *before* the canon closed, not after — Argument weight: Primary
"First Enoch fell out of favor with all Jewish authorities and most Christian authorities before that unbound category became a bound category, which is when we can begin to talk about the Bible. So, when the Bible was created, First Enoch was not there."[^5] The book's exclusion is real (see [[Book of Enoch - Exclusion from the Canon]]) but chronologically prior to canon closure — so "excluded from a forming canon," not "removed from a finished Bible."

### 3. The dating objection reverses the timeline — Evidence rating: Strong
The debate's anti-Enoch speaker claimed 1 Enoch "was written after" the canon was set ("by year 300 or 100"). McClellan corrects: those numbers "should be talking about 300 to 100 BCE."[^6] "First Enoch began to come together probably around 300 BCE and the latest parts of it were probably written between the 1st century BCE or the 1st century CE… it was written prior to the closing of the Jewish canon."[^7] So 1 Enoch predates canon closure; it was neither written "post-Bible" nor addable to one.

### 4. The mirror error — "so it was added" — is equally confused — Argument weight: Secondary
When the anti-Enoch speaker pivoted to "so technically that means it was added," McClellan notes this is the same category mistake in reverse: there was no bound Bible to add to either.[^3] Addition and subtraction both presuppose the very fixed boundary that did not yet exist.

### 5. The "warns against adding" verses are book-specific — Evidence rating: Strong
The appeal that scripture "warns you of false prophets… when people try to add things" invokes [[Deuteronomy 4.2|Deuteronomy 4:2]] or [[Revelation 22.18-19|Revelation 22:18–19]]. McClellan: "they're actually talking about their own books not the entire Bible because the Bible didn't exist when these texts were being written."[^8] The vault develops this rebuttal at length in [[Revelation 22 - Add or Take Away Rebutted]].

### 6. What the popular claim gets right — Argument weight: Secondary
McClellan repeatedly concedes the kernel of truth: 1 Enoch really was "considered inspired scripture by many early Jewish and early Christian folks, including very authoritative early Christians."[^2] [[Jude 1.14-15|Jude]] quotes it as prophecy (see [[Book of Enoch - Quoted as Scripture in Jude]]); [[Tertullian]] defended it; it is preserved in more Dead Sea Scrolls copies than all but ~5–6 books of the Hebrew Bible (see [[Book of Enoch - Dead Sea Scrolls Attestation]]). The intuition "this was treated as scripture" is sound; only the word "Bible" is anachronistic.

## Best Quotes

> "It would not be accurate to say it was taken out of the Bible because First Enoch fell out of favor with all Jewish authorities and almost all Christian authorities before we could speak of this thing that we call the Bible."[^1]

> "So, when the Bible was created, First Enoch was not there."[^5]

> "It's not additive… you're talking about either Deuteronomy or Revelation 22, which also warn against taking stuff away. However, they're actually talking about their own books, not the entire Bible, because the Bible didn't exist when these texts were being written."[^8]

## Apologetic Engagement

**Steelman (McClellan's case).** The correction is historically precise and hard to fault. "Canon" is a bounded, delineated list, and no source calls any such collection "the Bible" before the 4th century CE; the councils and criteria that fixed the boundary post-date 1 Enoch's loss of favor. To say a book was "taken out of the Bible" is therefore to project a late, bound object backward onto a period when the boundary of scripture was still open and contested. McClellan is also even-handed: he rebukes *both* the person overstating Enoch's canonicity and the person denying its early authority, and he anchors the point in the manuscript and citation record (Jude, Tertullian, Qumran) rather than in polemic.

**Informed LDS / Christian response.** McClellan's history is a gift to the Latter-day Saint reader more than a threat, because it dismantles the *closed-canon* assumption on which the strongest objections to additional scripture rest. The clumsy popular slogan ("taken out of the Bible") is a garbled version of a defensible Restoration insight: that the canon is a late human boundary, not a divinely sealed list, and that revelation and scripture were never meant to be closed (cf. Article of Faith 8–9). The Restoration does not need the crude claim McClellan refutes; it can hold the *stronger, historically grounded* claim he himself makes — scripture was an open category before it was a bound book. The honest limits cut both ways, and the informed apologist should name them: (a) McClellan's argument is **descriptive history**, not a warrant that 1 Enoch (or any excluded text) *ought* to be scripture, nor that Latter-day Saint additions are inspired — it clears an assumption without settling the authority question; (b) a traditional Christian can accept the whole timeline and still hold that providence guided the church's *bounding* of scripture (the confessional steelman already recorded in [[Book of Enoch - Exclusion from the Canon]] and [[Canon - Development and Closure]]); and (c) an inerrantist critic could reply that "taken out of the Bible" is merely loose shorthand for "excluded from the canon that was forming" — but that concession *is* McClellan's point, and it surrenders the rhetorical force the slogan was meant to carry. The open-category history therefore weakens closed-canon Protestantism while leaving the live LDS question exactly where the Restoration places it: *who* has authority to bound and re-open scripture.

## LDS Relevance

The fluid, unbound state of early "scripture" is congenial to the Latter-day Saint doctrine of an open canon and continuing revelation, and it neutralizes the stock "Revelation 22 forbids the Book of Mormon" objection by localizing that warning to its own book (see [[Revelation 22 - Add or Take Away Rebutted]]). It also foregrounds the very tradition Joseph Smith engaged: his Enoch material in Moses 6–7 develops Enochic themes (the weeping God, the Son of Man, the city of Zion) that the ancient corpus preserves — see [[Enoch]]. The disciplined caution the vault keeps elsewhere applies here too: an *open* category is not a *formless* one, so McClellan's history removes an objection without supplying the positive authority that the Restoration claims by prophetic office (cf. [[The Church Bounds Scripture It Does Not Write It - A Consensus Requires Enforcement]]).
