# Everyone Gets One Chance to Encounter Saving Grace in Mortality

## The Claim
Asked the classic hard case — "what about the guy in North Korea who just died at age 22 who doesn't know what Christianity is?" — **[[Mark (Ex-Mormon Baptist)]]** answers with [[Titus 2.11|Titus 2:11]]: "the grace of God that bringeth salvation hath appeared unto **all** men." He infers that "during mortality each one of us will have at least one opportunity to... encounter the grace of God that brings salvation," and to make a heart-choice about it. *How* God reaches the unreached he declines to specify — "How God does that I don't know... that's God's problem" — but the *that* is guaranteed by the verse.[^1] He backs it with Psalm 19:1 (the heavens "declare" God), holding that even those "in the deepest jungles" can look up and know there is a Creator to respond to.[^2]

## Supporting Evidence

1. **"All men" read distributively.** Titus 2:11's "hath appeared to all men" is taken to promise a real, per-person opportunity, not merely that grace is *available in principle*.[^1] *Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Weak-to-Moderate* — "all men" may mean "all kinds of men" or "the world" rather than "each individual gets a guaranteed encounter"; the universal-individual reading is a choice.

2. **General revelation as the fallback medium.** Psalm 19:1 and creation's testimony supply a channel by which even the never-evangelized can apprehend God and be accountable, so no one is left with *zero* light.[^2] *Argument weight: Secondary; Evidence rating: Weak* — general revelation grounds accountability (cf. Romans 1) more clearly than it grounds a *saving* opportunity.

3. **God's fidelity fills the gap.** Mark rests the unknown mechanism on God's character: "He's not a liar. He says that all mankind will have at least one opportunity."[^3] *Argument weight: Secondary; Evidence rating: Weak as proof, but internally consistent with his belief-alone soteriology.*

## Best Quotes
> "During mortality each one of us will have at least one opportunity to... encounter the grace of God that brings salvation. How God does that, I don't know. I can't answer that... That's God's problem."[^1]

> "Those who live in the deepest jungles can look up into the heavens and see that there's a Creator."[^2]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
**[[Hayden Carroll]] finds the position striking precisely because it is unusual for an evangelical**, and probes its coherence:

- **"I've never heard somebody say this before."** Carroll observes that a "Bible believer" / "sinner saved by grace" would typically *not* affirm a guaranteed opportunity for every person, and that many evangelicals would call Mark a heretic for it.[^4] He presses the mechanism: does God "shoot the doctrine into his mind and heart" at the moment of death? Mark can only answer "I don't know."[^5] The view thus purchases universal fairness at the cost of a fully unspecified — and un-evidenced — delivery method.

- **The Latter-day Saint irony (vault analysis).** Mark's instinct — that a just God guarantees *everyone* a fair chance — is close to a core Restoration conviction, but the LDS system supplies the very mechanism Mark leaves blank: the gospel is preached to the dead and ratified by [[Every Person Gets a Fair Opportunity Through Proxy Ordinances|proxy ordinances]], so opportunity need not be crammed into a mortal life the unreached person never had. Mark insists the single chance falls *within mortality*; the LDS model extends it beyond death, arguably a more complete answer to the same problem. See [[The Unevangelized Gap Is a Tu Quoque Against the Apostasy Objection]] and [[Scripture Explicitly Affirms Universal Salvific Will]].

- **Fair point for Mark:** his position is more generous and less arbitrary than strict exclusivism (only the evangelized can be saved), and it takes Titus 2:11's "all" seriously rather than narrowing it. A defender of Mark could add that "I don't know the mechanism" is honest agnosticism, not a defect — many theologies leave the fate of the unevangelized to God's mercy. The unresolved tension is whether an *in-mortality-only* universal opportunity is empirically credible for someone who demonstrably never encountered the gospel or, on Mark's own telling, even the name of Christ.
