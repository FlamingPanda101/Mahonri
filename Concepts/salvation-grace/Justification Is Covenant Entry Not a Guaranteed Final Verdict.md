# Justification Is Covenant Entry Not a Guaranteed Final Verdict

## The Claim
[[Jacob Hansen]] (LDS) reframes the whole grace/works debate by redefining **justification**. Drawing on the New Perspective on Paul (N. T. Wright, E. P. Sanders, James Dunn), he argues that in Paul "justified" means *present covenant membership* — who belongs to God's people now and how you can tell — the *beginning* of the relationship, not a verdict guaranteeing heaven. A final judgment "according to works" still determines which portion of the kingdom one inherits. [[Ryan (Protestant Debater)|Ryan]] answers that justification is a once-for-all forensic declaration grounded in Christ's *imputed* righteousness, that Romans 5:9 makes final salvation a *guarantee*, and that the New Perspective is a fringe reading.

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. Hansen's New-Perspective definition of justification (Argument weight: Primary — LDS side)
Hansen quotes [[N.T. Wright]]: "The point of justification by faith is that it takes place in the present time. It has to do with the question, who now belongs to God's people, and how can you tell?... used by Paul to denote those who hear the gospel and respond in faith and then are declared to be his people... given the status righteous within the covenant."[^1] He cites E. P. Sanders, [[James Dunn]], and Wright as the scholarly basis, framing Paul's problem as covenant *inclusion* of the Gentiles, not a Reformation debate about how to get to heaven. *(Evidence rating: Moderate — depends on the contested NPP reading of "works of the law"; see [[Works of the Law Means Mosaic Boundary Markers Not Obedience as Such]].)*

### 2. Covenant entry carries covenant obligations (Argument weight: Primary — LDS side)
"Once you're within the covenant, you still have covenant obligations. You can't have a covenant without covenant obligations."[^2] Hansen's marriage analogy: Israel was "graciously chosen... an act of grace," then told to "be faithful to it"; "I'm not perfect to my wife," but faithfulness means complying "with the terms of the covenant so that you can receive the promised blessings of the covenant."[^3]

### 3. Romans 5:9 — "much more shall we be saved": guarantee or provision? (Argument weight: Primary — the crux)
Reading [[Romans 5.1-9|Romans 5:9]], Ryan argues the logic is unconditional: "It's saying because justification's happened and you are in this status of being justified right now... if X then definitely Y... you're going to be saved from the wrath of God."[^4] Hansen replies that Romans 5:1 is *initial* justification — "entry into the covenant, which is entry into right relationship with God. But that isn't the end of the story because there's the final judgment where we're judged by our works," so "much more" means "we're in the right position so that we can be justified in the final judgment."[^5]

### 4. Ryan: imputed righteousness at the judgment (Argument weight: Primary — Protestant side)
Both agree "justified" means "declared righteous,"[^6] but Ryan locates the righteousness outside the believer: "For a believer I'm judged based on the merits of Jesus Christ. So when he looks at my life he sees me through the lens of Christ... he sees me as perfect. That's [[Philippians 3.9|Philippians 3:9]]."[^7] Final acceptance therefore cannot be endangered by the believer's imperfect works.

### 5. Ryan: the New Perspective is a minority view (Argument weight: Secondary — Protestant side)
"You're quoting some new Paul perspective scholars who are on a fringe view... They're not even the mainstream view."[^8] Ryan also invokes Romans 11:6 — "if it is by grace, it is no longer on the basis of works; otherwise grace would no longer be grace."

### 6. Ostler: the model is Second-Temple *covenantal nomism* (Argument weight: Primary — LDS side)
On a separate Thoughtful Faith episode, [[Blake Ostler]] gives the framework its scholarly name: to be justified "means to be put into a right relationship with God," which for Jews rested on "the grace of the covenant" — "this is called *covenantal nomism*." One enters the covenant "by our faith in Christ, not by our faith in the law of Moses," so Paul's "justified" is not the Protestant "saved once and for all with eternal security."[^9] *(Evidence rating: Moderate — the E. P. Sanders framework, contested by "old perspective" exegetes.)*

### 7. Ostler: Paul's real problem is Gentile inclusion, and Abraham proves it (Argument weight: Primary — LDS side)
"The issue that Paul is addressing is how do we count Gentiles as part of the covenant people?"[^10] Romans 4 answers it from Israel's own scripture: "Abraham came into right relationship with God before the law was even given, before the covenant was even given... merely because he had entered into a trust relationship with God" — before circumcision.[^11] So the point is covenant *entry* by faith, not a claim that post-entry obedience is optional: "once into the covenant relationship, he was obligated to accept and follow the terms of the covenant."[^12]

### 8. Ostler: covenant obligations remain, and one can "fall from grace" (Argument weight: Primary — LDS side)
Against "once saved always saved," Ostler presses [[Galatians 5.2-4|Galatians]]: it "expressly states that one could fall from grace... if you don't do the works of love that are part of the law, you don't remain in grace."[^13] The new covenant's terms are subsumed into a single command — "love one another as I have loved you" — so covenant faithfulness is the *response* to grace, not a way to earn entry. He adds that [[James the brother of Jesus|James]] corrects not Paul but *pseudo*-Paulinism, the "sin so grace abounds" distortion Paul himself rebuked ("God forbid").[^14] See [[Can a True Believer Fall Away]] and [[Once Saved Always Saved Means Salvation Is Irreversible]].

## Best Quotes
> "Who now belongs to God's people, and how can you tell?... they are given the status righteous within the covenant." — N. T. Wright, quoted by Jacob Hansen[^1]

> "To be justified means to be put into a right relationship with God... this is called covenantal nomism." — Blake Ostler[^9]

> "If X then definitely Y. If X has happened, definitely Y is going to happen... you're going to be saved from the wrath of God." — Ryan, on Romans 5:9[^4]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

### The Protestant steelman (Ryan): justification is a completed, guaranteed status
On the Reformation reading, to be "justified by faith" (Romans 5:1) is to be declared righteous once for all, with Christ's righteousness imputed ([[Philippians 3.9|Philippians 3:9]]); the *a fortiori* of Romans 5:9-10 then guarantees that the justified "shall be saved from wrath." Adding a further "judgment by works" that could reverse the verdict "adds something to the text" and collapses grace into works (Romans 11:6). The believer's future obedience is the *evidence and fruit* of a salvation already secured, not a second gate.

### The Latter-day Saint steelman (Hansen): two moments, entry and inheritance
Hansen's covenantal model distinguishes *initial* justification (freely entering the covenant by faith) from the *final* judgment by works (Romans 2:6-13) that determines one's inheritance in the kingdom. This is structurally close to the Catholic [[Initial vs Ongoing Justification|initial-vs-ongoing justification]] distinction and to the mechanism debate in [[Justification - Infusion vs Legal Fiction]]. On this view Paul's "not of works" excludes *earning entry* and *works of Torah as boundary markers*, not covenant faithfulness as a response to grace.

**Where it turns:** Ryan's decisive move is exegetical — Romans 5:9 says the *already-justified* "shall be saved," with no stated condition, so importing a works-conditioned final verdict is eisegesis. Hansen's decisive move is lexical-historical — first-century "justification" was covenant-membership language, and the very next chapters (Romans 2) promise judgment "according to works," so the Reformation reads a later go-to-heaven question back into Paul. The dispute ultimately rides on whether Paul's "justified" is a terminal verdict or an inaugural status.
