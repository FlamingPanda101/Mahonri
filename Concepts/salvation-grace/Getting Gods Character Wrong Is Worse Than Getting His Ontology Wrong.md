# Getting Gods Character Wrong Is Worse Than Getting His Ontology Wrong

## The Claim

The comparative core of the "is Calvinism worse than Mormonism?" question. [[Tim Stratton]] argues that getting God's **character** wrong (Calvinism's deity who deceives and damns most of humanity from the womb) is a graver error than getting God's **ontology/metaphysics** wrong (the LDS non-Trinitarian view). Applying to Calvinism the very "different gospel" test Protestants aim at Mormonism, he finds "worse problems" in Calvinism.[^1] [[Josh Klene]] pushes back that ontology *is* soteriologically decisive; [[Hayden Carroll]] presses that character-plus-sincerity should elicit mercy.

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. The character/ontology analogy (Argument weight: Primary)

Stratton's illustration: describe him as "5'11, 205, green eyes" instead of "6' tall, blue eyes, 195" and he will not take offense — you got his *ontology* slightly wrong but his *character* right. But "start saying that stuff about my character" — that he "likes to punch babies," "hated people" — "I'm going to take offense." Getting *who someone is morally* wrong ruptures the relationship in a way getting his measurements wrong does not.[^2]

### 2. The comparison, made explicitly at the EPS (Argument weight: Primary)

With Phil Colberg at the Evangelical Philosophical Society (Boston), before a largely Calvinist audience that agreed Mormonism is false, Stratton "appl[ied] the same method we used to show... why we think Mormonism is a different gospel... drop[ped] that onto Calvinism, and... [saw] worse problems."[^1] The tension he cannot square: a Calvinist may "affirm the truth of the Trinity" (right ontology) yet describe a God who does not desire all to be saved — while the Latter-day Saint may get the Trinity wrong yet rightly affirm that "God is perfectly loving and he wants all people to be saved... You guys are right."[^3]

### 3. Why the defect is hidden, not absent (Argument weight: Secondary)

Stratton's "under the hood" analogy: LDS heterodoxy is visible up front — "we wave our flags with the heresy... denied the Trinity up front" — whereas Calvinism's problems require an expert to see, "like looking underneath the hood" of an engine. That very concealment, he suggests, is Calvinism's only "saving grace" socially, not a sign the problems are smaller.[^4]

### 4. Stratton's own resolution on the salvation question (Argument weight: Secondary)

Pressed on whether a sincere Latter-day Saint is saved, Stratton declines "definitive claims" but affirms: "anybody who God knows would worship him as he truly is, he will make sure that they get what they need to make that happen" — the disqualifier is not imperfect understanding but *idolizing* a false theology over truth ("more committed to their theology than what God is giving them").[^5]

## Best Quotes

> "You get everything about me physically and ontologically right... but you start saying that stuff about my character — I'm going to stop you right there, and we're going to have words." — [[Tim Stratton]][^2]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

### Objection 1 (Klene): Ontology is not incidental — it identifies *whom* you worship
**Steelman:** [[Josh Klene]] argues you cannot have a true love relationship with "an entirely different being" than the one who exists: calling God a triune being when you mean three gods is not like mis-stating a height — it is like insisting "Josh is a really loyal and loving *dog*" when Josh is a person. On that ground he says a Latter-day Saint who dies affirming the LDS view "would not go to heaven," the same verdict he gives his Roman Catholic friends.[^6]

**Response:** Stratton (and the LDS host) reply that the two are worshiping the *same* Father, Son, and Spirit — "we are all agreeing that there's three persons" — disputing only the metaphysics of their unity, whereas the Calvinist disagreement is about God's very *character* (loving vs. deceiving). A wrong theory of a person's constitution is less relationship-destroying than a wrong account of his moral nature. Klene's own fallback — that God may reveal himself to the sincere at death — concedes that ontology is not an absolute bar.

### Objection 2 (Carroll): A merciful God judges sincerity, not test scores
**Steelman:** [[Hayden Carroll]] presses that the LDS God is *more merciful*: he asks "was he genuine? was he trying to follow truth?" and holds people "accountable to what they understand," so honest error is not damning. If God has not made the Trinity evident to a sincere seeker, punishing him for not seeing it impugns God's justice.[^7] This is the LDS orthopraxy-over-orthodoxy emphasis (see [[Orthopraxy over Orthodoxy in LDS Salvation]] and [[Distinction Between Knowing Rejection and Imperfect Understanding]]).

**Response:** Both guests converge toward a "pre-mortem/postmortem" encounter in which God meets the honest seeker and reveals himself, so that final separation reflects *continued* worship of "something else" rather than punishment for innocent ignorance — a move Carroll notes is "the exact same thing" the LDS say. The remaining disagreement is narrow: whether such a corrective encounter can occur *after* death (LDS: yes; Klene: uncertain).
