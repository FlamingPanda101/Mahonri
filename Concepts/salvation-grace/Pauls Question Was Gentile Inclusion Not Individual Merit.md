# Pauls Question Was Gentile Inclusion Not Individual Merit

## The Claim
The Protestant *faith-versus-works* reading of [[Paul the Apostle|Paul]] "totally misses the point" because it imposes a **sixteenth-century question on a first-century text** — an anachronism. Following the New Perspective on Paul ([[E.P. Sanders]], [[James Dunn]], [[N.T. Wright]]), Thoughtful Faith argues the Reformers "were using Paul to try and justify their break from the Catholic Church," reading his letters as answers to "How can I, as an individual sinner, be saved?" But Paul "wasn't debating medieval merit theology... whether a guilty sinner could earn heaven."[^1][^2] His actual crisis was the fate of **Israel's covenant** once Jesus rose: if the Messiah has come and now calls all nations, "Who are God's people now? Has circumcision ended?... What about Gentiles?"[^3] Paul's recurring questions — "Who belongs? Who inherits Abraham's promises? Who are God's people?" — are *covenant* questions, not questions about individual merit.[^4] Read in his own frame, Paul's letters "suddenly become coherent in a new way."[^5] This is the load-bearing frame beneath the boundary-marker reading of "works of the law" ([[Works of the Law Means Mosaic Boundary Markers Not Obedience as Such]]) and the covenant reading of justification ([[Justification Is Covenant Entry Not a Guaranteed Final Verdict]]).

## Supporting Evidence
1. **The New Perspective names the anachronism (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate).** "Biblical scholars from EP Sanders to James Dunn to NT Wright and many others argue... The reformers were fighting 16th century Catholicism. Paul was trying to explain something completely different."[^2] This is the mainstream NPP consensus, though contested by "Old Perspective" (Reformed) exegetes — see steelman.
2. **The Lincoln analogy (Argument weight: Secondary; Evidence rating: Illustrative).** Reading Paul through the Reformation is "like reading Abraham Lincoln's speeches through the lens of the Cold War. You'll find all sorts of interesting applications... but you'd almost certainly misunderstand what Lincoln himself was trying to accomplish."[^1] A later conflict can yield useful applications while distorting the author's own aim.
3. **The precipitating crisis is the resurrection's effect on the covenant (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate).** "Paul was trying to deal with a deep puzzle that exploded into the mind of Jesus's Jewish followers as soon as they accepted that Jesus rose from the dead. If Jesus really was Israel's Messiah, what does that mean for Israel, God's covenant people?"[^6] Paul's primary concern is "the climax of Israel's story," not a personal salvation formula.[^7]
4. **Every major Pauline controversy is a covenant-boundary controversy (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Strong on the texts).** "Acts 15, Galatians, Romans, almost every controversy that Paul addresses comes back to this issue" — whether "Gentiles have to become Jews before they belong to God's covenant family."[^8] The question is entrance into the covenant people, not earning heaven. Corroborated independently in [[Justification Is Covenant Entry Not a Guaranteed Final Verdict]] (Ostler: "how do we count Gentiles as part of the covenant people?").
5. **Good history lets the ancient author speak first (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Methodological).** "Historical interpretation does not begin with the question, 'How did Paul's words help me in my debates with Catholics?' but rather with honest questions like, 'What problem was Paul actually trying to solve in the first century?'... It lets ancient peoples speak before we recruit them into our modern debates."[^9] The same context-before-proof-text discipline the vault applies elsewhere (cf. anachronism critiques in [[Greek Categories Are Anachronistic to the Hebrew Bible]]).

## Best Quotes
> "The reformers were fighting 16th century Catholicism. Paul was trying to explain something completely different. He wasn't debating medieval merit theology. He wasn't debating whether a guilty sinner could earn heaven."[^2]

> "Protestants have often approached Paul with this question: How can I, as an individual sinner, be saved? Now, that's an important question, but it isn't the issue Paul's focused on."[^7]

> "That's what good history does. It lets ancient peoples speak before we recruit them into our modern debates."[^9]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
**The Old Perspective (Reformed) reply — and the video's own concession.** The video is careful not to overclaim: "Does this mean the reformers were wrong about everything? No. Many of the questions they asked were and are legitimate."[^10] That concession marks the honest boundary of the argument, and the Reformed case presses it:

- **Paul does address individual guilt.** Romans 1–3 indict all humanity ("all have sinned"), and Romans 3:20 says "by works of the law shall no flesh be justified" as a *universal* verdict, not merely a ruling on Gentile admission. So "how is a guilty sinner made right with God?" is not purely a medieval import — it is arguably in the text.
- **Abraham precedes the boundary markers.** [[Romans 4]] grounds justification in Abraham's faith *before* circumcision and *before* the law — which the Reformed read as proof Paul means faith over *any* human works, not just ethnic markers (cf. Romans 11:6, "if by grace, then no more of works").
- **Even Wright keeps a personal dimension.** N. T. Wright, the video's own authority, retains a *final justification according to works* and a real personal reconciliation — so the NPP corrects the *frame* without erasing individual soteriology. Pressed too far, the "it's only about Gentiles" reading becomes its own reductionism.

**Where it lands.** The strongest neutral formulation: the anachronism critique is a genuine corrective to *flattening* Paul into a Reformation tract, and it is well-evidenced that covenant-membership and Gentile inclusion are central to Galatians and Romans. It does not follow that Paul says *nothing* about individual grace and guilt — the both/and is the defensible position, and the video concedes it.

## LDS Relevance
This frame is why Paul "sounds like a Mormon." Once his question is covenant *membership* rather than individual merit, the pieces that embarrass *sola fide* fall into place as Restoration commonplaces: grace freely brings you into the covenant; faith is loyalty/allegiance to the risen King ([[Faith Is Allegiance to Christ Not Mere Mental Assent]]); covenant faithfulness (obedience, ordinances, judgment "according to works") is the covenant's terms, not a scheme to earn heaven; and the whole thing sits inside a cosmic story of new creation ([[The Gospel Is the Good News of God's Kingdom Not a Doctrinal Formula]]). Latter-day Saints thus escape the caricature that they try to *earn* salvation — the covenant/allegiance reading reframes works as fidelity, exactly the synthesis of 2 Nephi 25:23 and Articles of Faith 1:3. That a nineteenth-century Joseph Smith reproduced this first-century structure without access to the New Perspective is developed in [[Joseph Smith Reconstructed First-Century Soteriology Without Knowing It]].
