# Doctrine and Covenants 137 Anticipates Middle Knowledge

## The Claim

Joseph Smith's 1836 Kirtland-Temple vision ([[Doctrine and Covenants 137|D&C 137]]) grounds celestial salvation on a **counterfactual** — those who *would have received* the gospel had they lived — which [[Tim Stratton]] and [[Hayden Carroll]] agree is functionally **[[Molinism|middle knowledge]]**. On this reading a Restoration scripture articulates, in 1836, the very structure Molinists use to reconcile God's foreknowledge with human freedom.[^1]

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. The counterfactual clause in D&C 137:7 (Argument weight: Primary — Scriptural)

Carroll reads the text: "All those who have died without a knowledge of the gospel, who *would have received it* if they had been permitted to tarry, shall be heirs of the celestial kingdom." Stratton's response: "That's God's counterfactual knowledge. And if he had that prior to the formation of the universe, that would count as middle knowledge."[^1] The salvation of the honest-hearted unevangelized is decided by what they *would freely* have done — a counterfactual of creaturely freedom.

### 2. Alvin Smith as the occasion (Argument weight: Secondary)

The vision was prompted by Joseph seeing his older brother [[Alvin Smith]] — who died without LDS baptism — in the celestial kingdom. Stratton notes Joseph "appealed to what we now see as middle knowledge: God knew he would respond appropriately."[^2] The doctrine thus originates not as abstract philosophy but as an answer to a concrete pastoral case of the "unevangelized gap."

### 3. Infant salvation on the same logic (Argument weight: Secondary)

D&C 137:10 — "all children who die before they arrive at the years of accountability are saved in the celestial kingdom" — is, for the LDS, a definite answer where Protestants "get lots of different answers." Stratton grants its logic is better than the rival Christian positions he surveys in his article "What About Babies Who Die," several of which he judges "clearly false."[^3] The LDS reconcile it with agency by holding such souls still *choose* exaltation, consistent with the counterfactual structure of verse 7.

### 4. Foreknowledge without determinism (the Doctor Strange gloss) (Argument weight: Tertiary)

Stratton stresses Molinism "doesn't deny God's absolute foreknowledge": God "knows what will happen, but he knows what could have happened" — his running Doctor Strange analogy (knowing every branch, yet the agents act freely). Middle knowledge is *pre-volitional* knowledge of free choices, not a decree causing them.[^4]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

### Objection 1 (Calvinist): This is unconditional election, not middle knowledge
**Steelman:** The verse can be read as God simply *decreeing* whom he will save (the elect infants and elect unevangelized), with "would have received it" describing God's own sovereign appointment rather than a foreseen free choice. Nothing forces a libertarian reading.

**Response:** The proponents note the clause is explicitly *counterfactual and conditional* ("if they had been permitted to tarry"), which presupposes a real, unactualized alternative the person would have taken — the hallmark of middle knowledge, not of an unconditional decree. Read with [[TRUMP - The Molinist Alternative to TULIP]].

### Objection 2 (Reformed/anti-Molinist): The counterfactuals have no truth-makers
**Steelman:** The "grounding objection" holds that there is no fact of the matter about what a merely possible person *would freely* do, so verse 7 cannot be cashed out as knowledge of a determinate counterfactual.

**Response:** This is the standard philosophical dispute over Molinism generally (see [[Molinism]] and [[Molinism Does Not Solve Predestination]]); the LDS reply typically appeals to the reality of pre-mortal, actually-existing agents whose dispositions God genuinely knows, which supplies the truth-maker the grounding objection says is missing.
