# Eternal Life Is a Present Possession Not Only Future Exaltation

## The Claim
The ex-Mormon guest **[[Mark (Ex-Mormon Baptist)]]** argues that Scripture makes eternal life something a believer **already possesses** the moment he believes, not a reward withheld until after death. His proof text is [[John 6.47|John 6:47]] — "he that believeth on me *hath* everlasting life" — where "hath" is present tense: "to possess, to hold in your hands."[^1] He contends this is "the opposite" of Latter-day Saint theology, in which "eternal life" is defined as **exaltation in the highest celestial kingdom**, "attainable only after the final resurrection." His clinching citation is [[Dallin H. Oaks]] (1998): "if we use the word salvation to mean eternal life, none of us can say that he has been saved during mortality. That precious gift can only be obtained after the final resurrection."[^2] Mark's challenge: "Where is that [present-tense possession] in LDS theology?"

## Supporting Evidence

1. **The present-tense verb.** John 6:47's "hath," reinforced by John 6:40 seven verses earlier, is grammatically present; the parallel [[1 John 5.11-12|1 John 5:11-12]] ("He that *hath* the Son *hath* life") and John 3:36 / John 5:24 use the same present possession.[^1] *Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate* — the grammar is real, though "have eternal life" can denote a present guarantee of a future state without collapsing the two.

2. **A named, canonized LDS definition that defers it.** Mark's strongest point is not exegetical but documentary: Oaks's statement was, he says, folded into the Church manual *True to the Faith* (p. 154, "Salvation"), which defines eternal life as exaltation obtained after the final judgment — so the deferral is not one apostle's slip but published teaching.[^2][^3] *Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate-to-Strong on the narrow point that the manual's primary definition is post-mortal.*

3. **The assurance payoff.** Because he holds eternal life now, Mark can say what he says he never could as a Latter-day Saint: "I know that I have eternal life today because of Jesus Christ."[^4] *Argument weight: Secondary; Evidence rating: Weak as proof (it is testimonial), but it is the experiential engine of his whole case.*

## Best Quotes
> "He that believeth on me *hath* everlasting life. *Hath* is a present tense word. Obviously, it means *has*, to possess, to hold in your hands... Where is that in LDS theology?"[^1]

> "If we use the word salvation to mean eternal life, none of us can say that he has been saved during mortality. That precious gift can only be obtained after the final resurrection." — Dallin H. Oaks, as quoted by Mark[^2]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
**[[Hayden Carroll]]'s reply is that "eternal life" is not univocal, and that the present sense is fully at home in Restoration scripture** — a strong response on several fronts:

- **The Restoration teaches the present sense too.** Carroll's favorite verse, [[D&C 45.3-5|D&C 45:3-5]], has Christ the Advocate pleading that believers "have everlasting life," which he reads exactly as Mark reads John 6: "when you choose to believe in Jesus Christ, you have eternal life... in the now, from his perspective."[^5] He also affirms [[1 John 5.11-12|1 John 5:11-12]] outright: "I believe that actually... And that's what I've been taught as a Latter-day Saint."[^6] The remarkable outcome is that **both men affirm they have eternal life today** — narrowing the dispute to definitions and emphasis, not the reality of present assurance.

- **Multiple senses, contextually deployed.** Carroll distinguishes (a) eternal life as *knowing God* / relationship (John 17:3; 1 John 5:20), possessable now, from (b) eternal life as the *glorified, resurrected celestial state*, which by definition is future. Oaks, he argues charitably, was using sense (b): "on that definition, you don't have that right now — and neither does Mark." He cites President [[Russell M. Nelson]] that eternal life is "the type and quality of life" God lives, not merely unending duration.[^7] Mark concedes the term has "more than one definition," which undercuts his insistence that LDS usage is simply wrong. *This is the note's pivot: once Mark grants polysemy, "your church teaches the opposite" weakens to "your church emphasizes sense (b)."*

- **Prophets are not infallible — and it doesn't matter here.** Carroll grants that even if Oaks were pressing sense (b) too hard, "we don't hold to infallible apostles"; a single emphasis does not overturn canon ([[D&C 45.3-5|D&C 45:3-5]]) or the Church's grace teaching. He turns this into a question: even granting the point, how does it follow that one should leave the Church? See [[Distinction Between Knowing Rejection and Imperfect Understanding]].

- **Where the steelman cuts back toward Mark.** Carroll never produces an LDS manual or General Authority statement that *affirmatively* teaches present-tense possession as directly as John 6:47 does; he rests on D&C 45 plus his own interpretation. Mark's demand — show me the Church *teaching* John 6:47 to members as present possession, not merely having it in the canon — goes largely unmet on camera, and he fairly notes D&C 45:5's "*may* come... and have everlasting life" reads futurically.[^8] So the honest scorecard: Mark wins that the Church's *catechetical emphasis* is post-mortal; Carroll wins that the *doctrine* is not "anti" present possession, since Restoration scripture affirms it.
