#type/claim

# Joseph Smith Reconstructed First-Century Soteriology Without Knowing It

## The Claim
An **evidential argument for Joseph Smith's prophethood** built on soteriology. [[Blake Ostler]] and [[Jacob Hansen]] observe that D&C 76 (1832) reconciles four things the historic creeds could not hold together — **universal salvation by grace, graded rewards, judgment by works, and covenant membership** — which is exactly the synthesis the New Perspective on Paul now recovers as *first-century Jewish* soteriology. Yet Joseph Smith "had no idea about the soteriology of first century Jews," "was neither a theologian nor a philosopher," and lived in the culture most fixated on the heaven/hell binary that the framework overturns. That a "nobody" "serendipitously fell into a solution to all the major theological problems of Christianity" is, they argue, best explained by revelation.[^1][^2][^3]

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. First-century hope was resurrection and the renewed earth, not "going to heaven" (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate — mainstream in NT scholarship)
Paul was "obsessed with who's in the covenant and who's out of it," not the heaven/hell question Catholics and Protestants fixate on.[^4] The Jewish hope "was in the coming day of the Lord and the resurrection when the earth would be renewed as a new creation and the covenants would all be fulfilled."[^5] Ostler's proof text is Martha: at Lazarus's death she does not say he is "in heaven forever," but pins her hope on "that he will rise at the last day in the resurrection" — "that was the Jewish hope."[^6] This aligns with [[Christianity Inherited Jewish Day-of-the-Lord Eschatology]] and [[The Immortal Soul Is a Platonic Import Not a Biblical Doctrine]].

### 2. LDS soteriology matches that frame point-for-point (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate)
"That's what our soteriology is" — the spirit world / Sheol as a waiting "period before the great and final show," bodily resurrection, a renewed earth, and covenant membership (in or out).[^7] The distinctively "weird" parts of Mormonism (temples, new Jerusalem, Israel, covenants) turn out to be "all our stuff... it's all covenant, Israel, restoration in the last day," recovering the biblical frame the wider tradition lost. See [[LDS Resurrection Doctrine Recovers the Hebrew Whole-Person Framework]].[^8]

### 3. D&C 76 reconciles what the creeds could not (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Strong for the internal claim)
"Section 76 somehow has this way of reconciling this idea of a universal salvation and justification with... different rewards in heaven. And that we still can be judged by our works even though we're all saved by grace through faith."[^2] For the mechanism — three senses of "saved," universal confession, graded glory — see [[Saved Justified and Exalted Are Three Distinct Terms]] and [[Justification Is Covenant Entry Not a Guaranteed Final Verdict]].

### 4. Joseph Smith lacked the means to construct it (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate — the crux of the inference)
Ostler grants freely that Joseph Smith "was neither a theologian nor a philosopher," imprecise with terminology,[^9] and "had no idea about the soteriology of first century Jews."[^2] Hansen presses the point as a hypothetical concession: "let's say Joseph Smith was full of it and made all this stuff up — he made up the most incredible solves to these theological questions... the guy solved the problems that Catholicism, Protestantism, Orthodoxy couldn't solve. And he was a nobody... That seems miraculous."[^1] Ostler: he "bumbled and stumbled his way into the thought world of ancient Christianity to restore it."[^3]

### 5. Modern scholarship independently vindicates the match (Argument weight: Secondary; Evidence rating: Moderate)
"The scholarly research that's been done within the New Perspective on Paul... has really brought Paul's thought in a way that is very consistent with LDS thought," and stands as "a major challenge and critique of the standard Protestant view of Paul."[^10] The argument's force depends on this being *later, independent* scholarly convergence, not something available to Joseph Smith in 1832.

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

### The skeptic's steelman: retrofitting and a contested framework
The argument's weakest joint is that it reads a *contemporary, still-contested* scholarly paradigm (the New Perspective) back onto an 1832 text and then treats the fit as miraculous. A critic will note: (a) the New Perspective is itself disputed, so "Paul really taught this" is not settled ground to argue from ([[Justification Is Covenant Entry Not a Guaranteed Final Verdict]] logs the pushback); (b) graded post-mortem rewards, universalism, and covenant theology were **not** unknown in Joseph Smith's environment — universalism was a live New England movement (his own grandfather leaned universalist), Swedenborgian tiered heavens circulated, and the Bible he read is full of covenant and resurrection language; so the "means" were plausibly available; (c) any sufficiently flexible system can be mapped onto Paul after the fact (the parallelomania worry). The inference "best explained by revelation" therefore rests on how *independent* and *inaccessible* the match really was — precisely what the skeptic disputes.

### The believer's rejoinder
The LDS reply is that the *specific synthesis* — universal salvation held together with judgment-by-works and graded glory and covenant-in/out, framed by resurrection rather than a disembodied heaven — is not any one of those ambient ideas but their coherent integration, delivered by a farmer with no theological training, and that its convergence with independent later scholarship is a genuine datum even if not a proof. As with any [[Knowledge Runs on Degrees of Probability Not Certitude|probabilistic]] case, it is offered as one strand of cumulative evidence, not a stand-alone demonstration.
