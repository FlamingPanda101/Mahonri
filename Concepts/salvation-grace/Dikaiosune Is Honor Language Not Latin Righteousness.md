#type/claim

# Dikaiosune Is Honor Language Not Latin Righteousness

## The Claim
[[Blake Ostler]] argues that the Reformation grace/works impasse is downstream of a **translation error**. Paul's Greek *dikaiosynē* ("righteousness") is **honor-and-shame language** — being *accounted honorable* and put into correct relationship — not the Latin forensic *iustitia* the Reformers built their systems on. When Greek honor-talk was rendered into Latin legal categories, "righteousness" hardened into a courtroom verdict, and both the Protestant "legal fiction" of *imputed* (alien) righteousness and the Catholic notion of *infused* righteousness became category mistakes. Ostler's positive model is the *patron/honor* world dramatized in *A Knight's Tale*: because Christ's honor is unimpeachable, he can confer honor-status on the believer the way a king can knight a commoner.[^1][^2]

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. The biblical world is honor-shame, not Latin righteousness (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate)
"The biblical texts are operating in a culture of honor and shame. And honor is very different than the Latin view of righteousness."[^1] The people writing about justification "were Greeks using Greek terminology. It then got translated into Latin, where terms... have a very different meaning."[^3] Ostler ties this to the same Greek-to-Latin slippage he blames for original sin (Augustine's *in quo* — see [[The Infant-Damnation Reductio Against Inherited Guilt Plus Required Profession]]).

### 2. *Dikaiosynē* resists English "righteousness" (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate)
Reading [[Romans 4.4-5|Romans 4]], Ostler notes the Greek is *dikaiosynē*, "a term that isn't easily translated into English... something more like being in the correct relationship with someone," and the surrounding vocabulary ("counted," "reckoned") is *accounting* language.[^4] Applied to Abraham: his belief "was counted to him as an honor... so that God will now honor him and accept him."[^5] Ostler claims the scholarly consensus backs the point: "the best works all agree that it's not a straightforward righteousness as we use the term in English. Every single one of them."[^6]

### 3. The *Knight's Tale* illustration of honor-conferral (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Strong as illustration)
In the film, a commoner is exposed as a fraud with no letters of patent (proof of nobility) and pilloried — until Prince Edward steps forward, declares "This man is nobility," and knights him on the spot. "He's not really nobility. But someone is noble just because the king has unimpeachable honor... it's a matter of honor, not because he's righteous."[^7] So with Christ: "Because Christ's honor is unimpeachable. When he honors us, when we take him as our patron, and he accepts us... he stands before the Father and his honor is unimpeachable and says, 'This person is worthy of a kingdom of glory.'"[^2] This is the honor-conferral of adoption into the family, not a transfer of a moral quality.

### 4. Why imputation is a "legal fiction" — ethical voluntarism (Argument weight: Secondary; Evidence rating: Moderate)
Ostler diagnoses the Protestant scheme as resting on **ethical voluntarism / divine command theory**: since good and evil are "whatever God says they are," God can simply "regard you as righteous even though you're not" — the "famous Lutheran phrase, we have an alien righteousness that isn't our own... it's a simple fiction."[^8] He pairs it with imputed original sin as its mirror image — God "regards us as evil when we're not, and then he regards us as righteous when we're not."[^9] For the Calvinist metaethics behind this, see [[The Calvinist God Cannot Love Because He Controls Everything]].

### 5. The Catholic infusion view also misfires (Argument weight: Secondary; Evidence rating: Weak/assertion)
Ostler holds that Catholic *infusion* is likewise "a vast misunderstanding of the nature of righteousness," because righteousness is not a transferable substance — "I can't just hand my righteousness to you."[^8] Honor-conferral, on his model, avoids both errors: God does not smuggle a fictional or infused quality into the believer but confers *status/relationship*. Contrast the Catholic framing in [[Justification - Infusion vs Legal Fiction]].

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

### The Reformed steelman: forensic justification is exactly Paul's point
A Reformed exegete will answer that Paul's courtroom imagery is not a Latin corruption but native to the Greek: *dikaioō* is a declarative verb ("to declare righteous / acquit"), and Romans 4:4-5 deliberately contrasts wage-*debt* with *reckoned* gift — a legal-accounting frame Paul himself sets up. On this reading, imputed righteousness ([[Double Imputation Is Not the Gospel|"the great exchange"]], [[Philippians 3.9|Philippians 3:9]]) is not a "fiction" but God's gracious verdict grounded in Christ's real, historical obedience — no more fictional than a pardon. Calling it ethical voluntarism is a caricature: most Reformed theologians ground the atonement in God's justice, not bare will.

### The honor model still owes an account of sin's penalty
Even granting the honor-shame semantics (which the New Perspective supports — see [[Justification Is Covenant Entry Not a Guaranteed Final Verdict]]), honor-conferral does not by itself explain how the cross deals with guilt/penalty; Ostler's fuller answer is his relational/compassion theory of atonement ([[The Compassion Theory of Atonement]], [[Relationship Not Payment - Atonement Rejects Retributive Justice]]), which is itself contested. And "every scholar agrees" overstates a genuinely live lexical debate: forensic and relational senses of *dikaiosynē* coexist in Paul, and honor-shame is a lens, not a lexicon-closing proof.

**Where it turns:** the dispute is whether Paul's justification vocabulary is fundamentally *legal* (a verdict, imputing an alien righteousness) or *relational/honorific* (conferring covenant status/honor). Ostler's move dissolves the imputation-vs-infusion fight by denying the shared Latin premise both inherit; the Reformed reply is that the courtroom frame is Paul's own, not Rome's mistranslation.
