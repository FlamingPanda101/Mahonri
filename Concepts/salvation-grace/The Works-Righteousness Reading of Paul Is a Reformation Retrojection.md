# The Works-Righteousness Reading of Paul Is a Reformation Retrojection

## The Claim
If first-century Judaism was not works-righteousness (see [[First-Century Judaism Was Covenantal Not Works-Righteousness]]), then *why* do so many Christians read [[Paul the Apostle|Paul]] as attacking people who tried to earn heaven? [[Jacob Hansen]]'s answer is **anachronism**. About 1,500 years after Paul, [[Martin Luther]] was wrestling with *medieval Catholic* problems — merit, assurance, "what a sinner must do to get to heaven" — and the Reformers "read Paul as if he was answering the questions of their day without realizing the actual issues Paul was dealing with."[^1] Paul's real controversy was **Gentile inclusion**: now that the gospel goes to all people, who counts as the covenant family, and do the Torah boundary markers (circumcision, food laws) still define membership? Put back in that frame, "justification" is about *who is inside the covenant community*, not *who gets to go to heaven*.[^2]

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. Luther projected his sixteenth-century questions onto a first-century text (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate — a historiographical claim)
"About 1,500 years after Paul, Martin Luther was struggling with medieval Catholic ideas about merit, assurance, and what a sinner must do to get to heaven. Martin Luther and other reformers then read Paul as if he was answering the questions of their day."[^1] The grace/works impasse that defines Protestant–Catholic debate is, on this reading, a *sixteenth-century* problem retrojected onto Paul — not the problem Paul himself was addressing. (For a related Luther-anachronism, the inserted *allein* in Romans 3:28, see [[Luther Inserted Allein Faith Alone Into Romans 3-28]].)

### 2. Paul's actual problem was redrawing the covenant boundary for Gentiles (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate)
"The burning controversy among the earliest Christians was: who are the covenant people if it's not the Jews alone? ... In the past the boundary markers of the covenant had been the works of Torah, but they had to face the question, well, what is it now?"[^3] Paul "was not talking about who could get into heaven, but rather on what basis was someone inside or outside of the covenant family, as those borderlines were being redrawn with the inclusion of the Gentiles."[^4] The faith-side companion treatment develops this exegesis in depth — see [[Pauls Question Was Gentile Inclusion Not Individual Merit]].

### 3. The payoff: two words get redefined (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate — depends on the contested NPP reading)
"Suddenly justification isn't about who gets to go to heaven, but rather about who is included in the covenant community. Suddenly discussions about works of the law are not about an obsession with keeping, but about if things like kosher laws and circumcision are what determine if you are part of the covenant family."[^2] This is the same lexical move worked out in [[Works of the Law Means Mosaic Boundary Markers Not Obedience as Such]] and [[Justification Is Covenant Entry Not a Guaranteed Final Verdict]].

### 4. The scholarly correction (Argument weight: Secondary; Evidence rating: Moderate — contested)
The New Perspective — "[[E.P. Sanders]] to [[James Dunn]] to [[N.T. Wright]], and even many scholars who disagree with them on other issues" — "have largely abandoned the caricature of Judaism as a religion based on earning salvation through works."[^5] The correction is precisely a de-anachronizing move: reading Paul against Second-Temple primary sources instead of against the Reformation debate.

### 5. The hermeneutical lesson (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Strong as a method)
"Maybe the first question we should ask when reading the Bible isn't, 'What does this passage mean to me in my context?' Maybe it's, 'What question was this passage originally answering in its original context.' History matters. Context matters. Because sometimes the biggest obstacle to understanding scripture isn't what the Bible says. It's the questions we assume it was trying to answer."[^6] The works-righteousness misreading is the case study: the wrong answer flows from importing the wrong question.

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

### The Reformed reply: the retrojection charge cuts both ways
A careful Protestant answers on three fronts. (1) *Tu quoque:* if Luther read his context into Paul, New-Perspective scholars may be reading *modern* ecumenical and sociological concerns (identity, boundary markers, anti-supersessionism) back into Paul just as anachronistically. (2) *Text:* Paul's own language — the wage/gift contrast (Romans 4:4-5), "if by grace, then no more of works" (Romans 11:6), "not having mine own righteousness" (Philippians 3:9) — reads plausibly as a polemic against *merit*, not merely against ethnic markers. (3) *Recovery, not novelty:* the Reformers claimed to *recover* Paul's plain sense that the medieval church had obscured, so "the reading appeared late" does not make it wrong (the mirror of the dating argument in [[Synergism Is Ancient and Sola Fide Is the Real Innovation]]).

### The response
The New Perspective grounds itself in Second-Temple *primary sources* (Sanders's method), not in modern politics, so the *tu quoque* is not symmetrical — one side reads Paul's neighbors, the other read Paul's later critics. And even conceding that some verses touch merit, the burden remains to show Paul's *target* was works-righteousness rather than boundary markers — a burden the circumcision-and-food-law context of Galatians and Romans makes hard to carry. The claim here is deliberately modest: not that the merit-reading is impossible, but that it is a *later frame* whose fit with the first-century evidence should be argued, not assumed. This is contested scholarship, honestly flagged as such in [[Works of the Law Means Mosaic Boundary Markers Not Obedience as Such]].

## LDS Relevance
The Latter-day Saint payoff is twofold. Negatively, it neutralizes the "works-based gospel" charge by showing it rests on a sixteenth-century misreading rather than the apostolic text. Positively, it explains why the Restoration's covenantal, grace-then-faithfulness soteriology "looks a lot like" what the earliest Christians actually held — the convergence developed in [[Joseph Smith Reconstructed First-Century Soteriology Without Knowing It]].
