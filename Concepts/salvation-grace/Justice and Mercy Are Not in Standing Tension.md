# Justice and Mercy Are Not in Standing Tension

## The Claim
On [[James 2.13|James 2:13]] — "mercy triumphs over judgment" (NRSVUE; KJV "mercy rejoiceth against judgment") — [[Dan McClellan]] argues that the widespread notion, "in particular within the Latter-day Saint tradition, but in a lot of different Christian traditions," that *justice* and *mercy* stand in permanent tension rests on a mistranslation of biblical justice.[^1] Hebrew *mishpat* and *tsedeqah* are "not isometric with our contemporary concept of justice as... the proper and right and prescribed consequences of the law"; in the Hebrew Bible justice is about "generating the proper relationships, the proper outcomes, the proper equilibrium... making sure society is just rather than making sure the people who do things wrong are punished."[^2] So mercy is not the *suspension* of justice — going beyond the law (*chesed*) is what biblical justice was aiming at all along.

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. Biblical justice is right-relationship, not retribution (rated: strong, lexical)
"When we look in the Hebrew and we look at what *mishpat* is and what *sedeqah* is... it really in the Hebrew Bible... has to do with generating the proper relationships, the proper outcomes, the proper equilibrium. It's kind of making sure society is just rather than making sure the people who do things wrong are punished."[^2] The "justice = prescribed consequences of the law" that the tension model assumes is a modern, forensic import, not the Hebrew concept James inherits.

### 2. *Chesed* means going beyond the law's demand (rated: strong, lexical)
*Chesed* — rendered "lovingkindness" or "grace" in the KJV — is best explained as "a combination of two things: doing things for people that they can't do for themselves and doing things for people that are not necessarily demanded by the law."[^3] The Book of Ruth is the paradigm: Ruth "goes above and beyond what is expected of her by society, by the law, in relation to Naomi," and Boaz "goes above and beyond what is demanded by the law" in relation to Ruth.[^4] *Chesed* "is much closer to the idea of mercy than it is to the idea of justice or judgment."[^5]

### 3. James privileges mercy outright (rated: moderate, primary text)
Rather than balancing two competing principles, James "just says, 'Nah, mercy triumphs over judgment.'"[^6] McClellan allows this could also be read as contrasting "the law of Moses and the... law of liberty" (as Paul contrasts being under vs. free of the law),[^6] but on either reading James does not treat mercy as a grudging exception carved out of justice's due.

## Apologetic Engagement
**Steelman (the tension model).** The justice-versus-mercy tension is deeply scriptural in the Restoration: Alma 42 has "the work of justice could not be destroyed" without ceasing to be God, so the Atonement must "appease the demands of justice" *and* extend mercy — a real bilateral structure, not a confusion. Defenders can note that *mishpat* does carry juridical, consequence-bearing senses (courts, verdicts, penalties), so McClellan's "right-relationship, never punishment" gloss is a partial, chosen emphasis, and that a coherent doctrine of the Atonement needs *some* account of why sin cannot simply be waved away.

**Where it helps and where it diverges.** This is a source where the foil mostly *corrects* rather than confirms an LDS instinct — but the correction cuts a useful seam. McClellan's Hebrew-justice-as-relationship reading actually *converges* with the vault's more sophisticated atonement reframe, which already rejects a retributive, payment-based model (see [[Relationship Not Payment - Atonement Rejects Retributive Justice]] and [[Grace Is Unconditional Love Not an Earned Wage]]): if biblical justice is restored right-relationship, then the Atonement "satisfies justice" precisely *by* reconciling, not by extracting a penalty. Where he diverges is the popular Latter-day Saint (and Reformed) picture of justice as a ledger of "prescribed consequences" that mercy must offset — the frame behind the vault's [[The Law of Restoration as the Single Law of Justice|law-of-restoration]] articulation. The honest LDS response is to distinguish the folk "demands of justice" balance-sheet (which McClellan rightly critiques) from the covenantal reading in which God's *tsedeqah* is His saving faithfulness, mercy included.

## LDS Relevance
Latter-day Saint teaching leans hard on a justice/mercy polarity (Alma 34, Alma 42), and this episode is a caution: the polarity can harden into a picture of a God whose hands are tied by an impersonal law until a payment is made. Reading *mishpat*/*tsedeqah* as right-relationship reframes "mercy triumphs over judgment" not as mercy *defeating* justice but as justice *fulfilled* — the equilibrium God wanted. That coheres with the vault's relational atonement notes and with the *chesed*-shaped ethic James commends (care for orphans and widows, going beyond the law), while it presses Latter-day Saints to state the "demands of justice" language carefully so it does not smuggle in the retributive frame the Hebrew never carried.
