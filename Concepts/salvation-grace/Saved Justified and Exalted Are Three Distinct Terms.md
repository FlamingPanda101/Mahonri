# Saved Justified and Exalted Are Three Distinct Terms

## The Claim
[[Blake Ostler]] argues that the Mormon-vs-Protestant salvation debate is mostly a word game: **"saved," "justified," and "exalted" name three different things**, and both sides equivocate. *Justified* = entry into right covenant relationship (see [[Justification Is Covenant Entry Not a Guaranteed Final Verdict]]). *Saved* (in D&C 76) is **near-universal** — everyone who will bend the knee and confess Christ is "saved," and only sons of perdition are lost — so it does **not** mean Protestant "eternal security." *Exalted* is the deifying end of the covenant path (theosis). The Article of Faith 3 line "saved by obedience to the laws and ordinances of the gospel" is not a contradiction of grace but an **equivocation** on the over-broad word "saved."[^1][^2]

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. "Saved" ≠ "justified" ≠ "exalted" (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate)
"The way that Paul is using *justified* is not to be saved."[^3] To be justified is to be "put into a right relationship with God" (covenantal nomism), whereas Protestants hear "saved once and for all with eternal security" — "that's not what Paul is talking about at all."[^4] And "to be saved doesn't mean that we have eternal security and we're exalted — those are different things."[^1]

### 2. In D&C 76, salvation is near-universal (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Strong for the LDS text)
Reading [[D&C 76]] (the 1832 vision to [[Joseph Smith]] and [[Sydney Rigdon]]), Ostler notes vv. 39-42 have Christ "save all the works of his hands except those sons of perdition."[^5] Tied to [[Philippians 2.9-11|Philippians 2]] — "all will bend the knee... every single person" — this forces the conclusion: "if one takes the view that what you do is have faith and confess Christ to be saved, you have to be a universalist... And section 76 takes that and says all are saved."[^6] Only sons of perdition, "a hell of their own making," are excluded ([[Son of Perdition Requires the Highest Bar of Knowledge]]).[^7]

### 3. Exaltation is theosis, the covenant path's end — not bare salvation (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate)
Being saved "means that we have entered into a relationship with Christ that can lead to exaltation," but "to be exalted means... that we have entered into a relationship of unity with the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost that is deifying. It's a concept of theosis."[^8] Salvation is the entry; exaltation is "the end product of a person who enters on the path through baptism." See [[Human Deification in Mormonism]] and [[Degrees of Glory vs Heaven]].

### 4. The Article of Faith 3 tension is an equivocation, not a contradiction (Argument weight: Primary; Evidence rating: Moderate)
Pressed on "all mankind may be saved by obedience to the laws and ordinances of the gospel" (which seems to make salvation contingent, unlike D&C 76's universal salvation), Ostler answers: "I think we just have to admit this is an equivocation... in the use of the word *save*." Joseph Smith is "using saved in a more inclusive sense" — "to be in a full relationship with God" (i.e., exaltation) — trying to "subsume an entire soteriology in a single sentence," so "the term is over-broad here."[^2][^9]

### 5. Saved by grace, judged by works — a further distinct axis (Argument weight: Secondary; Evidence rating: Moderate)
Cutting across all three terms: "Saved by grace, universally judged by works." Judgment by works (the *reward/degree* one inherits) is taught in "virtually every single book of the Bible"; Paul uses the phrase "no less than eight times."[^10] D&C 20:30-32 is, Ostler says, the one place LDS scripture uses "justification" in a "straightforward Protestant way" — justification *and* sanctification both "by grace," with the warning that men "may fall from grace."[^11]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

### The Protestant steelman: "saved" is precisely eternal security, and multiplying senses is special pleading
A Reformed reader will object that Ostler rescues Article of Faith 3 only by inventing a private LDS lexicon: if the word "saved" can mean bare glory, covenant entry, *or* full exaltation depending on the verse, then it can be made to say anything, and the plain contingency of "saved by obedience to... ordinances" is simply read away. On the Protestant view, *sōzō* in Paul does carry final rescue from wrath ([[Romans 5.1-9|Romans 5:9]]), and D&C 76's "all are saved" is not Paul but a distinctively Mormon universalism that the New Testament's warnings about hell resist ([[Salvation Is Binary In Christ or Out of Christ Not a Spectrum]]).

### An internal LDS caution
Even sympathetic Latter-day Saints note the pastoral hazard of the very conflation Ostler untangles: collapsing salvation into exaltation breeds a works-treadmill ([[Conflating Salvation with Exaltation Breeds Works Burnout]]). Ostler's own concession — that Joseph Smith "was neither a theologian nor a philosopher" and was imprecise with terms[^12] — is candid, but a critic can press that a revelation delivered in God's voice (as D&C 76 claims) ought not need this much lexical repair.

**Where it turns:** whether "saved" in scripture is one thing (final rescue, as the Reformation reads it) or an umbrella spanning universal glory, covenant entry, and exaltation. Ostler's case is that first-century usage and D&C 76 both require the umbrella; the Protestant case is that the umbrella is a convenient equivocation.
