# Early Christian Catacomb Art and Vestments Depict Temple-Style Ordinances

## The Claim

[[Jonah Barnes]] argues that the most telling witnesses to a lost forty-day ordinance tradition are **material**, not textual. Rather than text-to-text "influence" (his earlier books' method), here he points to catacomb paintings and excavated garments that show early Christians *performing* temple-like rites — ring/prayer circles with joined hands, building altars, changing vestments, crowning one another, wearing aprons and sashes, washings and anointings, giving new names, and garments marked with rosettes over the breasts and a strange knot at the shoulder. "It's less about the influences… more like: this is what we're doing." He pairs the evidence with an explanation for why it dropped out of the record: a **double rejection** — Protestants reject anything ritualistic as too Catholic, and Catholics reject the same material as Gnostic — which "neutered out" the forty-day ritual texts, including even the first-century *Didache*.[^1]

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. Material remains show practice, not just ideas
Barnes's key shift: instead of comparing a text to a text, he cites carvings and paintings in the catacombs and objects from excavated sites — people depicted in a ring circle with joined hands, building an altar, changing their vestments, crowning each other, "wearing aprons and sashes," "anointing and washing," and "giving new names." These are enactments, not doctrines — evidence of *what early Christians did*, which he reads as a temple-style ordinance complex.[^1]

> **Evidence rating:** Moderate for the existence of the artifacts and iconography; Weak-to-Moderate for the specific identification with the modern endowment (robes, crowns, and anointings are widespread in Mediterranean religion and in mainstream Christian baptism/chrismation). **Argument weight:** Primary.

### 2. Excavated garments carry temple-like markings
Barnes points to grave clothes recovered from digs — "very suspicious clothes with rosettes over the breasts and a strange knot over the shoulder" — as material continuity between a first-century ritual and later temple garments. This is the same category of evidence developed at length for the Fag el-Gamous cemetery; see [[The Fag el-Gamous Coptic Burial Garments Mark an Ancient Temple Connection]].[^1]

> **Evidence rating:** Moderate — the marked garments are real finds; whether the markings are proprietary temple evidence or ordinary decorative/funerary convention is the contested point. **Argument weight:** Secondary.

### 3. A double rejection explains the loss
Barnes argues the forty-day ritual texts were rejected "based off of content" from both directions: post-Reformation **Protestant** scholars reject "anything ritualistic because they're afraid it's going to sound Catholic," while **Catholics**, seeing material that is not theirs, "reject it as Gnostic." Caught between the two, the ritual-heavy forty-day literature was marginalized out of the canon and the mainstream.[^2]

> **Evidence rating:** Moderate as a *plausibility* account of why such material would be sidelined; note that "it would have been suppressed" explains a loss, it does not by itself prove the content existed. **Argument weight:** Secondary.

### 4. The Didache: a first-century ritual witness left out of the canon
Barnes notes that the earliest Christian document, the first-century **Didache** — older than any surviving New Testament manuscript — is a manual of ritual/ordinance instruction, and asks why it "wasn't included in the canon." (He adds an aside that comparing it to Doctrine and Covenants 59 is striking: "you're like, wait a minute, how did Joseph know this?") It anchors the ritual genre in the first century rather than only in the late-dating Gnostic corpus. Barnes files the forty-day texts as a "loose genre" whose recurring theme is "during the 40 days Christ said XYZ." See [[Gnostic Apocrypha Preserve Temple Ordinance Patterns]].[^3]

> **Evidence rating:** Moderate — the Didache is genuinely first-century and non-canonical; but it does not itself contain the full temple-ordinance complex, so it anchors the *genre*, not the specific claim. **Argument weight:** Secondary.

## Best Quotes

> "We're doing a ring circle with our hands like this… we're building an altar. We're changing our vestments. We're crowning each other. We're wearing aprons and sashes. We are anointing and washing. We're giving new names." [^1]

> "There isn't a text that really lays this off, but there are lots of texts that will say: during the 40 days Christ said XYZ. And it is a theme in many of the texts that have been called apocryphal, called Gnostic." [^4]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

The strongest objection is raised on-air by co-host [[Austin Falter]] himself: **(a) the dating gap.** Much of the Gnostic material only traces to "the sixth, seventh century AD," and an unbroken oral tradition surviving half a millennium is implausible — "I would reject that cynically as heretical." Barnes's reply (the first-century Didache) is a single text and does not carry the full ritual complex. **(b) Parallelomania.** Rosettes, shoulder-knots, aprons, crowns, washings, and anointings admit many readings; robes and anointing are common to Mediterranean religion and to mainstream Christian **baptism and chrismation**, so reading them specifically as the modern LDS endowment is pattern-matching. **(c) Contested iconography.** Catacomb art is fragmentary and conventional — art historians typically read the *orans* prayer posture, funerary banquets, and biblical scenes where Barnes reads ritual circles. **(d) A suppression story is not evidence of the thing suppressed.** The double-rejection account explains why such material *would* be marginalized, but does not establish that the ordinance existed. A Latter-day Saint reply: the case is explicitly **cumulative** — no single artifact is decisive, but the convergence of independent material and textual witnesses on the *same* complex (marked garments, washings, anointings, new names, prayer circles, veils) is exactly the residue a genuinely early-but-suppressed ordinance would leave, and for the endowed the recognition is experiential ("I know what that is"). See [[The Lost Forty-Day Teaching Points to a Suppressed Temple Endowment]] and [[The Christian Prayer Circle Descends From an Ancient Temple Dance]].
