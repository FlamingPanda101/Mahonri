# Luke's Lacuna - The Forty-Day Ministry Is a Measurable Hole Between Luke and Acts

## The Claim

[[Jonah Barnes]] sharpens the forty-day argument into a **structural** one he calls "Luke's Lacuna." The point is not merely that the Gospels say little about the resurrection teaching, but that the one author who could tell us — [[Luke the Evangelist|Luke]], who wrote both the Gospel and Acts — leaves a *measurable* void exactly where the forty days should be. In the canon, John's Gospel is interposed between Luke and Acts; sew the end of Luke directly to the start of Acts and the two halves flow as one continuous narrative, with the forty days as the seam. Yet by Barnes's word counts the risen Lord's ministry gets only ~211 words while every adjacent period gets thousands. He argues the Book of Mormon's Bountiful ministry ([[3 Nephi]]) is a text that "fills that hole."[^1]

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. One author, two books, an interrupted seam
Luke wrote both the Gospel of Luke and the Acts of the Apostles; Acts opens by referring back to "the former treatise." The two "go pretty well together… they pick up immediately after one another," but John's Gospel sits between them in the canon. Barnes's rhetorical move: excise John, "zipper" the end of Luke to the start of Acts, and read across the join — what you get is a single resurrection-to-ascension narrative whose center is the forty days.[^2]

> **Evidence rating:** Moderate for the Lukan authorship of both books (mainstream); the "John was placed here to hide the lacuna" line is delivered as a joke, not a claim. **Argument weight:** Secondary (framing device).

### 2. The word-count collapse *is* the lacuna
Barnes graphs the English word counts across Luke-Acts: ~2,000 words on Jesus's pre-ministry, ~17,000 on the three-year mortal ministry, ~6,389 on Holy Week — then only **211 words** on "the Acts of the Risen Lord" (the forty days) — then ~24,000 on the Acts of the Apostles. Plotted, the risen-Lord period is a sharp trough: "he's writing and then it just goes and it just dips."[^1]

> **Evidence rating:** Moderate — the disproportion is genuine (Luke's resurrection material really is brief relative to the mortal ministry); but "211 words" is Barnes's own count of a category ("Acts of the Risen Lord") he defines, and the forty days are also witnessed outside Luke (Matthew 28, John 20-21, 1 Corinthians 15). **Argument weight:** Primary.

### 3. Read across the seam, the content is temple content
The end of Luke has Jesus tell the disciples to "tarry… until ye be endued with power from on high" ([[Luke 24.49|Luke 24:49]]), then lift his hands, bless them, and depart — after which they are "continually in the temple" ([[Luke 24.50-53|Luke 24:50-53]]). The start of Acts has the risen Christ "speaking of the things pertaining to the kingdom of God" for forty days and telling them to "wait for the promise of the Father" ([[Acts 1.1-5|Acts 1:1-5]]). Barnes reads the join as: *stay, receive an endowment of power, keep going to the temple* — i.e., the missing content points at the temple.[^3]

> **Evidence rating:** Moderate — the "tarry / endued / temple" language is really in the text; the inference that the withheld forty-day content was specifically a temple ordinance is the interpretive leap (see the master note's steelman). **Argument weight:** Primary.

### 4. The Book of Mormon supplies the missing genre
Barnes overlays the word count of the Nephite "Bountiful gospel" — the risen Lord's ministry in [[3 Nephi]] — on the trough and argues it "might fill that hole pretty well." Where Luke gives 211 words, 3 Nephi gives a full sustained account of the resurrected Christ teaching, ordaining, covenanting, and administering ordinances at a temple, which he presents as what a complete risen-Lord ministry looks like.[^4]

> **Evidence rating:** Weak-to-Moderate — this is an *illustrative* parallel (a Restoration text modeling the omitted genre), not evidence about the New Testament's own lost content. **Argument weight:** Secondary.

## Best Quotes

> "This is Luke's Lacuna. You see this? He's writing and then it just goes and it just dips. So that's why it's called Luke's lacuna." [^1]

> "We spent 6,000 words on Holy Week… but only 200. And you just read the 200. They're not very descriptive words. It's like: he did some stuff, it was really cool… and then he left." [^4]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning

A critic responds on several fronts. **(a) Argument from silence.** Brevity is not suppression: Luke compresses the resurrection appearances because his narrative purpose is the ascension-to-Pentecost hinge, and Acts itself names the awaited "promise of the Father" as the **Holy Spirit** poured out at Pentecost (Acts 2), then narrates it at length — so Luke *does* fill his own gap, just not with an ordinance. **(b) A loaded metric.** Isolating an "Acts of the Risen Lord" word count and setting it beside whole multi-year ministries manufactures the trough; the forty days are attested across Matthew 28, John 20-21, and 1 Corinthians 15:3-8, not only in Luke's 211 words. **(c) Canonical order.** John's position reflects the early grouping of the Synoptics followed by John, a matter of manuscript history — not a scribal or diabolical plot to conceal a seam; "sewing Luke to Acts" is an admitted thought experiment. **(d) 3 Nephi cannot back-fill the New Testament.** That a Restoration text models a fuller risen-Lord ministry shows what Latter-day Saints expect, not what the first-century record contained. A Latter-day Saint reply: the count is illustrative, not probative — the load-bearing case is the *convergence* of a real teaching gap with independent Restoration revelation ([[Joseph Smith Identified the Temple Endowment With Christ's Teaching to the Apostles at Jerusalem|D&C 95:8-9]]) and with apocryphal and material witnesses that a secret ordinance was attached to the risen Christ (see [[The Lost Forty-Day Teaching Points to a Suppressed Temple Endowment]] and [[Early Christian Catacomb Art and Vestments Depict Temple-Style Ordinances]]).
