# The Latter-day Logic Four-Step Framework for Any Anti-Mormon Claim

## The Claim
[[Devin Barnes]] ([[Latterday Logic|Latter-day Logic]]) offers a **triage sequence** meant to let a member with no memorized answers dismantle almost any historical or doctrinal accusation. It exists in a full fourteen-step form (a free evaluator tool on latterdaylogic.org) and an abbreviated four-step version.[^1] The four gates, run in order, are: **(1) Is the accusation even true?** **(2) Is your own church guilty of the same thing?** **(3) Is it biblical — and does God condone it?** and **(4) How does this prove the Book of Mormon false?** An accusation that fails any gate is defused without the member needing the underlying facts on hand.[^2] Barnes explicitly frames the tool as an application of "love God with all thy mind."[^3]

## Supporting Evidence

### 1. Step one shifts the burden of proof to the accuser — rate: strong (method)
"The first step is, is this accusation even true? ... when they make the accusation, the burden of proof is on their shoulders."[^4] A member who cannot immediately rebut a charge can still ask for evidence, and most charges collapse there: "95% of the time they leave you alone because they either can't find the evidence or they're too lazy to find it."[^5] This is the same disciplined move as [[Deny the Loaded Premise Before You Explain]] and [[The Pragmatic Argument Shifts the Burden to the Skeptic]] — refuse to defend a claim the critic has not first established. Worked example in the episode: "Joseph Smith married a 14-year-old" is met not with a memorized history but with "what *evidence* do you have?" (see [[Joseph Smith and Helen Mar Kimball Sealing]]).[^6]

### 2. Step two is a consistency (tu quoque) test — rate: moderate, and the presenters flag its limit — rate: honest
"Do we see this also in your church? Are you a little bit hypocritical in this?"[^7] If the same fault appears in the critic's own tradition (a Martin Luther or church-father parallel), the *argument* fails. Crucially, the hosts do **not** overclaim: "just because somebody else's church is guilty of something too doesn't mean it's not bad ... It just proves that their argument fails."[^8] That is the correct logical scope of tu quoque — it neutralizes a double standard without vindicating the practice — and matches [[The Reversibility Test for Bad Arguments]] and [[Internal Critique - Two Questions for Every Critic]].

### 3. Step three asks whether the practice is biblical and divinely condoned — rate: moderate
"Is it in the Bible? Not just is it in the Bible, but does God condone it in the Bible?"[^9] For charges like plural marriage or a lineage-based priesthood restriction, the point is that "the same source text that you're using is the same source text that I'm using and you're contradicting it" — a double standard where the critic accepts the practice in scripture but not in Restoration history.[^10] This is a species of internal critique: the critic's own authority undercuts the objection.

### 4. Step four is the keystone move — rate: strong (as framing), and it is the whole game — argument weight: primary
"How does this prove the Book of Mormon false?"[^11] Barnes's logic: "The evidence of [Joseph's] prophetic calling all hinges on if the Book of Mormon is an ancient authentic record of scripture. If it is scripture, then Joseph Smith is a prophet. If it's not scripture, then he's not a prophet."[^11] Therefore peripheral attacks — polygamy details, an errant Brigham Young statement — "[do] not have any impact on the authenticity and truthfulness of the Book of Mormon." This is a direct application of [[The Book of Mormon Keystone Argument]]: concentrate the entire dispute on the one testable artifact.

### 5. The abbreviated four is a triage tool, not a proof — rate: candid (the presenters' own concession)
Pressed that step two is logically incomplete, Barnes agrees: the four-step version has "some loose ends," "loose loopholes," and is "a quick tool set for somebody who doesn't have all the answers"; only the full fourteen-step version "has no logical loopholes in it."[^12] He also distinguishes audiences: the fourteen-step deep dive is for someone leaving over "a life or death situation spiritually," not merely someone lobbing soundbites.[^13]

## Counter-Arguments / Steelmanning
- **Step four can look like changing the subject.** A critic will say the honesty of the *founder* (did he deceive wives? teach later-repudiated doctrine?) is evidence about the *book*, since the same man produced both. Reducing every objection to "but the Book of Mormon" can read as refusing to let any datum count against Joseph. The reply only holds if the keystone case for the book is independently strong (see [[The Book of Mormon Keystone Argument]], [[No Naturalistic Source Accounts for the Whole Book of Mormon]]); the framework borrows all its force from that prior argument.
- **Steps two and three prove less than they feel like they prove.** As the presenters concede, showing a critic's tradition shares a fault, or that scripture contains it, defeats a *double standard* but leaves the moral question open. An honest interlocutor can grant the tu quoque and still press "is it *right*?" — which the framework does not answer.
- **The burden-shift can become a conversation-ender.** "Provide evidence or we're done" is fair against a drive-by, but a genuine seeker who *has* done reading may experience step one as a brush-off (compare [[Genuine Seekers vs Bullet-Shooters]]). Barnes's own fourteen-step "deep dive" is the concession that serious inquirers need more than the triage.
