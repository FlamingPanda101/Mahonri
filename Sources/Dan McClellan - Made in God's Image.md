# Dan McClellan — Made in God's Image

A short reaction video in which McClellan responds to an apologetic content creator's claim that being made "in God's image" means humans are the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and that the flesh is what makes us sin. McClellan lays out the mainstream biblical-studies account of the *imago Dei* — the Hebrew *tselem* (image) and *demut* (likeness) — as a **divine image**: primarily about outward appearance (God is human-shaped, so humans share that shape) and, by extension, about humanity functioning as God's royal representative on earth.[^1] He then critiques the "flesh is evil" framing as middle-Platonic/Gnostic and traces how that very debate produced creation ex nihilo.

## Summary

- **The image is a *divine image* (idol/*tselem*).** *Tselem* = "divine image" (Akkadian *tsalmu*); *demut* = "likeness, appearance, outward form." The fundamental sense is **appearance**: the authors of Genesis 1 and 2 pictured God as human-shaped, so humans are made in "more or less the same shape."[^2]
- **A royal layer: democratized representation.** Genesis 1 (exilic/post-exilic) borrows the Mesopotamian and Egyptian ideology of the king as "the image / idol of a deity" and **democratizes** it: all humanity — not just the king — becomes God's representative, counterpart, and image on earth, bearing his presence, power, and agency.[^3]
- **Divine images manifest presence; they are not mere objects of worship.** An idol's function was to make an unseen deity present and available (for petition, gratitude, worship, communion, or monitoring). Humanity as *imago Dei* works the same way.[^4]
- **The "flesh is sin" reading is Gnostic, not biblical.** Treating the flesh as the enemy of God is middle-Platonic/Gnostic; early Christians instead looked forward to the resurrection *of the flesh*, and the clash over that idea drove the late-2nd-century development of creation ex nihilo.[^5]
- **"Let us make" is the divine council, not the Trinity.** The plural is a vestigial reference to the divine council (the other *elohim*), corroborated by Genesis 3:5/3:22 and the *bene elohim* of Genesis 6 — the Trinity is a philosophical innovation of the mid-to-late 2nd century CE.[^6]
