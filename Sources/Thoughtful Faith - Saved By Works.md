# Thoughtful Faith — Saved By Works? The Context That Protestants Miss

A scripted [[Jacob Hansen]] explainer arguing that both halves of a common Evangelical charge — that first-century Jews, and Latter-day Saints after them, ran a "works-based religion where you tried to earn your way to heaven" — rest on a **historical mistake**. Drawing on the New Perspective on Paul ([[E.P. Sanders]], [[James Dunn]], [[N.T. Wright]]), Hansen argues there is "essentially no historical evidence" that first-century Jews thought they earned heaven by works; their framework was covenantal (grace-first election, obedience as faithful response, sacrifice for inevitable sin). The works-righteousness reading of [[Paul the Apostle|Paul]], he says, is a sixteenth-century anachronism — [[Martin Luther]] reading his own merit-and-assurance questions back onto a first-century text whose real controversy was Gentile inclusion in the covenant. This is the works-side companion to the faith-side case in [[Thoughtful Faith - Why Paul Sounds Like a Mormon]], and complements [[Blake Ostler]]'s treatment in [[Thoughtful Faith - Why Christians Misread Paul]].[^1]

- **First-century Judaism was covenantal, not works-righteousness.** "There's essentially no historical evidence that first-century Jews actually believed that they earned their way to heaven through good works."[^2] The proof is structural: Israel's story runs grace-first (rescue from Egypt *then* the law at Sinai),[^3] and the whole sacrificial system — day of atonement, priests, repentance, forgiveness — exists precisely because "the Jews knew that people would still sin... They did not expect sinless perfection. They expected covenant faithfulness."[^4] The challenge to critics: "Can you show me where a first-century Jew actually says that?"[^5] See [[First-Century Judaism Was Covenantal Not Works-Righteousness]].
- **The works-righteousness reading of Paul is a Reformation retrojection.** "About 1,500 years after Paul, Martin Luther was struggling with medieval Catholic ideas about merit, assurance, and what a sinner must do to get to heaven," and the Reformers "read Paul as if he was answering the questions of their day."[^6] Paul's real problem was who belongs to the covenant family now that the gospel goes to the Gentiles — so "justification isn't about who gets to go to heaven, but rather about who is included in the covenant community," and "works of the law" concern "if things like kosher laws and circumcision" define membership, not "an obsession with keeping."[^7] The hermeneutical lesson: ask "what question was this passage originally answering in its original context," not "what does this passage mean to me."[^8] See [[The Works-Righteousness Reading of Paul Is a Reformation Retrojection]].
- **The first-century hope was resurrection and covenant vindication, not "going to heaven."** The Jewish question was "Will God vindicate his covenant people?" not "How do I get to heaven when I die?"[^9] Martha's hope at Lazarus's death is that "he shall rise again in the resurrection at the last day" ([[John 11.24|John 11:24]]), and Paul anchors the Christian message in that same future resurrection ([[1 Corinthians 15.1-4|1 Corinthians 15]]).[^10] The fuller eschatology, and the argument that Joseph Smith rebuilt it blind, are in [[Joseph Smith Reconstructed First-Century Soteriology Without Knowing It]].
- **Payoff for the grace/works impasse.** Once justification is covenant membership and "works of the law" are boundary markers, the LDS both/and (grace-first, judged by covenant faithfulness) is not a corruption of the gospel but its original shape — cross-linking the exegesis in [[Justification Is Covenant Entry Not a Guaranteed Final Verdict]] and [[Works of the Law Means Mosaic Boundary Markers Not Obedience as Such]], and the dating argument in [[Synergism Is Ancient and Sola Fide Is the Real Innovation]].
