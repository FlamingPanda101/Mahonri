# LDS Religiousness Report - Dyer Erickson Hardy

**Full title:** *Latter-day Saint Trends in the United States: Religiousness, Well-Being, and Retention* (BYU Studies, June 2026).
**Authors:** [[Justin Dyer]] (PhD, BYU Religious Education; editor-in-chief of *BYU Studies*), [[Jenet Erickson]] (PhD, BYU Religious Education), [[Sam Hardy]] (PhD, BYU Psychology), Barbara Morgan Gardner (PhD, BYU Religious Education), and David C. Dollahite (PhD, BYU School of Family Life).

A 56-page empirical report synthesizing five large US datasets — the General Social Survey (GSS, 1977–2022), the 2024 Pew Religious Landscape Study (PRLS), the Cooperative Election Survey (CES), the 2025 Spiritual Seismology Survey (SSS, [[Ryan Burge]]'s ~100,000-respondent panel), and the longitudinal Family Foundations of Youth Development Study (FFYD, 2016–2024) — to describe Latter-day Saint religiousness, well-being, and retention and compare them across religions and generations. It is the **primary document** behind the secondary interview [[Informed Saints - The Surprising REAL Reason People Are Leaving The Church]], and it supplies precise, page-level statistics for claims previously resting only on that video.

- **Highest religiousness in America.** 76% of Latter-day Saint Millennial+ attend church at least monthly — more than any other US religious group; 69% attend weekly (vs. 51% Evangelicals, the next closest); 56% read scripture weekly; and 80% of LDS parents pray and/or read scripture with their children — each the highest of any faith surveyed.[^1][^2][^3][^4]
- **Strong well-being, smallest generational gap.** Latter-day Saints tie at the top for feeling "a deep sense of spiritual peace and well-being" weekly (62% Millennial+), and LDS Millennials/Gen Z are the *single most likely* of any religion to say they are "very happy" with life. On depression, anxiety, and suicide-attempt risk (Healthy Minds college data), they are statistically comparable to other faiths, while the religiously unaffiliated fare significantly worse.[^5][^6][^7]
- **Highest active retention.** ~50% of those raised LDS still identify as adults (higher than every Christian denomination except Catholics and Orthodox), and 42% both identify *and* attend monthly — the highest active-retention rate of any US religion. Retention has genuinely fallen (82% in the 1980s to 46% in the 2010s+), tracking a broad national decline; the LDS membership also has an unusually small nominal middle.[^8][^9][^10]
- **The strongest predictors of leaving are internal and political, not doctrinal.** In the FFYD longitudinal panel, the significant predictors of teen-to-young-adult deidentification were not feeling God's presence daily (24% left vs. 4% of those who felt it daily), agreeing the Church should accept same-sex marriage (28% vs. 5%), and being troubled by the Church's political stances (22% vs. 5%). Smartphone ownership and heavy video-watching also predicted leaving, apparently by crowding out spiritual experience.[^11][^12]
- **Those who leave are not monolithic — and most keep the door open.** Latent-class analysis of 527 former Latter-day Saints yielded four groups: Faith Staying (22%), Faith Bordering (32%), Faith Distancing (36%), and Faith Leaving (10%). Over half still value religion/spirituality and feel warm toward the Church; only ~10% wholly reject religion. About 2 in 3 leave open some possibility of returning, many "if a friend invited them."[^13][^14]
