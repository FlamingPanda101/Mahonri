# Bruce R. McConkie

LDS apostle (1915–1985), son-in-law of [[Joseph Fielding Smith]], and author of
the influential reference work *Mormon Doctrine*. Along with Joseph Fielding
Smith, he was one of the two figures "who more than anyone else emphasized the
importance of the King Follett Discourse and moving it more toward the center
of LDS teachings."[^1]

## Champion of the Discourse

McConkie "frequently cited the King Follett Discourse as the best sermon that a
Mormon leader had ever taught, even at one point arguing that it should be added
to the LDS canon."[^1]

## Critic of Roberts's Footnotes

Despite his enthusiasm for the discourse, McConkie was "upfront of his critiques
of BH Roberts's footnotes regarding the King Follett Discourse because they
disagreed both on the infant exaltation and the spirit birth."[^2]

This continued the pattern: even the discourse's greatest champions edited or
critiqued its most controversial elements — [[Infant Exaltation]] and [[Spirit Birth and Intelligences]].[^2]

## "After All We Can Do"

McConkie is the name most associated with a works-forward emphasis on [[2 Nephi 25.23|2 Nephi 25:23]] ("by grace we are saved, after all we can do"). A *Ward Radio* episode built around [[Jeff Harmon]]'s "defined term" reading takes its provocative title from him — jokingly filing "an official petition to update the footnotes... Bruce R. McConkie done messed up, according to Angel Studios" — the claim being that the crux verse's true sense is "saved by grace after we *repent*" (per [[Alma 24.11|Alma 24:11]]). The quip is banter, not a documented engagement with McConkie's writings. See [[All We Can Do Is Defined as Repentance in Alma 24]].[^3]

## "We Are Saved by Grace Without Works" (Davila Debate)

In a salvation debate on [[Brian Davila]]'s channel, [[Jacob Hansen]] quoted McConkie's general-conference statement — "We are saved by grace without works. It is a gift from God. How else could it come?" — to answer [[Ryan (Protestant Debater)|Ryan]]'s use of a [[Spencer W. Kimball]] quote calling grace-alone a "doctrine of Satan," arguing the two harmonize (grace as saving power vs. rejecting cheap grace). The apparent tension with McConkie's better-known "after all we can do" emphasis makes him a useful test case. See [[Kimball vs McConkie - Does LDS Grace Teaching Contradict Itself]].
