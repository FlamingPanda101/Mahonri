# E.P. Sanders

American New Testament scholar (1937–2022) best known for launching the **New Perspective on Paul**. In *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977) he argued that Second-Temple Judaism was not a religion of works-righteousness but of **covenantal nomism** — Israel was graciously brought into the covenant, and law-keeping was the *response* that maintained covenant standing, not a means of earning God's favor. This reframing implied that the Reformation's picture of Paul opposing Jewish "legalism" misreads first-century Judaism, and it opened the door to [[James Dunn]]'s and [[N.T. Wright]]'s work on "works of the law" and justification.

## Significance in the vault
The Thoughtful Faith explainer [[Thoughtful Faith - Why Paul Sounds Like a Mormon]] names Sanders (with Dunn and Wright) as a founder of the scholarship that recasts [[Paul the Apostle|Paul]]'s question as covenant membership and Gentile inclusion rather than individual merit.[^1] His *covenantal nomism* is the framework the vault's Pauline cluster leans on:

- **The New-Perspective reading of "works of the law."** Sanders's reframing underwrites the claim that Paul's polemic targets Mosaic boundary markers, not obedience as such — see [[Works of the Law Means Mosaic Boundary Markers Not Obedience as Such]] and [[Pauls Question Was Gentile Inclusion Not Individual Merit]].
- **Justification as covenant entry.** Covenantal nomism (grace-in, faithfulness-to-remain) is the scholarly name for the model in [[Justification Is Covenant Entry Not a Guaranteed Final Verdict]], where [[Blake Ostler]] cites the framework by name.
- **No first-century evidence for works-righteousness.** The companion explainer [[Thoughtful Faith - Saved By Works]] leans on Sanders's covenantal nomism to argue there is essentially no historical evidence that first-century Jews tried to earn heaven, and that the contrary reading is a Reformation anachronism — see [[First-Century Judaism Was Covenantal Not Works-Righteousness]] and [[The Works-Righteousness Reading of Paul Is a Reformation Retrojection]].[^2]
